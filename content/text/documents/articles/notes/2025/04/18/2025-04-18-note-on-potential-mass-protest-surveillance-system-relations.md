Public information indicates the existence and relations of the following [[surveillance system|systems]].

[[person|People]] [[participate|participating]] in [[mass protest]] in the [[United States of America]] near [[government facility|government facilities]], [[police station|police stations]], or [[utility infrastructure]] may be recorded by [[Fūsus]]. Fūsus [[collect|collects]] and [[aggregate|aggregates]] [[video]] from [[municipal CCTV]], [[police bodycam|police bodycams]], and [[private security camera|private security cameras]] (i.e. [[Amazon Ring]]). Fūsus is known to [[alert]] [[law enforcement fusion center|law enforcement fusion centers]] about [[crowd formation]], [[crowd size]], and [[crowd participant behavior]].

[[Banjo (formerly SafeXai)]] [[monitor|monitors]] [[cellular local data]] for [[anomaly|anomalies]] such as crowd formation near government facilities, police stations, and also alerts fusion centers.

Any government-generated footage, such as police bodycam and municipal CCTV footage, is automatically shared with the [[Homeland Security Information Network]], who distributes these feeds across federal, state, and local law enforcement agencies.

[[Clearview AI]]

Protesters gathering near government buildings, police stations, or infrastructure will be recorded by **Fūsus**. Fūsus collects video feeds from city-owned CCTV, police bodycams, and private cameras. It forwards alerts about crowd size and behavior to real-time crime centers.

**Banjo (SafeXai)** runs anomaly detection on live location data. It flags crowd formations near restricted areas like highways, courthouses, or power stations. These alerts are automatically forwarded to local law enforcement command dashboards and regional fusion centers.

Bodycam and CCTV footage are shared with the **Homeland Security Information Network (HSIN)**. HSIN stores and distributes these feeds across federal, state, and local agencies.

**Clearview AI** scrapes livestreams and social media images for facial recognition. It matches protest attendees against law enforcement and public records databases.

**Palantir Gotham** links names and faces from Clearview to arrest records, license plate scans, housing data, and known associates. The system assigns risk labels based on frequency of attendance, location history, and network ties.

**Geofence warrants** are issued to Google and Apple for device location data. Devices present within a designated protest zone are identified and linked to user accounts.

**Echosec** monitors open-source platforms like Twitter, Reddit, TikTok, Telegram, and Discord. It detects spikes in protest-related keywords and visual content. It maps social graph connections between accounts.

**ShadowDragon** aggregates metadata from posts and messages. It identifies central nodes in activist networks. These are flagged as organizers and added to threat assessments.

**Microsoft 365 Purview** scans internal communications in workplaces using compliance filters. Public employees discussing or planning to attend protests are flagged in internal risk dashboards.

**Slack Enterprise Grid** and **Zoom IQ for Sales** monitor chat and call content on large team platforms. Phrases linked to protest or direct action are logged for managerial review.

**CrowdTangle** monitors viral content across Instagram and Facebook. It flags hashtags, images, and links associated with protests. These are routed to Trust & Safety teams for downranking or removal.

**YouTube’s CSAI Match** checks uploaded protest footage against existing flagged content. Reposts or high-emotion visuals are removed automatically. Flagged accounts lose access to livestreaming and monetization.

**X (Twitter)** uses internal detection tools to flag coordinated posting behavior. Users resharing protest links at high velocity are added to automated suppression queues.

**CLEAR by Thomson Reuters** compiles personal identity files using address history, court records, credit data, and vehicle registration. Law enforcement accesses this data during investigations of protest participants.

**Skopenow** links individual names and phone numbers from flyers, donation platforms, or livestream chats to public records and social media accounts. These profiles are stored for legal targeting or HR actions.

**Banjo’s** location scoring system flags protest disruptions near infrastructure. These events trigger response escalation: mutual aid requests, crowd control deployments, or National Guard notification.

**Stripe** and **PayPal** monitor transaction clusters to activist-linked bail funds and supply groups. Unusual donation patterns generate suspicious activity reports. These reports are forwarded to internal risk teams and, in some cases, to **FinCEN**.

**BlackRock’s Aladdin** and **MSCI ESG** pull location-based protest data into investment risk models. Cities with protest activity are scored for volatility and flagged on corporate watchlists.

Compiled reports from Fūsus, Banjo, Clearview, and platform moderation tools are used to generate law enforcement bulletins. These bulletins are distributed to state AG offices, fusion centers, and contractor threat intelligence teams.

Fusion centers insert names, locations, and footage into **Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs)**. These entries justify continued surveillance and allow information sharing across jurisdictions.

These systems do not wait for violent incidents. They act when enough signal overlaps to match threat pattern criteria. April 19 protest activity will be processed across these systems in parallel. Each signal increases the probability of designation, suppression, or legal action.
