The clinic at [Hevi Mala](project-frame.md) is the fiction project's
central site. It acts at once as surgery ward, radio relay, shelter,
waypoint, and political liability. Stories need not begin there, but
they remain accountable to it. The clinic is what keeps the project's
separate narratives in the same field.

The building is a damaged farmhouse held together by patchwork repair.
Its second story has largely collapsed, while the ground level remains
in use through reinforcement, improvisation, and refusal to leave. The
interior is sparse and functional: an entry and triage space, a narrow
hallway, sleeping rooms, a surgical room, locked storage, a tea nook,
and a radio loft improvised into the surviving part of the stairwell.
The site is not orderly in the abstract. It is orderly in the sense
that the people working there know where the important things are.

The grounds around the clinic matter as much as the rooms. The yard,
wellhead, orchard, silo, and route toward the canal all extend the
clinic's operating space. These are places where people arrive, wait,
watch, hide, rinse tools, move supplies, or decide whether to risk
being seen. The clinic has no clean perimeter. Its boundary is made by
use, not by fencing.

Its survival depends on distributed usefulness rather than consensus.
Smugglers use it as a midpoint. Fighters know that destroying it would
shift local instability rather than resolve it. Border authorities read
it as a known variable. Aid systems can cite it as proof that help has
not vanished completely. The clinic is therefore neutral only in an
operational sense. It survives because too many opposed systems have
reasons to leave it standing.

Daily function is unstable but patterned. Leyla drives medical
decisions. Bissan governs supplies and external access. Haval keeps the
radio live when possible. Amina's labor makes the place inhabitable.
Ciwan brings the pressure of governance and factional expectations to a
site that cannot fully absorb them. There is no formal hierarchy strong
enough to settle everything, but there is a practical order that holds
until exhaustion, conflict, or shortage breaks it.

Those breakpoints are part of the setting's recurring logic. Night
shifts can leave the radio exposed. Leyla can refuse sleep long past
sense. When Bissan leaves to procure supplies, everyone else feels the
gap immediately. If Amina disappears for too long, the absence becomes
its own signal. These recurring instabilities help stories register the
clinic as a living site under pressure rather than a static backdrop.

The clinic should therefore be written as breached but not broken. It
is always too exposed to last and too embedded to remove. That tension
is the condition that gives Hevi Mala its narrative center.
