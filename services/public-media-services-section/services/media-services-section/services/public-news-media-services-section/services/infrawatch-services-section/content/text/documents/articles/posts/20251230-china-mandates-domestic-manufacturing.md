# China mandates domestic manufacturing equipment used for computer chips
China has begun enforcing a new requirement that chipmakers use at least 50 percent domestically produced equipment when adding new semiconductor manufacturing capacity, according to people familiar with the matter. The rule, which has not been formally announced, is being applied through government approval processes for new fabrication plants and expansion projects, marking a significant escalation in Beijing’s push for technological self-reliance.

The policy comes as China faces tightening export controls from the United States and its allies, particularly on advanced chipmaking tools. By mandating a minimum share of local equipment in new capacity, authorities are seeking to accelerate the development of China’s domestic semiconductor equipment industry while reducing exposure to foreign suppliers.

Under the new approach, chipmakers planning to expand production lines must demonstrate that at least half of the tools they intend to install are sourced from Chinese vendors. Exceptions may be granted for specific machines that domestic firms cannot yet supply, but industry sources say approvals are increasingly conditioned on substituting local alternatives wherever possible.

The rule is expected to benefit a growing group of Chinese equipment manufacturers that specialize in etching, deposition, and other key fabrication steps. These companies have made rapid gains in recent years, supported by state funding and procurement preferences. However, China remains heavily dependent on foreign technology for the most advanced processes, particularly in lithography, where global suppliers still dominate.

Foreign equipment makers could see their share of China’s new investments shrink as a result, even as overall spending on chip plants remains high. Analysts say the policy may also deepen global fragmentation in the semiconductor supply chain, as countries pursue parallel ecosystems under growing geopolitical pressure.

For Beijing, the mandate reflects a strategic calculation that near-term inefficiencies are acceptable if they help build a resilient, self-sustaining semiconductor base. For the global industry, it signals that China’s drive to localize critical technologies is entering a more forceful phase.
