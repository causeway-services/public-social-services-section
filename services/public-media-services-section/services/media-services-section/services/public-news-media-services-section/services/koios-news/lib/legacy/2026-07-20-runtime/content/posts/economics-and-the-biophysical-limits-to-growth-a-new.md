A new collection edited by Edward Fullbrook and Jamie Morgan, [23 Responses to Economics' Biggest-ever Question](https://rwer.wordpress.com/2026/07/14/23-responses-to-economics-biggest-ever-question/), puts the limits to growth — as an intellectual problem, a policy problem, and a disciplinary crisis — on the table in terms the economics profession cannot easily dismiss. Subtitled "Economics and the Biophysical Limits to Economic Growth," the volume's framing rests on a claim made by natural science: the global economy caused the climate crisis, and the crisis, now in an accelerating phase, threatens the economy's own existence. For a discipline that has "loosely guided the global economy's national economies" for over a century, that is not a refinement. It is a structural challenge.

## The Policy Architecture Already Built on a Bet

The difficulty is that major institutional decisions have not waited for this reckoning. Green growth — the premise that economic expansion can be decoupled from resource use and made consistent with ecological stability — is not a fringe hypothesis. [Green growth](https://unevenearth.org/2022/10/green-growth/) by Winne van Woerden for Uneven Earth documents it as the governing assumption embedded in the European Green Deal, the Paris Agreement, IPCC reports, and the US Inflation Reduction Act, promoted by leading multilateral organizations and treated as common sense across national and international policymaking.

The institutional bet embedded in those frameworks is that the answer to the collection's central question is yes — growth can continue, cleanly enough, within biophysical limits. If the 23 responses suggest otherwise, the implications don't settle in academic journals. They land in every policy instrument that has already staked its architecture on decoupling working at the required scale.

## The Ecomodernist Premise and Its Discontents

Green growth is not simply an optimistic reading of the data. It rests on a specific intellectual foundation: an ecomodernist belief in technological progress holding that there are no limits to growth because there are no limits to human ingenuity. From this premise, the limits-to-growth tradition represents not a scientific constraint to be accommodated but a failure of imagination to be overcome. Innovation, efficiency, and circular-economy design will, in theory, allow economic output to expand indefinitely while the ecological costs of that expansion fall toward zero — a process the green growth framework calls decoupling.

Van Woerden's analysis for Uneven Earth argues that this foundation is dangerously misguided, and that the decoupling concept on which green growth's credibility depends does not hold up to scrutiny. This is the contested ground the Fullbrook and Morgan collection enters. If decoupling is not achievable at the scale and pace that the current policy architecture requires, then every framework premised on it — every Green Deal, every Paris alignment, every climate instrument that assumes growth and ecological stability are compatible — has been built on an assumption the evidence does not support.

The stakes of that evidentiary question are not symmetrical. A collection confirming that decoupling is feasible would validate the direction multilateral institutions have already chosen. A collection arguing otherwise is not merely an academic critique; it is a challenge to the legitimacy of the entire policy generation that the green growth consensus produced.

## What Remains to Be Reckoned

The Fullbrook and Morgan volume arrived this month, and its 23 responses represent the fuller substance that the collection's framing only introduces. What the framing already establishes is that the volume proceeds from the premise that mainstream economics has not adequately grappled with biophysical limits — that a discipline which guided the global economy through a century of expansion is confronting, in the accelerating climate crisis, the consequence of that guidance.

The green growth apparatus documented by van Woerden is the discipline's current answer: growth can continue, technology will provide, decoupling will scale. The 23 responses in the Fullbrook and Morgan collection engage that answer formally and in detail. The significance of the volume does not depend on which view ultimately prevails. It depends on the fact that the question is now being asked — systematically, by 23 contributors, in a dedicated collection — at the moment when the climate crisis has removed the option of treating it as merely theoretical.
