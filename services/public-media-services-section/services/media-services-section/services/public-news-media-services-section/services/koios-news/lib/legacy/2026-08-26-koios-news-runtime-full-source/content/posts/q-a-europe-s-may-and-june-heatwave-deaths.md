Public health agencies and climate scientists have published a wave of death estimates in the weeks since Europe's record-setting summer heatwaves, and the figures vary considerably depending on how each organisation defines and measures heat-related mortality. [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-europes-may-and-june-heatwave-deaths-and-how-they-were-counted/) reports that France's national public health agency tallied 2,025 excess deaths during the seven-day period when the June heat peaked, while German and Dutch authorities put their own totals for the same week at 5,753 and 533, respectively. Dr. Christopher Callahan of Indiana University, working alongside Prof. Andrew Dessler—director of the Texas Center for Extreme Weather at Texas A&M University—applied statistical modelling to French mortality data for Carbon Brief and arrived at 2,766 deaths across an 18-day window running from 12 to 29 June. Researchers at Imperial College London, the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, and the Met Office used the same statistical approach to estimate 2,700 deaths tied to the UK's May and June heat events, with 42% of those fatalities attributed to human-caused climate change. The divergence in these totals reflects not only differences in the severity of heat experienced across each country but also the distinct analytical frameworks each authority applied.

## A Century of Measuring Heat and Death

The scientific literature connecting temperature extremes to human mortality has been accumulating for nearly a hundred years, [according to Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-europes-may-and-june-heatwave-deaths-and-how-they-were-counted/). The field's foundations include a 1923 paper by geographer Ellsworth Huntington and economist Margaret Justin, who examined New York City mortality records covering the years 1882 through 1888 and found that fatality rates climbed steeply once temperatures exceeded 17C. As human-caused carbon emissions have driven global temperatures upward, scientists have directed growing attention toward the mortality implications of that warming. Research specifically focused on individual heatwave events is a more recent development, with Chicago's deadly 1995 heatwave among the first events to receive systematic academic study. Over roughly the past decade, investigators have gone further, building two-stage analyses that first quantify deaths during a particular heat event and then assign a portion of those fatalities to the human influence on the climate.

Carbon Brief notes that a 2016 paper in Environmental Research Letters—the first of its type that the outlet covered—found that human-caused climate change accounted for 506 of 735 summer deaths in Paris and 64 of 315 in London during the 2003 European heatwave. More recent research published in Climatic Change attributed 27% of deaths in a 2018 Zurich heatwave to human-driven warming, and a paper in Science Advances placed 11 to 15% of fatalities during a 2021 British Columbia heatwave in the same category. Dr. Callahan, an assistant professor at Indiana University's O'Neill School of Public and Environmental Affairs, told Carbon Brief this category of research has "really exploded" in recent years: "It is really only in the last five to 10 years that we have seen this, partly because it does require interdisciplinary expertise. You need people who know how to run the epidemiological models and you need a climate analysis of the counterfactual [world] without climate change, which is its own effort."

## How the Numbers Are Reached

Measuring heat deaths presents an inherent challenge: high temperatures rarely appear on death certificates as a primary cause, yet the physiological burden of heat—the effort placed on the heart and kidneys as the body works to stay cool—worsens outcomes from cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, dementia, and Alzheimer's, and can trigger acute kidney injury, [Carbon Brief reports](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-europes-may-and-june-heatwave-deaths-and-how-they-were-counted/). Because death certificates provide only a partial picture, public health authorities have converged on two main estimation strategies. Dr. Garyfallos Konstantinoudis, who studies excess mortality methodology at Imperial College's Grantham Institute for Climate Change and the Environment, told Carbon Brief there is "no ground truth" in this area: "We don't know what the heat-related deaths are, so we rely on different models to describe the picture."

The more widely used approach—"excess deaths"—measures how many more people died during a heatwave than a historical baseline for the same calendar period would predict. Germany's federal statistics agency, Destatis, used this method when it reported on 14 July that 23,932 deaths were recorded in the week of 22 to 28 June, against a four-year average of 18,179 for that same calendar week—a difference of 5,753 and a 32% increase above the norm. The Netherlands' RIVM applied a comparable baseline comparison and estimated 577 excess deaths during the same seven-day period, revising upward from a preliminary figure of 480. Dr. Callahan told Carbon Brief that the excess deaths approach can be assembled quickly and without complex modelling—"a short-term, high-impact, rapid estimate of mortality."

The second strategy uses long-run mortality data to establish a statistical relationship between temperature and death rates at a specific location, then applies that model to estimate how many people a given heatwave killed. This is the method Callahan and Dessler used for their France analysis, and the approach the Imperial College, LSHTM, and Met Office team applied to UK figures. A third technique—recording on death certificates deaths explicitly attributed to heatstroke—has largely fallen out of use across Europe, which Callahan told Carbon Brief is a "smart move" because it misses the broader range of heat-induced cardiovascular and respiratory failures.

## What the Figures Reveal

The cumulative picture that emerges from published government and research estimates, [as compiled by Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-europes-may-and-june-heatwave-deaths-and-how-they-were-counted/), is one of widespread mortality across the continent during June. Multiple European countries each recorded more than 1,000 heat-related deaths during the late June heatwave, with Germany's toll surpassing 5,000 on most estimates. A separate statistical modelling analysis by Callahan put the Europe-wide toll at approximately 13,975 over the 14-day period from 15 to 28 June. The EuroMoMo surveillance network, tracking mortality across 27 countries, estimated more than 10,650 excess deaths for the single week of 22 to 28 June alone.

The difficulty of measuring these deaths shares structural features with the long-standing challenge of quantifying mortality from air pollution—another cause of death that, until recently, was absent from death certificates. Dr. Konstantinoudis noted to Carbon Brief that the scientific evidence on air pollution deaths is now extensive enough that no one questions whether particulate matter is lethal, but he added that "this sort of messaging for heat is more recent." As the methods for measuring heat mortality mature, the figures governments and researchers publish in the wake of each extreme weather event will continue sharpening the tools that public health institutions rely on to track the human cost of a warming climate.
