Hundreds of Indigenous delegates gathered in Geneva this week for the 19th annual session of the United Nations Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (EMRIP), confronting a reality where climate volatility, militarization, and industrial extraction no longer act as separate crises but as a single, compounding force. [As climate and conflict collide, Indigenous leaders confront overlapping crises at the UN](https://ictnews.org/news/as-climate-and-conflict-collide-indigenous-leaders-confront-overlapping-crises-at-the-un/) reports that despite the adoption of the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples nearly two decades ago, the lands and lives the document aims to protect are vanishing under rising seas, dessicated by drought, and targeted by extractive industries. Binota Moy Dhamai, a former chair of EMRIP and member of the Tripura community from Bangladesh, noted that these drivers reinforce one another, creating a feedback loop where legal marginalization accelerates environmental loss and vice versa.

## The Convergence of Extraction and Violence

The mechanism of collapse becomes visible when examining how resource demands drive both ecological degradation and human conflict. Valmaine Toki, the EMRIP member representing the Pacific region and of Māori descent, explained that conflict invariably leads to the dispossession of traditional lands, drawing Indigenous populations into fights over resources they did not initiate. This dynamic creates a lose-lose situation where communities face alienation from their territories while simultaneously bearing the brunt of violence. The session in Geneva highlighted that militarization and extractivism are not merely concurrent issues but intertwined strategies that strip communities of agency. In regions where the state fails to recognize Indigenous legal status, such as Botswana, this convergence leaves populations like the Indigenous Nama vulnerable to forced assimilation and displacement without recourse to national legal systems. Nichodimas Cooper, a Nama delegate, described the UN forum as a rare platform to pressure states that otherwise ignore their inalienable rights, emphasizing that the lack of domestic recognition makes international solidarity a survival necessity rather than a diplomatic exercise.

## Frontlines of the Global Energy Transition

Nowhere is the tension between global climate goals and local survival more acute than in the push for minerals required for the energy transition. In northern Ontario, the Canadian government has granted mining rights on the Breathing Lands, a vast wetland ecosystem within the Ring of Fire mineral deposit that holds enormous stores of nickel and other metals essential for green technology. org/news/as-climate-and-conflict-collide-indigenous-leaders-confront-overlapping-crises-at-the-un/) details how these extraction projects are proceeding despite strong resistance from the Nishnawbe Aski Nation, which represents 49 communities in the region. Kohen Mattinas of Lac Seul First Nation and Constance Lake First Nation stated that the right to free, prior, and informed consent is being bypassed by legislation allowing projects to advance through accelerated processes. In response, Mattinas co-founded Okiniwak, a youth organization established in 2025 to organize rallies and protect land rights on the frontlines. The situation illustrates how the global rush to mitigate climate change through technological substitution can replicate the very extractive logics that caused the crisis, treating Indigenous territories as sacrifice zones for the broader economy.

## Decolonization as Climate Defense

For many delegates, the fight against climate breakdown is inseparable from the unfinished project of decolonization. Viro Xulue, a Kanak advisor for the Drehu Customary Council in New Caledonia, framed the resistance to external control as a prerequisite for protecting resources and ensuring self-determination for future generations. He pointed out that 170 years of colonization have left the Kanak people facing persistent inequity, where the injustice of historical dispossession remains unresolved even as new climate threats emerge. Although the International Court of Justice has issued advisory opinions holding countries accountable for climate damage, Xulue noted that legal victories at the international level do not automatically translate to justice on the ground for Indigenous communities fighting for independence. The connection between colonization and climate vulnerability is particularly stark in the Pacific, where rising seas threaten to erase nations that never contributed significantly to global emissions. The delegates in Geneva argued that true climate resilience requires respecting the self-determination of peoples who have managed these ecosystems for millennia, rather than imposing external solutions that further erode local governance.

## Emerging Threats and the Limits of Recognition

As the session progressed, delegates identified new vectors of oppression emerging alongside traditional threats, including the rapid deployment of artificial intelligence and the shrinking of civic space. Dhamai highlighted the need for data sovereignty and protections against technologies developed without community consent, warning that digital transformation could become another tool for marginalization if Indigenous perspectives are not embedded in international standards from the outset. The approaching 20th anniversary of the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples next year serves as a mirror for the international community, reflecting a central question that has remained unchanged since 2007: whether governments are prepared to uphold rights they have already recognized on paper. The gathering in Geneva made clear that the declaration alone cannot stop the dynamic compounding of crises; it requires a fundamental shift in how power is distributed and how resources are managed. The connections forged between delegates from Canada, the Pacific, Africa, and Asia offer a pathway to strengthen messaging and collaborate on resistance, yet the window for effective action narrows as the overlapping threats of conflict and climate intensify. This event makes visible the fragility of a global order that continues to prioritize extraction over the survival of the communities guarding the world's remaining biodiversity.
