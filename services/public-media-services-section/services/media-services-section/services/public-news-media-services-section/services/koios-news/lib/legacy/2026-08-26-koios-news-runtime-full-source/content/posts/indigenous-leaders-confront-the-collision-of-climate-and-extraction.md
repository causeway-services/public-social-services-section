Hundreds of Indigenous delegates have gathered in Geneva for the 19th annual session of the United Nations Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (EMRIP) to address the converging threats of climate breakdown, militarization, and resource extraction, as reported in [As climate and conflict collide, Indigenous leaders confront overlapping crises at the UN](https://ictnews.org/news/as-climate-and-conflict-collide-indigenous-leaders-confront-overlapping-crises-at-the-un/). Nearly two decades after UNDRIP's adoption, participants report that its promised rights remain under siege. The discussions reveal a pattern where environmental degradation and violent conflict do not merely coexist but actively reinforce one another.

## The Compounding Nature of Crisis

The central argument emerging from the Geneva session is that climate change, militarization, extractivism, and legal marginalization function as a single, compounding system rather than isolated challenges. Binota Moy Dhamai, a former chair of EMRIP from the Chittagong Hill Tracts in Bangladesh, noted that these forces reinforce one another, creating a trap for Indigenous peoples. Valmaine Toki, the EMRIP member representing the Pacific region, described this dynamic as a "lose-lose situation" where conflict leads to the dispossession of traditional lands even as the people are drawn into the fighting. The source text highlights that Indigenous lands are currently being battered by record-breaking storms, dried out by drought, and submerged by rising seas, all while hundreds of Indigenous people continue dying in wars and while defending their lands, even as they are persecuted by government officials. This convergence means that a community facing a climate-driven disaster often finds its capacity to respond crippled by simultaneous military conflict or legal exclusion.

## Extraction Over Consent

A primary fault line exposed during the proceedings is the continued advancement of resource extraction projects without the free, prior, and informed consent of Indigenous inhabitants. In northern Ontario, the Canadian government has granted mining rights on the Breathing Lands, a vast wetland ecosystem that serves as a massive carbon sink and biodiversity hub. This area, part of the Ring of Fire mineral deposit, holds enormous stores of nickel and other metals, yet extraction proceeds despite strong resistance from the Nishnawbe Aski Nation, which represents 49 communities. Kohen Mattinas of Lac Seul First Nation and Constance Lake First Nation, representing the nation at EMRIP, emphasized that the right to consent must be respected, noting that new Canadian legislation allows projects to be advanced through an accelerated process. In response, Mattinas co-founded Okiniwak, a youth organization that organizes rallies and engages in international advocacy to protect their land.

## The Burden on the Unrecognized

The stakes are particularly high for Indigenous groups in nations that do not legally recognize their status, leaving them with few domestic avenues for redress. Nichodimas Cooper, an Indigenous Nama from Botswana, explained that the lack of national recognition makes it difficult to effect change locally, as his people face forced assimilation and displacement. For communities like the Nama, the UN platform offers a rare opportunity to pressure states to respect inalienable rights. Similarly, Viro Xulue, a Kanak advisor from New Caledonia, linked the struggle against climate change directly to the ongoing process of decolonization. He stated that respecting self-determination is essential for protecting resources and securing a future for the next generation, noting that 170 years of colonization have left a persistent inequity for Kanak people. Even with legal victories, such as an International Court of Justice advisory opinion holding countries accountable for climate damage, the injustice remains unresolved on the ground. These examples demonstrate how historical colonization and modern climate policy intersect to maintain systemic vulnerability.

## Emerging Threats and Future Standards

Beyond physical land disputes, the delegates are also confronting emerging digital threats that could further erode Indigenous sovereignty. Artificial intelligence is among the session's emerging issues, and delegates are pushing for data sovereignty and stronger protections against technologies being developed without free, prior, and informed consent. For delegates like Mattinas, the international forum serves a dual function, advancing formal advocacy while enabling the horizontal exchange of knowledge that strengthens communities in turn. "Through speaking at these international forums, we're also able to connect with other Indigenous people around the world who are either facing the same things, or they face similar things, and we just kind of learn from each other, bring back that knowledge home," Mattinas said. Dhamai expressed hope that EMRIP would provide guidance on these mounting issues, alongside climate finance and the global energy transition, so that Indigenous perspectives shape international standards rather than merely respond to them. As the 20th anniversary of UNDRIP approaches, the session's discussions are expected to be shaped by a reckoning with that gap: nearly two decades after adoption, the central question before delegates remains the one posed in 2007—not whether Indigenous rights should be recognized, but whether governments are prepared to uphold them. The gathering in Geneva makes visible the reality that without a fundamental shift in how extraction and governance operate, the overlapping crises of climate and conflict could continue to compound, leaving Indigenous peoples to absorb the most severe damages of a destabilizing world.
