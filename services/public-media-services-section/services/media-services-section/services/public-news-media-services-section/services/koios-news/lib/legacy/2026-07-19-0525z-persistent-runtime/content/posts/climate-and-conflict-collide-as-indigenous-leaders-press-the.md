Indigenous leaders arrived at the United Nations this week — hundreds of Indigenous delegates gathering in Geneva for the 19th annual session of the UN Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples — to push back against a compounding threat to Indigenous land rights and lives. The forces bearing down on their communities are not moving on separate tracks. [As climate and conflict collide, Indigenous leaders confront overlapping crises at the UN](https://ictnews.org/news/as-climate-and-conflict-collide-indigenous-leaders-confront-overlapping-crises-at-the-un/) describes territories battered by record-breaking storms, desiccated by drought, and vanishing under rising seas — while peoples are simultaneously dying by the hundreds in wars and while defending that same ground. That convergence is not incidental. Climate breakdown, armed conflict, state pressure, and legal marginalization do not move on separate tracks — they reinforce one another, bearing hardest on the communities with the least capacity to absorb any of them.

## A Declaration and the Distance It Has to Travel

Nearly two decades ago, more than 100 countries adopted the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, a framework designed to protect the rights and lives of Indigenous peoples worldwide. The ICT News reporting makes clear that the distance between that adoption and actual protection is where the current crisis is concentrated. Record-breaking storms still batter Indigenous lands. Drought still desiccates them. Seas keep rising, and those territories keep vanishing. The declaration's existence has not paused any of it.

The political texture of that gap is visible in separate reporting: [Nepal's New Government's Fast Track Project Priority Sparks Conflict Over Indigenous Newar Lands in Khokana](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/news/nepals-new-governments-fast-track-project-priority-sparks-conflict-over-indigenous-newar-lands) documents Nepal's new government prioritizing infrastructure in a way that has put it in direct conflict with Indigenous Newar communities over their lands in Khokana.

## Climate Is Not the Only Front

The climate threats catalogued in the ICT News report do not operate in isolation. The death toll documented in that same piece runs into the hundreds, spread across wars and acts of direct territorial defense. Government persecution compounds the picture further. All three forces — climate breakdown, armed conflict, state pressure — are running simultaneously against the same communities, not sequenced across separate timelines or distinct geographies. The overlap is not coincidental. Each pressure intensifies the others: communities defending their territories in active conflict zones are also the communities absorbing the first and worst effects of changing weather systems.

"Climate change, militarization, extractivism, and legal marginalization reinforce one another," said Binota Moy Dhamai, who is Tripura from the Chittagong Hill Tracts in Bangladesh and a former chair of EMRIP. That framing names the structural dynamic the Geneva gathering exists to address: not separate crises running in parallel, but interlocking pressures that compound each other's damage.

## What the Forum Makes Legible

Bringing these crises to the United Nations rather than to individual national capitals signals something about the scale at which Indigenous leaders see this problem — and where they locate the failure of authority that shapes it. The 19th session exists explicitly to discuss implementing solutions to these abuses, not only to document them. A UN forum is also a record: what was said, by whom, and when. Whether that record translates into anything material depends on whether the states that adopted the declaration treat it as a constraint on their own development priorities and security decisions.

The crises described this week did not begin at the UN, and a forum alone does not end them. What this confrontation makes legible is the distance between a framework built to protect Indigenous land rights and the physical reality of those lands today — a distance that grows as climate disruption accelerates.
