Sunday's World Cup final between Argentina and Spain, scheduled at a venue just outside New York City, faces an unwanted complication: smoke from Canadian wildfires has pushed air quality across more than a dozen states in the Midwest and Northeast into ranges officials describe as hazardous, and FIFA, soccer's governing body, has disclosed no contingency plan to protect players or fans, [according to Grist](https://grist.org/extreme-weather/wildfire-smoke-threatens-the-world-cup-final-fifa-still-doesnt-have-a-plan/).

Smoke drifting south from Canadian wildfires settled over a broad stretch of the region, turning New York's sky orange and hazy and prompting the state to issue a Thursday alert after air quality index readings climbed into categories the state labeled "unhealthy" and "very unhealthy." Officials urged residents to stay indoors where possible. As of Friday morning, readings near the stadium remained in the unhealthy range. Forecasters expected conditions to ease to "moderate" by kickoff Sunday, though Grist noted that smoke patterns can shift quickly.

The stadium where Lionel Messi and Lamine Yamal are set to face each other is open-air, meaning it cannot be sealed against smoke. That leaves both players and the attending crowd exposed to irritants that can cause throat burning, coughing, and headaches — risks that are heightened for children, elderly spectators, and anyone with a respiratory condition such as asthma, [Grist reported](https://grist.org/extreme-weather/wildfire-smoke-threatens-the-world-cup-final-fifa-still-doesnt-have-a-plan/).

Players were already training in the affected air. Spain's squad held an outdoor practice in New Jersey on Thursday. Dr. Courtney Howard, an emergency room physician and Global Climate and Health Alliance official, told the Associated Press that elite athletes present a particular concern: "These are high-level athletes who are moving a lot of air through their lungs. They shouldn't be practicing outside if the air quality levels are at hazardous sort of ranges for wildfire-related air pollution."

FIFA has not published any air quality protocols. In a previous statement to Grist, the organization outlined detailed heat-management procedures — mandatory water breaks among them — but made no mention of smoke. When Grist sought comment, neither FIFA nor New York City Department of Health officials responded. FIFA's prior written position was that "climate-related risks are assessed as part of overall tournament planning and managed in close coordination with host cities, stadium authorities, and national agencies."

Other leagues have moved faster. The National Women's Soccer League policy requires additional hydration breaks once the AQI crosses 101, and mandates cancellation or postponement above 200. A Thursday NWSL match at Citi Field in New York received those extra breaks. Major League Baseball rescheduled a Philadelphia game, and Major League Soccer called off a Chicago fixture, citing smoke.

Nicholas Watanabe, a sport and entertainment management professor at the University of South Carolina, told Grist that FIFA's silence on the issue amounts to a gamble that has so far paid off over the tournament's five weeks — but that luck is not preparation. "FIFA seems unprepared for these wildfires," he said. "At the same time, it looks like the worst of the pollution will dissipate before the World Cup Final, so FIFA will dodge a bullet and likely push forward with the match."

Trinity Rodman, who played in the smoke-affected NWSL game in New York, questioned where the threshold lies: "If we have to have a hydration break every 15 minutes, then we shouldn't be playing the game."

Tour de France leader Tadej Pogačar, whose race this year has been disrupted by wildfires, smoke, and extreme heat, framed the problem more broadly. "I would change the whole calendar," he said earlier this month. "It's not something I can do." That authority, as Watanabe noted to Grist, rests with governing bodies, not athletes — and he argued this near-miss should prompt action: "Hopefully this is a wakeup call to FIFA and all other sport leagues and competitions of the need for regulations and a comprehensive plan to deal with climate risks."
