New polling data indicates that a substantial share of American likely voters connect the worsening of extreme weather to the broader climate emergency, even as some politicians work to dismiss or minimize that link, the Guardian [reported on Saturday](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study).

Data for Progress, a progressive polling organization, gave the Guardian exclusive access to survey results showing that 61% of American likely voters perceive a rise in extreme weather events over the preceding five years, [according to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study). The finding suggests that direct exposure to heat, flooding, and other weather extremes is shaping public opinion in ways that cross partisan boundaries.

The survey revealed notable cross-party agreement on that point. Among Democratic respondents, 72% said extreme weather had worsened; the figure stood at 63% among Independents and at 46% — nearly half — among Republicans, [according to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study). Grace Adcox, a senior strategist at Data for Progress, told the Guardian that regional differences in extreme weather experiences help account for variation in those perceptions.

When respondents were asked to connect specific weather types to climate change, the results reflected broad public recognition of that link. According to the Guardian's account of the poll, 67% of respondents named heatwaves as worsening because of climate change — topping the list of weather types surveyed. Majorities also identified hurricanes (64%), severe thunderstorms (63%), droughts (62%), and wildfires (59%) as climate-linked. Among Republican respondents specifically, the Guardian reported, 48% chose heatwaves as the weather event they most strongly associated with climate change, the highest share within that partisan group.

Adcox told the Guardian that nearly everyone across the United States had experienced unusually hot days in recent years, framing that shared exposure as a natural entry point for bipartisan conversation about extreme weather. She said the poll asked participants whether specific weather forms had increased and worsened, and described the results as demonstrating that climate concerns retain bipartisan traction.

Anthony Leiserowitz, director of the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication, told the Guardian that the Data for Progress results were consistent with conclusions drawn from Yale's own surveys, which have increasingly shown Americans connecting extreme weather and heatwaves to the climate crisis. The Guardian also cited a separate spring survey conducted by the Yale program finding that roughly 27% of respondents attributed recent climate shifts primarily to natural cycles, [according to the report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study). Yale senior research scientist Jennifer Marlon described that persistent belief as probably the biggest ongoing misconception about climate change, the Guardian reported, noting that many Americans still think warming is due to some kind of natural cycle even as the evidence points elsewhere.

Senator Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island told the Guardian he has long criticized what he called "climate-hushing" — the quieting of public discussion about climate change among politicians — and said new polling data adds to the weight of evidence that politicians should be speaking openly about dire consequences rather than going quiet on the subject.

The Data for Progress findings land against a backdrop of sustained political efforts to minimize the climate emergency in Washington. The Guardian noted that despite the survey results, Donald Trump and aligned politicians have continued to dismiss global heating as a hoax while pushing back against climate policy, even as public polling increasingly documents the opposite sentiment.

Extreme weather already carries a measurable human toll. According to [the Guardian's report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study), floods, hurricanes, tornadoes, and high temperatures kill people across the United States annually, and their combined impact represents a significant ongoing burden.

Data for Progress described the survey, which was shared exclusively with the Guardian, as intended to open conversation about climate concerns at a moment of scorching summer conditions — an opening move in a broader effort to demonstrate that public concern about the climate crisis has not been suppressed by political attempts to sideline it, [the Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jul/19/climate-crisis-us-voters-study).

Whether the cross-partisan public recognition documented in the survey — particularly the nearly half of Republican respondents who connected heatwaves to climate change — creates meaningful pressure on elected officials who have aligned with the Trump administration's dismissal of climate science remains the central unresolved question the poll raises.
