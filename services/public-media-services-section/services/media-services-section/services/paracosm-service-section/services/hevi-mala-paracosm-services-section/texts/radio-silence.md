*Start Over, Start Right*

"Wasn't there a call today?" Haval asked.

He was at the bottom of the stairs now, one hand still on the railing. The radio was on upstairs, cycling a soft hum, nothing clean. The frequency that sometimes meant a supply window, or just a man in Raqqa testing his new antenna.

Leyla looked up from the sink. She didn't stop washing.

> "Not today."

"You sure?"

"If it was today, Bissan would've said."

A pause. Then from the hallway:

> "I didn't say because I didn't know."

Bissan stepped into the doorway, rubbing her temple with the heel of her palm. She hadn't slept. That meant something had been rescheduled. Or forgotten. Or bargained away and no one had told her yet.

"Could be tomorrow," she added.

Haval frowned. "I remember it being today."

"From where?"

"From my head."

That got half a laugh out of Amina, carrying a box of bandages to the corner shelf.

By 08:52, Haval had scanned the band twice. Still nothing useful. Still just that fluttery edge of signal drift.

He came back down.

"Still nothing," he said.

Bissan didn't look up. "If they called early, it's on them."

"If they call late?"

"Then we're not late."

Leyla dried her hands on her pants and asked, "What did we even need?"

There was a pause. No one answered.

Amina said, "Soap, maybe. Or are we out again."

Ciwan walked in carrying an empty water jug and said, "Out of what?"

Leyla pointed at him. "That."

---

At 09:10, Haval keyed the mic anyway. He didn't know what to say, but saying nothing felt worse.

> "Clinic Nine. This is Clinic Nine. Standing by for confirmation. Standard window. Please advise."

He waited.
Static.
An echo from somewhere, too far gone to trace.
Then nothing.

He let go of the mic and leaned back.

"Could've been tomorrow," he muttered.

Behind him, on the shelf, someone had left a pack of gauze with the corner torn. Useless. He pressed it flat anyway.

At 10:12, they started arguing. Not loudly. Just enough to hear it through two walls and over the kettle.

"It wasn't my job to prep the list," Bissan said.
She was sitting on the edge of the folding cot, boots half-laced, forehead pressed to her hand.

"I didn't say it was," Leyla replied.
She was restocking the drawer that never stayed closed, mostly so she didn't have to look up.

"You're implying."

"No. I'm assuming if you needed something, you'd say so."

"I always say so."

"Did you say so this time?"

Bissan didn't answer.

---

In the back, Ciwan was trying to fix the water filter. The rubber gasket kept folding in on itself. He pressed harder.

Haval stood nearby, arms crossed, watching the floor like it might open a few answers.

"You remember the last call?" he asked, to no one in particular.

"The one with the gauze or the one with the buckets?" Amina asked, folding linen with the kind of precision that made answers seem optional.

"The one where they said they might move the window," he said. "The timing."

"They say a lot of things."

---

By 11:00, it was clear no one knew when the next call would be.

Not exactly.

Not in a way that could be proven.

Leyla checked the side room for extra IV lines. Didn't find any. Said nothing.
Ciwan gave up on the filter and went to log his failed repair in the corner notebook — only to find someone had torn out the last two pages.

No one asked why. They just started listing supplies aloud, the way people do when silence starts sounding too much like error.

The truck came two days later. Not early. Not late. Just wrong.

No markings. Just dust and dents and a man in the passenger seat who didn't get out. The driver spoke Arabic with a southern lilt and looked past everyone when he talked.

"This is for Nine," he said.

Leyla checked the manifest. It was handwritten, smudged, and partially in French.

Bissan scanned the list.
"Not ours," she said.

"It says Nine."

"It says *Unité Mobile Nine.* That's the dental crew."

The driver shrugged. "Close enough."

---

They unloaded it anyway. It was that or nothing.

Three cartons of toothpaste. Seventeen toothbrushes, individually wrapped.
A portable UV sterilizer no one knew how to plug in.
Two dozen rubber bite guards.
A set of plastic dentures, child-sized.

No gauze. No saline. No fuel tabs.
One of the cartons had a corner soaked through — smelled like bleach and mint.

Amina stacked the items in the dry room. Didn't label the shelf. Didn't ask where to put the dentures.

Ciwan opened a box, held up a toothbrush, and said, "At least we'll have clean morale."

No one laughed. Not really.

---

That night, Haval taped a note above the radio:

> *NEXT CALL: ??*
> *IF YOU REMEMBER SOMETHING, SAY IT OUT LOUD.*

Underneath, someone had added in pencil:

> *AND SAY IT TO THE RIGHT PERSON.*
