# Lithium for Batteries: Who Gets the Water, Jobs, and Costs?

## Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries connect two very different landscapes. At one end are electric vehicles, electronic devices, and electricity-storage systems, usually concentrated in cities and industrial economies. At the other are salt flats, mines, processing plants, and Indigenous territories where lithium-bearing materials are extracted. The resulting distributional question is not simply whether lithium mining is “good” or “bad.” It is who receives the benefits, who bears environmental and social risks, and who has authority to decide what trade-offs are acceptable.

The global energy transition strengthens this connection. Lithium demand grew by nearly 30% in 2024, with energy applications accounting for much of the recent growth in demand for battery metals; lithium prices nevertheless fell sharply after the 2021–2022 price surge because supply also expanded rapidly ([International Energy Agency, 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary)). In the IEA’s 2024 Net Zero Emissions scenario, electric vehicles and stationary storage account for more than 90% of lithium demand by 2030, although that is a conditional projection rather than a prediction ([IEA, 2024](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/outlook-for-key-minerals)).

These demand projections help explain political pressure to open or expand mines, but they do not determine how extraction must occur. Battery size, public transportation, material efficiency, recycling, vehicle ownership patterns, and alternative battery chemistries can all change primary-mineral requirements. Nor does lithium’s role in lower-carbon technologies make an individual project environmentally or socially sustainable. Climate benefits arise mainly when batteries displace fossil-fuel use or help integrate low-emissions electricity; extraction impacts, by contrast, are experienced in particular watersheds, workplaces, and communities.

This report uses Chile’s Salar de Atacama as its principal case because it combines large-scale production, extreme aridity, long-standing Indigenous presence, and unusually visible debates over brine and freshwater. Argentina provides a useful institutional comparison. The evidence shows substantial national revenues and some local employment and community funding, but also unresolved hydrogeological uncertainty, uneven bargaining power, and a mismatch between globally distributed climate benefits and locally concentrated risks.

## 1. What is being extracted, and why water accounting is difficult

Lithium is not normally mined as pure metal. Commercial production begins with lithium-bearing hard rock, clay, geothermal fluids, or saline groundwater known as brine. In 2025, Australia was the largest producer of mined lithium, while Chile, China, Argentina, Zimbabwe, Brazil, and several other countries also produced significant quantities. The U.S. Geological Survey estimated global mine output at approximately 290,000 metric tonnes of lithium content in 2025, excluding confidential U.S. production; its figures are estimates expressed as elemental lithium, not tonnes of lithium carbonate ([USGS, *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026*](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-lithium.pdf)).

That unit distinction matters. Lithium carbonate, \(Li_2CO_3\), is only partly lithium by mass:

\[
\text{Lithium fraction in } Li_2CO_3
= \frac{2M_{\mathrm{Li}}}{2M_{\mathrm{Li}}+M_{\mathrm{C}}+3M_{\mathrm{O}}}
\approx 0.188
\]

using approximate atomic masses \(M_{\mathrm{Li}}=6.94\), \(M_{\mathrm{C}}=12.01\), and \(M_{\mathrm{O}}=16.00\). Thus one tonne of lithium corresponds stoichiometrically to about 5.32 tonnes of lithium carbonate equivalent, before process losses. Reports that mix lithium content, lithium carbonate, and “lithium carbonate equivalent” can therefore appear to disagree by more than a factor of five even when describing the same material flow.

At a conventional Andean brine operation, wells pump saline groundwater from beneath a salt flat. The brine is moved through evaporation ponds, where solar energy removes water and progressively concentrates dissolved salts. Operators harvest salts and chemically process the concentrated stream into lithium carbonate or hydroxide. The method exploits favorable geology, high evaporation, and low rainfall, but the same aridity means that disturbances to water systems may be important even when volumes look modest by global industrial standards.

“Water use” can refer to several non-equivalent quantities:

- freshwater withdrawn from an aquifer or surface source;
- water consumed, meaning it is evaporated, embodied in products, or otherwise unavailable for prompt return;
- brine withdrawn from beneath a salt flat;
- water returned after treatment;
- a scarcity-weighted footprint that multiplies consumption by a regional characterization factor; or
- ecological effects such as changes in groundwater level, spring discharge, lagoon condition, or freshwater–brine interfaces.

Brine is too saline for drinking or irrigation without treatment, but that does not make it hydrologically irrelevant. A salar is a coupled system of brine, fresher groundwater, inflows, evaporation, soils, wetlands, and lagoons. If brine pumping changes hydraulic gradients, effects can propagate toward ecological zones even though brine and potable freshwater are not interchangeable commodities. Conversely, counting every litre of brine as if it were a litre of drinking water can exaggerate direct competition for human water supplies. Sound assessment must track the different water stocks separately and then investigate their connections.

## 2. What the water evidence establishes—and what it does not

A 2025 cradle-to-gate study of SQM’s Salar de Atacama and chemical-processing operations evaluated 2022–2023 production using two scarcity-impact methods. It reported 442 cubic metres of “world-equivalent” impact per tonne of lithium product under AWARE and 5.5 cubic metres equivalent under WAVE+, with 87% of the modeled product being lithium carbonate and 13% lithium hydroxide. Concentrated-brine production accounted for 326 cubic metres world-equivalent per tonne under AWARE ([Marinova et al., 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652624040848)).

Those figures are impact indicators, not physical claims that exactly 442 cubic metres of local freshwater were pumped for every tonne. A scarcity footprint weights consumptive use according to a model of local water deprivation; different models embody different assumptions, which is why AWARE and WAVE+ produce numerically different results. The study considered “blue water”—surface water and groundwater—but excluded degradative effects such as eutrophication and acidification. It also relied on confidential primary operating data supplied by SQM, limiting independent reproduction of the inventory ([Marinova et al., methodology and data statement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2024.144635)).

The study found that all its aggregate water indicators improved by approximately 9%–42% between 2020–2021 and 2022–2023, attributing the improvement principally to higher brine recovery and lower energy use in several processing steps. Yet water consumption associated with lithium-carbonate production increased slightly, from 13 to 14 tonnes of water per tonne of lithium product, because greater inputs of chemicals and energy were required. The chemical plant’s use of desalinated water reduced the modeled local scarcity burden of final processing ([Marinova et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2024.144635)).

These findings support a limited conclusion: operational efficiency can reduce some measured water burdens, and the concentration stage is a major modeled hotspot. They do not establish that extraction is ecologically harmless. Product-footprint accounting asks how much burden is attributable to a unit of output; hydrogeological assessment asks how pumping changes a particular basin over space and time. Neither substitutes for the other.

A separate localized modeling study of the Salar de Atacama reported that mining-associated surplus evaporation was no more than about 4% of basin-wide evaporation in the models assessed, and that modeled drawdowns exceeding 25 centimetres to several metres were largely confined to areas the authors did not classify as critical ecosystems ([Mardones et al., 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/17/22/3311)). That is evidence against the simplest claim that all basin drying can be attributed to lithium evaporation ponds. But it remains model-dependent. Results depend on boundary definitions, recharge estimates, subsurface connectivity, pumping histories, and criteria for deciding which locations are ecologically critical.

The most defensible synthesis is therefore neither “lithium is draining all the desert’s water” nor “brine pumping cannot affect freshwater.” The established facts are that extraction deliberately removes brine, conventional ponds deliberately evaporate much of its water, freshwater is used in parts of production, and the basin is extremely arid. The uncertain questions concern the strength, timing, and spatial extent of connections among deep brine, marginal aquifers, springs, wetlands, and lagoons. Long response times add a governance problem: harm might become clear only after years of extraction, while permits and investments create pressure to continue.

Climate variability and other users further complicate attribution. Copper mining, tourism, towns, agriculture, and natural evaporation can affect the same regional water system. Cumulative assessment is therefore more appropriate than evaluating each lithium project as though it occupied an empty watershed.

## 3. New extraction technologies are options, not proven guarantees

Direct lithium extraction, or DLE, is a family of methods that uses sorbents, membranes, solvents, ion exchange, or electrochemical processes to separate lithium from brine without relying primarily on long evaporation sequences. A proposed system may extract lithium, process the brine, and reinject the depleted fluid underground. In principle, this can reduce pond area, shorten processing time, and avoid evaporating most of the pumped brine.

But “direct” does not mean impact-free. A major scientific review found that DLE systems must be evaluated simultaneously for freshwater demand, energy consumption, chemical inputs, sorbent manufacture, waste generation, lithium selectivity, recovery rates, and management of spent brine. The authors emphasized that many methods still needed testing with real, chemically complex brines and that strong performance on one indicator does not guarantee strong overall performance ([Vera et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-022-00387-5)).

Reinjection also creates physical questions. Operators must show that returned fluid can be injected at the intended depth and pressure without clogging formations, mobilizing contaminants, mixing inappropriately with fresher water, or simply displacing brine toward another part of the system. If water or chemicals are added during lithium removal, the volume and composition returned may differ from what was extracted. Site-specific pilots and transparent monitoring are therefore more informative than generic claims that DLE “saves water.”

Chile’s national strategy proposes investigating new extraction technologies and strengthening environmental protection, but those are policy commitments, not evidence that any particular future installation will meet them. The government has also presented state participation, research capacity, protected salt flats, public–private projects, and stakeholder dialogue as core elements of its strategy ([Government of Chile, National Lithium Strategy](https://www.gob.cl/litioporchile/en/)).

## 4. Where the economic benefits go

Lithium’s economic benefits travel through several channels: wages, procurement, private profits, taxes, royalties or lease payments, state-company dividends, infrastructure, and community agreements. These channels distribute value differently.

Chile’s position is unusual because lithium has a special legal status and the state, through the development agency CORFO, owns important mining properties in the Salar de Atacama. The 2018 restated agreement with SQM authorized production under leases extending through 2030 and raised the contractual production ceiling to 349,553 metric tonnes of metallic-lithium equivalent ([CORFO’s description of the Salar de Atacama contract](https://www.corfo.cl/sites/Satellite?blobcol=urldata&blobkey=id&blobtable=MungoBlobs&blobwhere=1475168300448&ssbinary=true)). Lease payments and state control over access allow Chile to capture value in ways that differ from a simple low-rate royalty.

High prices can generate extraordinary public revenue, but revenue is volatile. Chile’s lithium exports rose from an annual average equivalent to 0.3% of gross domestic product in 2019–2021 to 2.7% in 2022, during an exceptional price boom ([International Monetary Fund, 2024](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/CR/2024/English/1CHLEA2024001.ashx)). That sharp change illustrates both the opportunity and the danger: governments may receive large windfalls, but budgets, local expectations, and investment plans become exposed to global commodity cycles. Lithium spot prices then fell by about 75% during 2023, even as lithium demand increased by roughly 30% ([IEA market review, 2024](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/market-review)).

Chile’s planned post-2030 partnership between state-owned Codelco and SQM is intended to preserve state lease revenue, regional and community contributions, research funding, and preferential-price supplies for Chilean value-added producers. The agreement discussed possible production of roughly 280,000–300,000 tonnes of lithium carbonate equivalent annually, subject to environmental approvals ([CORFO, 2024](https://www.corfo.cl/sites/cpp/sala_de_prensa/nacional/28_12_2024_acuerdo_litio)). Because these are forward-looking contractual and policy statements, they should not be confused with delivered production, proven environmental performance, or realized community benefits.

Argentina illustrates another model. Provinces exercise crucial authority over mineral projects. Argentina’s mining-investment law historically capped participating provinces’ royalties at 3% of “mine-mouth” value; a 2024 amendment permits up to 5% for projects that had not begun construction before the amendment took effect. The taxable value can be calculated after specified transport, treatment, commercialization, and refining deductions ([Argentina, Law 24,196, Articles 22 and 22 bis](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/594/actualizacion)). A nominal royalty rate therefore does not tell readers the share of final battery value retained locally.

Capturing more value can involve public equity, progressive rent taxation, local procurement, worker training, and domestic processing. Yet downstream industrialization is not automatically profitable or environmentally preferable. Refining and battery manufacturing require reliable energy, chemicals, infrastructure, specialized labor, markets, and quality control. Subsidizing a plant can create capabilities and jobs, but it can also transfer public money to firms that leave when incentives expire. The relevant test is not whether a policy is called “value addition,” but who owns the assets, who receives durable skills and income, what public support costs, and how commercial risk is divided.

## 5. Jobs: real benefits, limited scale, uneven access

Mining employment can substantially improve household income in remote regions, particularly where formal jobs are scarce. But brine operations are capital-intensive and relatively automated. Construction produces a temporary employment surge; steady operation generally requires fewer workers. Specialized technical and managerial positions may be filled by commuters or migrants if local education and training have not been developed in advance.

A 2024 social life-cycle study of SQM’s operations reported 268 local hires and 1,104 non-local employees in December 2021, meaning local hires accounted for 19.5% of the reported workforce. It also documented apprenticeship and recruitment programs intended to connect nearby Indigenous communities with vacancies ([Roche et al., 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11367-024-02378-8)). This is useful evidence that jobs and training are tangible benefits, but it also shows that most positions in the reported workforce were not locally filled.

The same study illustrates why methodological scrutiny matters. Researchers interviewed 13 people from seven Indigenous communities in an exploratory round and later 17 people from eight communities and ayllus, together with local officials and a rural drinking-water committee. SQM supplied company data and introduced the researchers to interview contacts. The authors acknowledged that this recruitment method could create selection bias and that not every community’s voice was represented ([Roche et al., methods and limitations](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11367-024-02378-8)). The evidence is therefore a detailed case study, not a representative survey of all Atacameño people.

Job counts also conceal job quality. Questions include whether workers are permanent or subcontracted, whether shifts allow community life, how wages compare with local living costs, whether women and Indigenous residents reach skilled roles, and who receives occupational-health protection. An operation can meet a local-hire target while reserving senior positions and procurement profits for firms headquartered elsewhere.

Mining can additionally raise housing costs, traffic, and pressure on services. Local businesses may gain customers, but pastoralism, tourism, agriculture, and unpaid subsistence work may face water or land constraints. These effects are not commensurable through wages alone. A household that gains one mining salary may value the project differently from a household whose livelihood depends on a wetland, spring, or landscape used for tourism and cultural practice.

## 6. Decision-making power and Indigenous rights

Distribution concerns authority as well as money. Indigenous peoples around Andean salars do not relate to land and water only as market inputs. Territory can support identity, kinship, spirituality, food systems, and obligations to future generations. Compensation cannot be presumed to replace these relationships.

International Labour Organization Convention No. 169 requires governments to consult Indigenous peoples through appropriate procedures and representative institutions whenever contemplated legislative or administrative measures may affect them directly. It also requires means for participation at all levels of decision-making concerning policies and programs that affect them ([ILO Convention No. 169, Article 6](https://wwwex.ilo.org/dyn/f?p=1000%3A12100%3A%3A%3A%3A12100%3AP12100_INSTRUMENT_ID%3A312314)). Consultation is therefore not equivalent to a company information meeting or an invitation to comment after the central project design has been fixed.

Chile has conducted or announced Indigenous consultation processes for new special lithium-operation contracts, including processes associated with the Ascotán, Ollagüe, and Coipasa salt systems ([Chile Ministry of Mining, 2024](https://www.minmineria.gob.cl/?noticia=ministerio-de-mineria-inicia-tres-nuevos-procesos-de-consulta-indigena-para-la-asignacion-de-contratos-especiales-de-operacion-de-litio)). The existence of a process establishes that consultation was formally initiated; it does not by itself establish that affected peoples had equal technical resources, adequate time, influence over alternatives, or power to refuse a particular outcome.

Power is affected by who controls hydrogeological data and modeling. Companies often collect the most detailed well, pumping, and chemistry records, while communities and local governments may lack independent experts. Confidentiality can prevent outside researchers from testing company conclusions. A more balanced system would make baseline data, monitoring methods, model assumptions, compliance records, and machine-readable time series publicly accessible, while protecting legitimately sensitive cultural knowledge.

Community-benefit agreements can redirect revenue locally, but they introduce difficult questions: Who represents the community? How are funds allocated among settlements and generations? Does accepting money weaken the ability to challenge permits? Are payments conditional on production or silence? No single answer applies everywhere. The important principle is that revenue-sharing must supplement, rather than purchase away, environmental protections and participation rights.

## 7. A fairer framework for allocating water, value, and risk

A defensible lithium policy would evaluate at least six linked dimensions.

First, establish basin-wide water limits before assigning project volumes. Monitoring should distinguish freshwater, transitional water, and brine; measure levels, flows, chemistry, land deformation, and ecosystem indicators; and include non-mining users and climate trends. Thresholds should trigger predetermined reductions or pauses rather than another negotiation after damage appears.

Second, treat uncertainty as a management condition. Permits can use staged expansion: pilot production, evaluation against published thresholds, and authorization of larger volumes only if evidence supports it. Financial assurance should cover monitoring, closure, remediation, and post-closure care even if an operator fails.

Third, make distribution visible. Governments should publish project-level taxes, royalties, lease payments, subsidies, infrastructure obligations, procurement, workforce composition, and community payments. Reporting gross export value without public costs or deductible expenses overstates social benefit.

Fourth, strengthen local capabilities before production. Training tied to portable qualifications is more durable than narrow instruction for one employer. Procurement programs should address financing and technical barriers faced by local businesses. Health, housing, education, and water infrastructure should not depend entirely on discretionary company philanthropy.

Fifth, give affected peoples influence over alternatives, not merely mitigation. Meaningful participation begins while authorities are still considering whether, where, how fast, and with which technology extraction should occur. Communities require independent technical and legal support funded through arrangements that do not place consultants under company control.

Sixth, reduce pressure at the demand end. Smaller batteries, longer vehicle life, repair, public transportation, efficient freight systems, and recycling can lower the amount of new extraction required for a given mobility or energy service. Recycling is not an immediate substitute for all mining because rapidly growing battery fleets contain material that will remain in use for years. Under the IEA’s Announced Pledges Scenario, recovered battery materials could nevertheless provide roughly 20%–30% of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by 2050 if collection rates continue improving; lower collection produces lower contributions ([IEA, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*](https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary)).

## Conclusion

Lithium extraction distributes benefits and burdens across radically different scales. Battery users and national governments may receive climate, energy-security, export, and fiscal benefits. Mining companies and investors can receive profits. Some workers and local businesses gain wages, contracts, and training. Meanwhile, water-system risks, landscape change, social disruption, and uncertainty are concentrated around extraction and processing sites.

The evidence does not support a single universal water number for “lithium.” Hard-rock mining, conventional evaporation, and DLE have different material requirements, while every brine basin has distinct geology. Scarcity-weighted footprints are not physical withdrawal figures, and freshwater cannot simply be equated with brine. Nevertheless, conventional brine production intentionally removes and evaporates subsurface fluid in arid environments, and uncertainties about connected aquifers and ecosystems are consequential rather than trivial.

Nor does national revenue automatically become local development. Contracts, tax bases, public ownership, commodity prices, procurement rules, workforce access, and community agreements determine where value settles. Jobs can transform household opportunities, yet operational employment may be modest and predominantly non-local. Consultation can satisfy a procedural calendar without equalizing control over information or project design.

The central lesson is that a “green” end use does not settle the justice of its supply chain. A fair energy transition requires both decarbonization and institutions capable of limiting extraction, sharing value, protecting rights, and acting before uncertain harm becomes irreversible. The key question is not only how much lithium the world can produce, but what energy and mobility services societies actually need—and under whose authority the associated costs may be imposed.
