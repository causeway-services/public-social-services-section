# Carbon Removal: Who Bears the Costs, Who Decides?

## Introduction

Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) is not a single technology. It is a family of practices that take carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and store it in vegetation, soils, products, geological formations, or the ocean. Examples include reforestation, soil-carbon management, biochar, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS), enhanced rock weathering, and several proposed marine interventions. These methods differ sharply in maturity, cost, resource use, durability, and governability. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change therefore cautions that implementation must account for differences in removal processes, storage times, risks, co-benefits, and governance requirements—not merely the number of tonnes nominally removed ([IPCC, Chapter 12](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-12/)).

The physical benefit of removing a tonne of atmospheric CO₂ is geographically diffuse. Once atmospheric concentrations fall, however slightly, the climate benefit is shared globally. The project’s burdens are usually concentrated: land conversion occurs in a particular landscape; a pipeline crosses particular properties; mineral dust reaches particular workers and neighbors; electricity and water come from particular systems; and stored carbon requires someone to monitor a particular forest, field, reservoir, or seabed.

This creates the central political problem of CDR. A strategy may look beneficial in a global carbon model while appearing to a host community as an industrial facility, a land claim, an uncertain liability, or a competitor for water and electricity. Conversely, a project that removes relatively little carbon might still produce locally valuable outcomes—healthier soils, restored wetlands, coastal protection, employment, or community revenue. Carbon efficiency, local welfare, and justice are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A defensible assessment must therefore ask four questions together:

1. How much additional CO₂ is removed after all associated emissions are counted?
2. How long and how securely is it stored?
3. Who receives income, assets, public-health improvements, or climate protection?
4. Who controls siting, design, monitoring, benefit sharing, and decisions about closure or failure?

## Why removal is considered—and what it cannot substitute for

The IPCC identifies three complementary functions for CDR: reducing net emissions in the near term, counterbalancing hard-to-abate residual emissions while reaching net zero, and producing net-negative emissions when removals exceed remaining emissions. It simultaneously states that these functions operate **in addition to deep, rapid, and sustained emissions reductions** ([IPCC Working Group III Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/summary-for-policymakers/)). CDR is thus not equivalent to avoiding an emission. Avoidance prevents new carbon from entering the atmosphere; removal attempts to retrieve carbon after it is already there.

A simplified carbon ledger for a removal project is

\[
R_{\text{net}}
=
R_{\text{gross}}
-
E_{\text{energy}}
-
E_{\text{materials}}
-
E_{\text{transport}}
-
E_{\text{land change}}
-
L,
\]

where \(R_{\text{gross}}\) is measured atmospheric CO₂ uptake, the \(E\) terms are supply-chain and operational emissions, and \(L\) represents leakage or displaced emissions outside the project boundary. A credible claim also needs an agreed time horizon and treatment of reversal risk. A tonne stored in a forest vulnerable to fire for several decades is not physically identical to a tonne mineralized into stable carbonate or retained in a well-managed geological reservoir.

“Additionality” asks whether the removal would have happened without the payment or policy. “Leakage” asks whether an intervention merely moves emissions elsewhere—for example, protecting one forest while timber production shifts to another. “Durability” describes how long carbon remains isolated. “Measurement, reporting, and verification” concerns how uptake, lifecycle emissions, and reversals are quantified and audited. The U.S. Department of Energy’s own definition of high-quality CDR requires net-negative lifecycle emissions, additionality, unique quantification, third-party validation, and isolation for at least 100 years through technical, financial, or substitution mechanisms ([DOE Carbon Dioxide Removal Purchase Challenge](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/cdr-challenge)). That definition is a program standard, not proof that every credited project satisfies it.

These accounting questions are distributive questions as well. A loose baseline may create saleable credits for a developer without producing the claimed public benefit. An overly narrow project boundary can hide emissions from electricity, fertilizer, mining, or transport in other communities. A short crediting contract may transfer long-lived reversal risks to landholders or future governments after investors have been paid.

## Biological land-based removal

### Forests, agroforestry, and ecosystem restoration

Trees remove CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in biomass and soils. Reforestation restores forest on recently deforested land; afforestation establishes forest where it has not existed for a longer period. Agroforestry integrates woody plants with crops or livestock, while improved forest management may change harvesting, rotation, or conservation practices.

These approaches are relatively mature and can deliver more than carbon. Depending on location and design, restoration may improve habitat, reduce erosion, regulate water, provide forest products, or support recreation and cultural practices. Yet “planting trees” does not specify who owns the land, which species are planted, what previous land use is displaced, or who retains access.

The IPCC estimates a very broad 2050 technical potential of approximately 0.5–10.1 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent per year for afforestation and reforestation and 1–2.1 GtCO₂-e per year for improved forest management. It warns that technical potential is reduced by economic, environmental, institutional, and sociocultural constraints ([IPCC, Chapter 12](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-12/)). These wide intervals are scenario ranges, not forecasts of what will actually be delivered.

Environmental costs arise when carbon plantations replace biodiverse ecosystems, reduce catchment water yield, lower groundwater, or compete with food production. Carbon may later return to the atmosphere through harvest, wildfire, pests, drought, or political changes. The IPCC assesses vegetation and soil storage as more reversible than geological storage or carbon held in biochar ([IPCC Working Group III Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/summary-for-policymakers/)).

The distributional outcome depends heavily on tenure. A landowner who can document title, finance planting, and navigate a carbon registry is better positioned to receive credit revenue than a tenant farmer, customary user, pastoralist, or Indigenous community whose rights are weakly recognized. If a carbon contract restricts grazing, gathering, or cultivation, local people can lose material and cultural benefits even while a distant purchaser claims progress toward a climate target. Conversely, community ownership, secure tenure, locally chosen species, and revenue-sharing rules can make restoration a source of durable assets rather than dispossession.

Decision-making should therefore begin before a site and project design are fixed. Consultation after a developer has acquired land or signed a buyer is not equivalent to shared authority. Particularly where Indigenous rights are involved, governance must distinguish ordinary stakeholder consultation from the authority of rightsholders whose territorial rights do not arise from the project.

### Agricultural soil carbon and biochar

Soil-carbon practices include cover crops, reduced disturbance, altered grazing, organic amendments, and changes in crop rotation. Biochar is carbon-rich material produced by heating biomass under limited oxygen and then commonly added to soil. Both can be integrated into working landscapes, potentially allowing farmers to receive carbon revenue while maintaining production.

Their attraction lies partly in possible local co-benefits, but outcomes vary with soil, climate, feedstock, management, and measurement. Soil carbon commonly accumulates only until a new equilibrium is reached and can be lost if management changes. Measuring small stock changes against large, spatially variable background carbon pools is difficult. Crediting programs must decide who pays for sampling, who owns the data, and who bears liability if credited carbon is later reversed.

The IPCC reports wide cost and potential ranges: soil-carbon sequestration estimates include costs from roughly negative USD 45 to positive USD 100 per tonne of CO₂, while biochar estimates span approximately USD 10–345 per tonne. Negative cost means that non-carbon benefits might exceed direct costs under some assumptions; it does not mean universal profit or unlimited deployment ([IPCC, Chapter 12](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-12/)).

Benefits can flow to farmers through payments, improved soil properties, or reduced input costs. They can instead concentrate among registries, aggregators, consultants, technology vendors, and large landowners able to spread verification costs across extensive acreage. Small producers may face long contracts, data requirements, uncertain prices, or rules that reward only new practices while offering nothing to farmers who adopted good stewardship earlier. Interviews with agricultural actors in northern Germany, for example, found concern that farmers who had already accumulated soil carbon could be disadvantaged relative to those changing practices only after incentives appeared ([Frontiers study of regional agricultural actors](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1627432/abstract)). This is evidence about stakeholder concerns in one region, not a universal finding about all farmers.

Biochar adds questions about biomass sourcing. Residues can have existing uses, and dedicated feedstock production can claim land and water. Pyrolysis facilities may create rural employment and useful heat, but they also concentrate truck traffic, combustion emissions, and occupational risks near a facility. A fair comparison must evaluate the whole biomass system rather than treating feedstock as impact-free waste.

## BECCS: removal tied to energy and land systems

BECCS grows biomass, converts it into electricity, fuels, or other energy products, captures the resulting CO₂, and stores it. Because plants absorb atmospheric carbon while growing, the system can in principle produce energy and net removal. Whether it actually does so depends on land-use change, fertilizer, harvesting, processing, transport, capture rate, energy penalties, and storage integrity.

BECCS illustrates how one project can distribute benefits and burdens across several regions. Farmers or forestry companies receive feedstock income; conversion facilities receive energy revenue or subsidies; storage operators earn fees; and governments or credit buyers fund the carbon value. Meanwhile, land competition and water use occur where biomass is grown, air emissions and truck traffic occur near the conversion plant, and pipeline and storage risks fall along another corridor.

The IPCC gives BECCS a technical potential range of about 0.5–11.3 GtCO₂-e per year by 2050, but it also finds that biomass production can compete for land, water, biodiversity conservation, livelihoods, and food security. Reported cost estimates vary substantially with system design and assumptions ([IPCC, Chapter 12](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-12/)). The upper potential should not be read as a socially acceptable deployment level.

Economic benefits may be meaningful in agricultural or former industrial regions, especially where projects create unionized employment and stable tax revenue. But benefits are not automatic. Mechanization can limit job numbers, profits may leave the region, and reliance on a subsidized facility can make a municipality vulnerable to policy or credit-market changes. Contracts that guarantee local procurement, training, wage standards, tax payments, and decommissioning funds are more enforceable than general promises of “green jobs.”

BECCS also raises mitigation-deterrence concerns. If an emitter buys removal instead of reducing avoidable pollution, the atmosphere may receive a formally balanced carbon account while the emitting community continues to experience particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, toxic releases, noise, or traffic. CO₂ removal is not a general air-pollution control technology. Climate accounting and local public-health regulation must therefore remain separate rather than assuming that a carbon-negative label resolves co-pollutant exposure.

## Direct air capture and geological storage

DAC uses chemical sorbents or solvents to separate dilute CO₂ directly from ambient air. The captured gas is regenerated, compressed, transported, and then stored or used. When powered by sufficiently low-carbon energy and paired with durable storage, DACCS offers measurable removal with a relatively compact land footprint compared with extensive biomass cultivation. Its principal constraints include cost, energy, materials, infrastructure, and the availability and governance of storage.

The United States has adopted unusually large public supports for this pathway. The Department of Energy’s Regional DAC Hubs program is intended to develop four hubs, each with the potential to capture at least one million metric tonnes of CO₂ annually; a 2022 funding opportunity announced USD 3.5 billion for the program ([DOE Regional Direct Air Capture Hubs](https://www.energy.gov/oced/DACHubs)). U.S. tax policy has also offered enhanced per-tonne incentives for qualifying capture and storage, meaning taxpayers can bear a significant share of project revenue risk while private firms may own the facilities and intellectual property.

DAC’s electricity and heat demand links it to local energy systems. If a facility draws from a constrained grid, it may compete with households and industry, require new generation and transmission, or affect rates. If supplied by fossil energy, upstream and combustion emissions reduce net removal and can add local pollution. Dedicated renewable generation reduces some of these conflicts but still requires land, materials, interconnection, and decisions about whether scarce clean electricity would produce greater climate benefit by displacing fossil generation elsewhere.

Empirical research shows why technical design cannot be separated from local experience. A U.S. study combined focus groups in Houston, Monaca, Bakersfield, and Rock Springs with a nationally representative survey. Participants’ concerns included pipeline and storage safety, energy costs, local infrastructure strain, job quality, corporate accountability, and the possibility that DAC could prolong polluting industries. Community participation and job creation increased support, while many participants preferred some degree of community ownership or oversight ([Communications Earth & Environment study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01334-6)).

Those findings should be interpreted carefully. The focus-group locations were selected because they were near potential storage and had histories with carbon-intensive industry; they do not represent every possible host community. Survey support for a hypothetical project may also differ from behavior during permitting or construction. The study itself notes that only 61% of focus-group participants had previously heard of DAC and that stated support before development may not predict support afterward ([Communications Earth & Environment study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01334-6)).

### Transport, storage, and long-term liability

Captured CO₂ must reach a storage site, commonly by pipeline. Dense-phase CO₂ behaves differently from ordinary air: a large release can displace oxygen near the ground, while water in a pipeline can contribute to corrosion. Storage introduces questions about pore space ownership, pressure migration, old wells, induced seismicity, groundwater protection, monitoring, and responsibility after injection ends.

In the United States, Class VI wells inject CO₂ into deep formations for long-term storage. EPA rules require site characterization, plume and pressure-front modeling, corrosion-resistant construction, monitoring, emergency planning, financial responsibility, plugging, and post-injection care. The rules focus specifically on protecting underground sources of drinking water; they do not by themselves regulate every environmental or social effect of a capture, pipeline, and storage network ([EPA Class VI overview](https://www.epa.gov/uic/class-vi-wells-used-geologic-sequestration-carbon-dioxide)).

Decision authority can reside either with EPA or with a state, territory, or tribe that receives federal approval for “primacy.” Each well requires a permit through site closure, and draft EPA permits receive at least a 30-day public-comment period ([EPA Class VI overview](https://www.epa.gov/uic/class-vi-wells-used-geologic-sequestration-carbon-dioxide)). A comment opportunity is procedurally important, but it is not community consent or a local veto. It also presumes that residents have time, technical assistance, and access to the modeling needed to challenge a proposal.

Financial responsibility rules reduce—but cannot abolish—the risk of stranded liability. The central question is whether security remains sufficient if an operator restructures, fails, or transfers a project. A robust regime would specify who monitors after closure, who compensates people harmed by leakage or a pipeline accident, who pays if remediation exceeds the operator’s bond, and whether the public ultimately assumes indefinite stewardship.

## Enhanced weathering and mineralization

Enhanced rock weathering spreads finely ground silicate minerals on soils or in other environments to accelerate reactions that consume CO₂. Mineralization can also react CO₂ with mine tailings, industrial alkaline wastes, concrete, or geological materials to form solid carbonates. Stable mineral storage is attractive because reversal risk can be low once reactions are complete.

The costs are shifted upstream and downstream. Mining, crushing, and transport consume energy and create dust, noise, traffic, habitat disruption, and waste. Farmers and rural workers may handle unfamiliar materials. Trace metals or other constituents can create soil and water concerns, depending on mineral composition and application rate. The National Academies has warned that exposing minerals at removal-relevant scale could create enormous quantities of waste rock, potentially containing substances that contaminate air or water ([National Academies, *Negative Emissions Technologies and Reliable Sequestration* highlights](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/25259/Negative%20Emissions%20Technologies.pdf)).

Possible benefits include mining and transport employment, payments to participating farmers, and—in some soil contexts—changes in nutrient supply or acidity. Yet the party selling a verified removal may be far removed from the mine or farm bearing the operational risks. Governance therefore requires feedstock testing, dust and occupational standards, transparent field trials, soil and water monitoring, and a clear rule for assigning liability if contaminants accumulate.

Measurement remains challenging because researchers must distinguish accelerated uptake from natural weathering, track carbon transported through soils and waters, and determine whether it remains stored. A project can measure tonnes of rock applied much more easily than tonnes of atmospheric CO₂ durably removed. Payment should follow verified net removal, not merely material throughput.

## Marine carbon removal and coastal communities

Marine approaches include ocean alkalinity enhancement, electrochemical processing, nutrient fertilization, macroalgal cultivation and sinking, artificial upwelling or downwelling, and ecosystem restoration. The ocean’s scale makes these proposals appear physically promising, but it also makes ecological effects, carbon attribution, transboundary movement, and enforcement difficult.

Ocean alkalinity enhancement aims to change seawater chemistry so that the ocean absorbs more atmospheric CO₂ and stores more carbon in dissolved forms. Potential burdens include mining and transporting alkaline materials, trace-element contamination, changes in chemical cycling, and uncertain biological effects ([NOAA draft CDR research strategy](https://sciencecouncil.noaa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Clean-copy-of-Draft-CDR-Research-Strategy.pdf)). Macroalgal cultivation may capture carbon and support aquaculture, but durable removal depends on the fate of harvested or sunk biomass. Decomposition at depth can consume oxygen or redistribute nutrients, and the credited atmospheric effect may be difficult to observe directly.

NOAA describes major unknowns in marine CDR scalability, effectiveness, cost, and social and ecological impacts ([NOAA Ocean Acidification Program](https://oceanacidification.noaa.gov/carbon-dioxide-removal/)). The National Academies similarly concludes that present knowledge remains inadequate given the interactions among ocean physics, chemistry, biology, and society, and recommends research grounded in climate justice and precaution ([National Academies ocean CDR report](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/26278/Ocean_CDR_2021.pdf)). These are recommendations to investigate, not findings that large-scale deployment is safe.

Marine interventions make the mismatch between beneficiaries and affected communities especially visible. Atmospheric benefits are global, while fishers, coastal Indigenous peoples, tourism workers, and port communities may face localized ecological or access changes. Ocean currents can carry effects beyond the permitting jurisdiction. A government may possess formal legal authority while lacking the ecological knowledge or moral legitimacy to decide alone for communities with customary marine tenure.

Early governance should therefore include public research protocols, independent ecological baselines, disclosure of commercial interests, staged trials, stopping rules, and participation by affected rightsholders before deployment pathways become economically entrenched. “Social license” is not a one-time public-relations achievement; the National Academies describes it as dynamic and potentially revocable ([National Academies marine CDR glossary](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/pubs/29342/glossary.html)).

## What the evidence says about fair decision-making

The strongest recent experimental evidence suggests that fairness is not a secondary preference that can be exchanged for a larger carbon claim. A 2026 study surveyed 10,852 people in Brazil, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, Italy, Norway, and the United Kingdom. Respondents evaluated hypothetical DACCS, BECCS, or enhanced-weathering projects that varied by implementing actor, consultation, profit distribution, and removal capacity. Consultation increased support by roughly 1.0–1.7 points on a ten-point scale, while direct local revenue sharing increased it by about 0.50–1.25 points. Greater removal capacity also mattered, but did not compensate for unfair decision-making or profit allocation ([Nature Climate Change study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02741-7)).

This is strong evidence about stated preferences under randomized scenarios, not proof that a particular governance model will prevent harm. “Consultation” in an experiment is less complex than real participation, and cultural meanings differ across countries. The authors explicitly caution against assuming that consultation is understood identically in each political context ([Nature Climate Change study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02741-7)).

Still, the result supports a practical distinction:

- **Information** tells residents what a developer plans.
- **Consultation** asks for views but leaves final control elsewhere.
- **Participation** gives community representatives an ongoing role in design and oversight.
- **Shared authority** establishes enforceable consent, co-ownership, vetoes over specified changes, or jointly governed funds.
- **Restorative governance** also addresses prior pollution, dispossession, or exclusion rather than treating the new project as historically isolated.

Benefit agreements can specify local hiring, apprenticeships, wages, procurement, utility protections, emergency resources, tax payments, monitoring access, and funds controlled by residents. The Department of Energy has required DAC-hub applicants to propose ways to maximize benefits, minimize harms, measure impacts in disadvantaged communities, and create accountability and feedback mechanisms ([DOE Regional DAC Hubs funding guidance](https://oced-exchange.energy.gov/FileContent.aspx?FileID=2adfbdec-4409-439c-b2be-fedadba3149f)). Whether these plans redistribute actual power depends on enforceability, transparency, community capacity, and consequences for noncompliance.

## A framework for comparing projects

No single ranking of CDR methods is defensible everywhere. A more useful assessment uses several ledgers at once:

| Dimension | Questions for evaluation |
|---|---|
| Climate integrity | Is removal additional, net-negative over its lifecycle, measurable, and matched to an appropriate storage duration? |
| Environmental burden | What land, water, energy, biomass, minerals, transport, and waste are required, and where are their impacts located? |
| Public health | Which routine emissions, occupational hazards, accident risks, or co-pollutant changes occur? |
| Economic distribution | Who receives subsidies, credits, profits, wages, tax revenue, infrastructure, and ownership? Who faces higher prices or stranded liabilities? |
| Procedural power | Who sets baselines, selects sites, reviews data, issues permits, enforces standards, and decides whether operations expand or stop? |
| Recognition and rights | Are Indigenous rights, customary tenure, cultural relationships, prior harms, and cumulative pollution treated as governing facts? |
| Intergenerational responsibility | Who monitors stored carbon, replaces failed infrastructure, and pays for reversals decades later? |
| Alternatives | Could the same money, land, or clean energy avoid more emissions or produce greater health benefits elsewhere? |

This framework prevents a low dollar-per-tonne estimate from ending the discussion. Market cost is not social cost: it can omit public subsidies, unpaid care, ecological loss, accident risk, weak land rights, and future monitoring. Nor does a high-cost method necessarily lack value if it offers verifiable, durable removal with limited land conflict. The comparison must make assumptions visible.

## Conclusion

Different removal strategies distribute power and cost through the systems they inhabit. Forest and soil projects place tenure, rural livelihoods, ecological management, and reversal risk at the center. BECCS links carbon accounting to agriculture, forestry, energy, pipelines, and storage. DACCS concentrates finance and technical infrastructure while drawing heavily on energy systems and creating transport and long-term liability questions. Enhanced weathering shifts attention to mining, material handling, and difficult measurement. Marine CDR extends uncertainty and authority across mobile ecosystems and jurisdictional boundaries.

Several findings are comparatively well established. CDR has a complementary role in climate mitigation but does not replace rapid emissions reductions. Methods differ materially in durability and resource demand. Lifecycle accounting, additionality, and monitoring are essential. Local burdens can be much more concentrated than global climate benefits. Empirical studies also show that consultation and benefit sharing affect public support.

Greater uncertainty surrounds deployment at large scale. Technical potentials are not predictions; costs can change with energy, finance, regulation, and learning; ecosystem effects may emerge only over time; and hypothetical public-support surveys cannot guarantee acceptance of real facilities. Marine interventions and some mineral pathways remain particularly research-dependent.

The most defensible policy is therefore neither blanket endorsement nor blanket rejection. It is a portfolio governed by strict carbon accounting, priority for emissions prevention, differentiated standards for storage durability, protection of land and resource rights, cumulative public-health assessment, enforceable local benefits, long-term financial responsibility, and meaningful authority for the people who live with the infrastructure. The decisive question is not simply whether carbon can be removed. It is whether removal can be accomplished without treating particular communities, ecosystems, or future generations as inexpensive storage space for benefits enjoyed elsewhere.
