# Waste Management: Who Bears the Costs, Who Decides?

## Introduction

Waste management is often presented as a technical choice among recycling, composting, incineration, and landfilling. That framing is incomplete. Every strategy also determines where pollution occurs, who pays for infrastructure, who receives revenue or employment, whose knowledge counts in decision-making, and who remains responsible when facilities close or materials cross jurisdictional borders.

The central distributional problem is that the benefits of products and convenient disposal are often geographically dispersed, while many burdens are concentrated. Consumers and producers may be far from the transfer stations, truck routes, sorting plants, incinerators, landfills, informal recovery sites, or contaminated waterways through which discarded materials pass. Conversely, a host community may receive jobs, tax revenue, heat, electricity, or disposal fees while bearing noise, traffic, odors, accident risks, emissions, and long-term liability.

No single ranking of waste strategies can resolve all these questions. The US Environmental Protection Agency’s hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling and composting, followed by energy recovery, treatment, and disposal. EPA explicitly cautions, however, that no one approach is suitable for every material and circumstance ([EPA, “Sustainable Materials Management”](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-materials-management-non-hazardous-materials-and-waste-management-hierarchy)). A careful assessment must therefore ask two related questions:

1. What are the total effects of a strategy across the material’s life cycle?
2. How are those effects, benefits, costs, risks, and decision rights distributed?

This distinction explains why a project can appear efficient in a municipal budget while remaining environmentally damaging or socially unjust.

## Prerequisites and definitions

**Municipal solid waste (MSW)** generally means ordinary discarded materials from households and institutions, plus comparable commercial waste. It does not automatically include industrial process waste, mining waste, sewage, or construction debris. Even official datasets use different boundaries, so comparisons require attention to what is counted. EPA’s national US figures, for example, cover municipal solid waste and report quantities in US short tons; EPA says its latest complete national series remains based on 2018 data ([EPA, “Facts and Figures about Materials, Waste and Recycling”](https://www.epa.gov/facts-and-figures-about-materials-waste-and-recycling)).

**Source reduction** prevents waste before it exists—for example, through durable product design, reuse systems, reduced packaging, repair, or lower toxicity. **Recycling** collects and processes discarded materials into feedstocks for new production. **Composting** biologically transforms separated organic materials into a soil amendment. **Anaerobic digestion** decomposes organics without oxygen, producing biogas and a residual digestate. **Incineration with energy recovery** burns waste and uses some of the released heat to produce electricity or useful heat. **Landfilling** isolates waste in an engineered disposal site, although organic material can continue decomposing and producing gas for decades.

**External costs** are harms not fully reflected in the price paid by the party generating or managing the waste. Examples include pollution-related illness, greenhouse-gas emissions, degraded amenities, road damage, and future remediation. External benefits can also occur, such as avoided extraction of virgin materials or broader climate benefits.

**Environmental justice** concerns both outcomes and procedures. Distributional justice asks who receives burdens and benefits. Procedural justice asks who has information, representation, veto power, negotiating leverage, and access to remedies. Recognition justice asks whether institutions respect affected groups’ histories, knowledge, identities, and existing cumulative burdens.

A useful conceptual accounting identity is:

\[
\text{Net social value}
=
B_{\text{materials}}
+
B_{\text{energy}}
+
B_{\text{service}}
-
C_{\text{financial}}
-
C_{\text{health}}
-
C_{\text{environment}}
-
C_{\text{future liability}}.
\]

This is not a claim that every term can be measured precisely. Rather, it prevents a narrow calculation—such as disposal fees minus electricity revenue—from being mislabeled as the project’s total value. Distribution can be represented separately:

\[
C_{\text{total}}=\sum_{g}\sum_{t}\frac{C_{g,t}}{(1+r)^t},
\]

where \(C_{g,t}\) is the cost borne by group \(g\) in year \(t\), and \(r\) is a chosen discount rate. Aggregation can conceal injustice: two projects with the same total discounted cost can be very different if one spreads minor costs widely while the other imposes serious risks on a small community with little political power. Discounting also gives less weight to distant harms, so the choice of \(r\) is ethical and political as well as technical.

## The scale of the system and the limits of averages

US data illustrate the material scale involved. EPA estimated that the United States generated 292.4 million short tons of municipal solid waste in 2018: 69.1 million tons were recycled, 24.9 million composted, 34.6 million combusted with energy recovery, and 146.1 million landfilled or otherwise disposed. EPA warns that the sharp increase in reported generation between 2017 and 2018 partly reflected a revised food-waste measurement method rather than a purely physical increase in discards ([EPA, *Advancing Sustainable Materials Management: 2018 Fact Sheet*](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2020-11/documents/2018_ff_fact_sheet.pdf)). This is an important evidentiary lesson: apparent trends can depend on accounting boundaries.

Waste management is also a major fiscal obligation. The World Bank’s global assessment found that solid-waste spending averaged 19% of municipal expenditure among the low-income-country cities in its sample, compared with 11% in middle-income and 4% in high-income cities. The calculation used one city from each of 46 countries and therefore should not be treated as a universal national average. The report also estimated that integrated operating costs generally exceeded US$100 per metric tonne in high-income countries, while lower-income countries spent around US$35 per tonne and often found cost recovery more difficult ([World Bank, *What a Waste 2.0*](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstream/handle/10986/30317/9781464813290.pdf)).

These figures show why local governments may favor methods with predictable contracts or immediate revenue even when their long-term social costs are uncertain. They also show why unequal public finance matters. A wealthy municipality may afford separated collection, enforcement, modern treatment, and long-term monitoring, while a poorer one may struggle to provide universal collection. Inadequate collection can itself create severe public-health hazards through open dumping, blocked drainage, uncontrolled burning, pests, and direct contact with waste. Justice therefore cannot mean merely opposing facilities; it must include reliable, safe service for communities that have historically lacked it.

## Source reduction and reuse: preventing costs upstream

Source reduction generally has the strongest environmental logic because it avoids collection, processing, and disposal as well as some upstream extraction and manufacturing. EPA identifies waste prevention as the most environmentally preferred strategy and includes redesign, reduced toxicity, reuse, remanufacturing, and reduced packaging among its forms ([EPA, “Sustainable Materials Management”](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-materials-management-non-hazardous-materials-and-waste-management-hierarchy)).

Its distributional advantage is that it can prevent local burdens rather than merely relocating them. A refillable package, durable appliance, repairable device, or reduction in food waste can mean fewer truck trips and lower demand for landfills or combustors. Prevention can also reduce municipal expenditure because material that never becomes waste requires no collection or treatment.

Yet the authority to prevent waste often lies upstream with manufacturers, retailers, and national regulators, while the obligation to manage it commonly falls on municipalities. Local residents can sort packaging but usually cannot redesign it. This mismatch distributes decision-making power away from those paying disposal costs.

Extended producer responsibility (EPR) attempts to correct the mismatch by making producers financially or operationally responsible for products after use. In principle, fees can internalize end-of-life costs and reward products that are durable, reusable, or easy to recycle. OECD guidance argues that producer fees should recover the full cost of end-of-life management and, where possible, vary with weight or design features that affect recyclability ([OECD, *Extended Producer Responsibility: Updated Guidance for Efficient Waste Management*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/09/extended-producer-responsibility_g1g6742c/9789264256385-en.pdf)).

Design details determine who actually benefits. The OECD’s case study of Japan’s packaging system found that municipalities paid about JPY 423.5 billion for sorted collection, additional sorting, and storage in 2003—about 3.4 times producer expenditure under the scheme. The report concluded that leaving these costs with municipalities weakened producers’ incentive to reduce packaging and recycling costs ([OECD, *Extended Producer Responsibility*, pp. 275–276](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/09/extended-producer-responsibility_g1g6742c/9789264256385-en.pdf)). Thus, an EPR label alone does not prove that responsibility has meaningfully shifted.

Prevention policies may also have regressive effects if reusable systems require deposits, transportation, storage space, or time that poorer households lack. These are design problems rather than arguments against prevention. Deposit refunds, convenient return networks, accessibility standards, repair rights, and protections against excessive consumer charges can determine whether the benefits are broadly shared.

## Recycling and composting: recovery, markets, and labor

Recycling can reduce demand for virgin resources and avoid some landfill or combustion capacity. EPA describes recycling as a chain comprising collection, sorting, processing, remanufacturing, and ultimately the purchase of recycled-content products ([EPA, “Sustainable Materials Management”](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-materials-management-non-hazardous-materials-and-waste-management-hierarchy)). This definition matters: collection alone is not recycling. A bale that is contaminated, stockpiled, exported without reliable oversight, or ultimately discarded has not necessarily displaced virgin production.

The environmental result depends on the material, contamination rate, transport distance, processing technology, and what recycled feedstock replaces. Metals can offer high recovery value; mixed or contaminated materials may be costly to sort and have weak markets. Recycling facilities also create local truck traffic, dust, noise, fire hazards, and occupational exposures. Consequently, “recyclable” is not an intrinsic guarantee of a good outcome.

Composting and anaerobic digestion are particularly relevant to organic waste. When food and other organics decompose anaerobically in landfills, they generate methane. EPA reports that municipal solid-waste landfills produced about 14.4% of US human-related methane emissions in 2022 and describes landfill gas as roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide by volume ([EPA, “Basic Information about Landfill Gas”](https://www.epa.gov/lmop/basic-information-about-landfill-gas)). Diverting clean organics can therefore reduce future methane formation and return nutrients or organic matter to soils.

These benefits depend on careful operation. Contaminated feedstocks can degrade compost; poorly controlled sites can create odors, bioaerosols, leachate, pests, and traffic. Anaerobic digestion still produces digestate that must be safely used or disposed, and leakage can erode the climate benefit of captured biogas. A fair system must not treat a neighborhood’s odor complaints as trivial merely because the facility serves a broader climate goal.

Labor is another major distributional issue. UNEP’s 2024 assessment reports that in regions where formal systems remain under development, more than eight in ten waste-management workers operate in the informal economy. It also warns that formalization can remove informal workers’ access to valuable materials and livelihoods unless they are deliberately included ([UNEP and International Solid Waste Association, *Global Waste Management Outlook 2024*](https://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/44939/global_waste_management_outlook_2024.pdf)). OECD guidance separately estimated about 20 million informal waste workers worldwide and described their work as frequently dangerous and socially precarious ([OECD, *Extended Producer Responsibility*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/09/extended-producer-responsibility_g1g6742c/9789264256385-en.pdf)).

Formal recycling may improve safety, wages, and environmental controls, but mechanization or exclusive contracts can also transfer income from waste pickers to large firms. A “green” transition can therefore be distributively harmful unless workers participate in its design and gain secure access, compensation, training, protective equipment, cooperative ownership opportunities, or formal employment.

## Incineration and energy recovery: concentrated risks and dispersed services

Incineration offers municipalities two tangible services: it greatly reduces the volume requiring final disposal and can produce electricity or heat. EPA estimates that after energy recovery, approximately 10% of the original waste volume remains as ash, which is generally landfilled ([EPA, “Sustainable Materials Management”](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-materials-management-non-hazardous-materials-and-waste-management-hierarchy)). Incineration therefore changes the waste and its location; it does not eliminate disposal.

Potential burdens include stack emissions, ash handling, truck traffic, occupational exposure, and the financial risk of capital-intensive infrastructure. Long-term supply contracts can create institutional pressure to deliver enough waste to repay debt or satisfy minimum-tonnage commitments. This can conflict with later prevention or recycling efforts, although the severity depends on the contract and local system.

Health evidence must be interpreted carefully. A National Research Council review found important uncertainties in estimating exposure and health effects from incinerators. Its qualitative concern categories considered emissions, persistence, environmental mobility, exposure routes, and toxicity, but the committee emphasized that these judgments were not direct proof that particular health effects were occurring. It also cautioned that facility type, operating conditions, pollution controls, and worker protections can materially alter risk ([National Research Council, *Waste Incineration and Public Health*, Chapter 5](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/5803/chapter/7)).

A recent Baltimore case study illustrates both potential magnitude and uncertainty. Researchers combined reported or estimated emissions, EPA’s AERMOD dispersion model, concentration-response functions, and monetized health outcomes for a municipal incinerator and a medical-waste incinerator. For the municipal facility, the model estimated annual health-related economic damages of US$53.8 million in 2024, with nitrogen oxides driving most modeled mortality damages. This is a model-based attribution, not a count of clinically identified individuals harmed by the facility ([Tu et al., “Health damages and disparities from municipal and medical waste incineration in Baltimore, USA”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236935/)).

For the medical-waste facility, sparse reported emissions required the researchers to construct controlled and uncontrolled scenarios. Estimated annual damages ranged from US$0.58 million to US$36.9 million at typical throughput and from US$10.51 million to US$107.11 million at maximum permitted throughput. Modeled concentrations were most intense within roughly one kilometre of the facility, and the authors identified missing stack parameters and unquantified black-smoke emissions as limitations ([Tu et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236935/)). The breadth of these ranges is not a reason to ignore possible harm; it shows why monitoring quality, operational compliance, and transparent assumptions are central to legitimate decision-making.

The geographic distribution is crucial. Electricity and disposal service may benefit an entire city, while the greatest exposure falls near the plant. Monetary valuation may show that health damages exceed facility revenue, but even a favorable aggregate balance would not answer whether the host community consented, received meaningful benefits, or was already burdened by other pollution sources.

## Landfills: lower immediate visibility, long-lived obligations

Engineered landfills can provide controlled disposal where open dumping would present greater hazards. Liners, leachate collection, daily cover, groundwater monitoring, gas collection, and financial assurance can reduce risk. Their effectiveness depends on design, operation, enforcement, geology, climate, waste composition, and continued maintenance.

Landfill burdens unfold over unusually long periods. Organic waste generates methane after burial; leachate must be managed; covers and gas systems can fail; and monitoring or remediation may be required after disposal revenue has ended. Methane capture can produce useful energy, but capture is never identical to prevention: gas forms before, during, and after collection infrastructure operates, and not all generated gas is captured.

Landfills may deliver host fees, employment, or tax revenue, but nearby communities can experience odor, litter, vermin, heavy-truck traffic, noise, stigma, and concern about water contamination. Property owners, renters, workers, and future residents may experience these effects differently. A negotiated payment to a municipal government does not necessarily compensate each exposed household or give residents meaningful power.

Because land is finite and facilities are politically unpopular, waste may travel from jurisdictions with greater purchasing power to communities willing—or pressured—to accept disposal contracts. This separates consumption from consequence. It also creates a fiscal dilemma: a host government can become dependent on tipping fees even when residents oppose expansion.

## Decision-making power and better evaluation

Waste decisions are distributed across manufacturers, consumers, municipal councils, regulators, contractors, facility owners, workers, neighboring jurisdictions, and courts. Formal authority does not guarantee effective power. A community may receive a legally required hearing only after technology, financing, and site selection are largely settled.

A defensible process should therefore evaluate at least five dimensions:

1. **Full life-cycle effects.** Include extraction and manufacturing avoided, collection, transport, processing, residual disposal, emissions, and long-term care.
2. **Spatial distribution.** Map truck routes, air dispersion, watersheds, odors, noise, and residual destinations rather than examining only the facility boundary.
3. **Social distribution.** Identify workers, nearby residents, ratepayers, taxpayers, producers, consumers, and future generations separately.
4. **Cumulative burden.** Assess the proposed facility alongside existing industry, traffic, housing conditions, occupational exposures, and vulnerability.
5. **Decision rights and accountability.** Disclose contracts, emissions, violations, ownership, financial assumptions, monitoring results, and emergency plans in accessible forms.

Governance reforms can redistribute power as well as money. Community advisory boards need independent expertise, timely data, and influence over permit conditions to be more than symbolic. Host-community agreements should specify enforceable benefits and monitoring rather than vague promises. Procurement should preserve public oversight even when service delivery is privatized. OECD guidance recommends transparent, competitive procurement for collection, sorting, recovery, and disposal and warns that concentrated producer-responsibility organizations can create competition and accountability problems ([OECD, *Extended Producer Responsibility*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/09/extended-producer-responsibility_g1g6742c/9789264256385-en.pdf)).

## Conclusions

Different waste strategies distribute costs and authority in systematically different ways.

Source reduction can avoid the largest range of downstream burdens, but producers often control product design while municipalities pay for disposal. Recycling and composting can conserve materials and reduce landfill demand, yet their benefits depend on real markets, clean inputs, safe operations, and fair treatment of workers. Incineration can provide energy and reduce volume, but it concentrates emissions and ash-related burdens near facilities while spreading disposal and energy benefits more widely. Landfills provide necessary controlled disposal but create methane, local nuisance, and long-term monitoring obligations that can outlast present budgets and political leadership.

The most important conclusion is not that one technology is always just and another always unjust. It is that technical performance, economic accounting, and political power cannot be separated. A system is not equitable merely because it diverts many tonnes from landfill, generates revenue, complies with average emission limits, or appears favorable in a citywide cost-benefit analysis. Equity depends on whether waste was prevented where feasible, whether residual risks were minimized, whether benefits and burdens were fairly distributed, whether workers and host communities had meaningful power, and whether institutions remain accountable for long-term consequences.

Important evidence gaps remain. National averages obscure facility-level conditions; proximity does not by itself establish exposure or causation; modeled health damages depend on emissions data and concentration-response assumptions; and informal work is frequently undercounted. These uncertainties strengthen the case for transparent monitoring and precaution in overburdened communities. They do not justify pretending that unpriced or poorly measured costs are zero.
