# The Cold Chain: Who Bears the Risk of Spoilage?

## Introduction

A cold chain is not merely a collection of refrigerators. It is a coordinated system for keeping temperature-sensitive products within suitable environmental conditions from harvest or slaughter through processing, storage, transport, wholesale, retail, and final use. Its physical components include precooling equipment, insulated rooms and vehicles, refrigeration systems, packaging, temperature sensors, loading facilities, and backup power. Its institutional components include standards, contracts, finance, maintenance, trained workers, records, inspections, and procedures for deciding whether an exposed shipment remains safe.

The system matters because perishability is both biological and economic. Fresh produce continues to respire after harvest; microorganisms can multiply in foods; moisture moves between products and surrounding air; and freezing, excessive cooling, dehydration, bruising, or exposure to ethylene can damage quality. Refrigeration slows some of these processes, but it does not reverse deterioration or repair mishandling. Nor is “colder” universally better: the appropriate conditions depend on the commodity. A U.S. Department of Agriculture transport handbook, for example, recommends approximately 0–2°C and 95–100% relative humidity for asparagus, but 13–14°C and 90–95% relative humidity for green bananas; the latter can suffer chilling injury at inappropriate low temperatures ([USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, *Protecting Perishable Foods During Transport by Truck*](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Tropical%20Products%20Transport%20Handbook.pdf)).

The central economic problem follows from this technical sensitivity. Cooling requires capital, energy, coordination, and information, while the value destroyed by a failure belongs to—or is imposed upon—someone. The party able to prevent a failure may not be the party that loses money when it occurs. A farmer may produce a sound crop but have no affordable access to precooling. A carrier may control the refrigerated vehicle but operate under a contract that limits liability. A retailer may reject a warm shipment to protect consumers and its brand, leaving the supplier with the loss. Consumers may ultimately encounter higher prices, poorer diets, or foodborne illness without ever seeing where the chain failed.

Cold-chain risk is therefore distributed through a combination of physics, ownership, contracts, bargaining power, public regulation, and access to infrastructure. Refrigeration can reduce losses and expand food availability, but its benefits and costs do not automatically flow to the same people.

## Prerequisites and definitions

“Food loss” and “food waste” are related but not interchangeable. The Food and Agriculture Organization’s Food Loss Index covers losses from harvest through supply-chain stages before retail; the corresponding Food Waste Index, administered by the United Nations Environment Programme, concerns retail and consumption. FAO also emphasizes that loss can involve deterioration in quality and economic value even when physical mass is not discarded ([FAO Technical Platform on Food Loss and Waste](https://www.fao.org/platform-food-loss-waste/food-loss/food-loss-measurement/en/)). This distinction is crucial for cold chains. A tomato may still be sold after bruising or warming, but only into a lower-priced market. That is an economic loss even if it remains food.

Several other distinctions help clarify the problem:

- **Safety** concerns whether food can cause illness. Temperature control may be necessary to suppress pathogen growth, but a thermometer reading alone does not always establish whether a particular shipment is safe.
- **Quality** includes appearance, texture, flavor, remaining shelf life, nutritional attributes, and conformance with a buyer’s grade.
- **Quantity loss** is a reduction in saleable mass or volume.
- **Shelf-life loss** means consuming part of the product’s remaining marketable time. It can transfer spoilage risk downstream even when no product is immediately discarded.
- **Temperature excursion** means departure from the required range for some duration. Its consequences depend on the product temperature—not only surrounding air—the excursion’s length, prior handling, packaging, humidity, and the relevant biological process.
- **Custody** identifies who physically holds the product. It need not coincide with ownership, control of refrigeration, or legal liability.

A cold chain can consequently fail without a dramatic equipment breakdown. Produce may wait in the sun before entering a functioning cold room; a refrigerated truck may be loaded with warm product it cannot cool rapidly; incompatible commodities may share a temperature setting; doors may be repeatedly opened; sensors may measure return air rather than the warmest part of the load; or the final retailer may lack reliable refrigeration. “Cold storage” at one location does not compensate for discontinuity elsewhere.

## Why time and temperature create economic value

Cooling preserves options. A product with only a few hours of commercial life must be sold quickly, usually to a nearby buyer. Extending that life can give its owner time to aggregate shipments, wait through a temporary glut, negotiate, reach a more distant market, or redirect a rejected load. This option value can be as important as the quantity physically saved.

A simple accounting model makes the distributional issue visible. Let:

- \(Q\) be the quantity harvested;
- \(P\) be the price per saleable unit;
- \(s\) be the share remaining saleable without an intervention;
- \(\Delta s\) be the improvement attributable to cooling;
- \(C\) be the cooling, handling, energy, and transport cost;
- \(r\) be the probability-adjusted cost of breakdown, rejection, or other residual failure; and
- \(B\) be any additional price premium or bargaining benefit enabled by quality preservation.

The private net benefit to the actor paying for cooling is approximately

\[
NB = P Q \Delta s + B - C - r.
\]

Investment is attractive to that actor when \(NB>0\). But the social benefit may also include safer food, additional nutrients reaching consumers, lower public-health risk, reduced pressure to expand production, and avoided environmental costs from producing food that is never eaten. Conversely, social cost includes electricity generation, refrigerant leakage, noise, congestion, and the disposal of rejected food. A project may be socially worthwhile even when no individual farmer can finance it; it may also be privately profitable while shifting environmental or health costs to the public.

The variables are not independent. Cooling can raise \(s\), but only if packaging, handling, roads, market demand, and subsequent refrigeration are adequate. It may increase \(B\) by making premium markets accessible, yet those markets can impose stricter grades and reject an entire consignment for defects that would be tolerated locally. The intervention can therefore reduce biological risk while increasing dependence on a powerful buyer.

## The technical chain and its failure points

### Harvest and precooling

Fresh horticultural products arrive from the field carrying “field heat.” Rapid removal of that heat slows respiration and other deterioration, but the appropriate technique—such as forced-air, hydro-, vacuum, or room cooling—depends on the commodity, packaging, water tolerance, available power, and throughput. Placing warm produce in a cold room is not equivalent to precooling: the refrigeration system may be designed to maintain already-cold goods rather than extract a large initial heat load.

Humidity matters as well. USDA’s commercial-storage guidance gives typical relative-humidity ranges of about 85–90% for many fruits and 90–95% for leafy vegetables and root crops, while noting commodity-specific exceptions. It also explains that insufficient evaporator-coil area or an excessive temperature difference between coil and commodity can promote dehydration ([USDA Agriculture Handbook 66](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-A-PURL-gpo18376/pdf/GOVPUB-A-PURL-gpo18376.pdf)). The farmer or packing-house operator thus faces a multidimensional task: remove heat rapidly, avoid excess water loss, prevent condensation and decay, and avoid chilling or freezing.

### Storage and transport

During transport, heat enters through vehicle walls, door openings, warm packaging, air leakage, solar exposure, and product respiration. Air must circulate around the load; blocked channels or overly dense stacking can create warm regions even when a vehicle’s displayed air temperature appears acceptable. Reliability therefore depends on vehicle design, loading practice, refrigeration capacity, maintenance, fuel or electricity, and the accuracy and placement of sensors.

U.S. regulation illustrates how technical duties become legal responsibilities. The Food and Drug Administration’s sanitary-transportation rule requires covered shippers, loaders, motor or rail carriers, and receivers to use sanitary practices. Vehicles must be capable of maintaining necessary temperatures, while operations must address temperature control, cross-contamination, training, and records ([FDA, FSMA Final Rule on Sanitary Transportation](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/fsma-final-rule-sanitary-transportation-human-and-animal-food)). The rule places primary responsibility for determining appropriate transportation operations on the shipper, while permitting responsibilities to be reassigned through written agreements; when a possible temperature-control failure could make food unsafe, the food must not be sold until safety is determined ([FDA summary of key changes](https://www.fda.gov/media/97317/download)).

This framework is geographically specific rather than a universal allocation of liability. It applies to covered U.S. motor- and rail-transport operations and contains exemptions and waivers. Nevertheless, it demonstrates a general principle: responsibility is partly constructed by law and contract, not dictated by the location of a refrigeration unit.

### Retail and household stages

Retailers decide how much remaining shelf life they will accept. Strict receiving specifications can protect customers and reduce in-store waste, but they can also push risk upstream. A retailer able to reject a load immediately may avoid recording spoilage in its own inventory, while a supplier with no secondary buyer absorbs the loss.

Consumers bear a different set of risks. When effective chains increase the supply of perishable foods, consumers may gain more consistent access, greater geographic variety, and potentially lower prices. When failures are hidden, however, they may bear illness risk or pay for losses through higher retail margins. Households also inherit the final refrigeration task. Advice aimed at U.S. households commonly uses 40°F, approximately 4.4°C, as an upper limit for keeping cold food cold, but that simplified safety rule is not a complete commercial storage specification for every commodity ([USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f)).

## How risk is distributed among actors

### Producers: biological and bargaining risk

Farmers ordinarily bear the first losses because they own the crop when it is most exposed and may be paid only for accepted output. Perishability weakens their ability to refuse a low offer: waiting is costly when the product is deteriorating. Small producers may also lack the volume, credit, land tenure, electricity connection, or assured market needed to justify their own cold facility.

Primary research in Kenya shows how these constraints interact. A study of 457 horticultural farmers in Lari Subcounty, Kiambu County, found that 295 reported some postharvest loss. Reported crop-specific losses ranged from 12% for courgettes and garden peas to 29% for carrots and onions. More than 97% used sacks or gunny bags, fewer than 2% used alternatives such as crates or baskets, and only 11% belonged to a farmer group or cooperative. The researchers also documented poor roads, vehicle breakdowns, low prices offered by brokers, and lack of refrigerated transport as linked problems rather than isolated technical defects ([Muiruri, Kalele, and Kibe, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/horticulture/articles/10.3389/fhort.2024.1474056/full)).

These are self-reported losses from a purposively selected subcounty, not a nationally representative measurement of Kenya. The study nevertheless demonstrates why offering a cold room alone may be insufficient. A farmer still needs suitable packaging, aggregation, transport, market information, and a buyer who rewards preserved quality.

### Logistics providers: utilization, energy, and liability risk

Cold-store and refrigerated-transport operators make capital-intensive investments whose revenue depends on throughput and utilization. Their expenses continue even when harvests are seasonal or storage occupancy is low. They also face energy-price volatility, unreliable grids, equipment failure, refrigerant management, maintenance, and claims over damaged shipments.

Contracts can allocate these risks in several ways. A carrier may promise a temperature range but exclude loss caused by warm loading, defective packaging, delay by the shipper, inherent product deterioration, or force majeure. A warehouse may charge per crate or per day while limiting compensation to the storage fee rather than the cargo’s market value. Cargo insurance can transfer some financial exposure to an insurer, but exclusions, deductibles, documentation requirements, and disputes over causation remain important. A temperature log might show an excursion without proving whether it caused the loss; conversely, poor sensor placement may fail to reveal damaging conditions.

Pay-per-use or “cooling as a service” models can reduce the producer’s capital barrier by moving equipment ownership to a specialist. Yet the operator then bears utilization and collection risk and may charge fees that small producers cannot afford during low-price periods. If access is rationed at harvest peaks, larger or repeat customers may receive priority unless governance rules explicitly protect smaller users.

### Wholesalers and retailers: rejection and inventory risk

Intermediaries are often described simply as beneficiaries of farmers’ weakness, but they perform real risk-bearing functions: assembling small lots, grading variable produce, finding buyers, financing transport, and absorbing unsold inventory. Their informational and financial advantages can nevertheless let them pass losses back to suppliers or forward to consumers.

The timing of title transfer is decisive. If ownership passes at the farm gate, the buyer bears subsequent quantity loss but can lower the price in anticipation. Under consignment, the farmer may retain ownership until final sale and therefore bear deterioration far downstream. Under strict purchase specifications, a buyer may take ownership only after inspection. A visible pile of discarded produce at a wholesale market does not by itself reveal who paid for it.

The location of measured waste is consequently not the same as the location of economic loss. Retailers may record little waste because they reject borderline shipments; farmers may record little physical disposal because damaged produce is sold at a deep discount. Measurement must follow money, ownership, quality downgrades, and final destinations as well as kilograms.

### Consumers and communities: price, access, safety, and public costs

Consumers can bear cold-chain costs through higher prices, especially where energy, transport, and financing are expensive. They may also bear the cost of an absent chain through scarcity, seasonal volatility, reduced access to fruits, vegetables, dairy, fish, and meat, or increased safety risk. Which effect dominates is an empirical question. Saved product does not automatically become cheaper food: additional value may be captured as farmer income, logistics revenue, retail margin, or quality premium.

Distribution among consumers also matters. A cold chain designed for export or premium supermarkets may bypass low-income domestic markets. The Kenyan study observes that cold infrastructure in developing-country settings has often favored export-oriented chains, while domestic smallholder channels remain fragmented ([Muiruri, Kalele, and Kibe, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/horticulture/articles/10.3389/fhort.2024.1474056/full)). Even when national supply increases, affordability and geographic access may remain unequal.

Communities and governments bear public costs too. Roads, electricity networks, market sites, inspection systems, workforce training, and emergency power may be publicly supported. If refrigeration equipment uses inefficient technology, carbon-intensive electricity, or high-global-warming refrigerants, environmental costs extend beyond the contracting parties.

## What empirical evidence does—and does not—show

Global estimates provide scale but should not be treated as precise measurements of what one local cold-room project will save. A joint UNEP–FAO assessment estimates that ineffective refrigeration contributed to the loss of about 12% of total food production in 2017. It also estimates that food cold chains account for roughly 4% of global greenhouse-gas emissions when both cold-chain technologies and losses attributable to inadequate refrigeration are included ([UNEP and FAO, *Sustainable Food Cold Chains*, 2022](https://www.unep.org/resources/report/sustainable-food-cold-chains-opportunities-challenges-and-way-forward)). These are modeled global estimates assembled from multiple datasets and assumptions, not the result of weighing every lost shipment. They establish that both missing refrigeration and refrigeration itself have material environmental consequences; they do not establish the return on any particular investment.

Detailed supply-chain studies expose additional uncertainty. In a 2019–2020 study of tomato chains in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, India, researchers surveyed 75 farm households, 83 tomato traders, 52 vegetable traders, and 50 retailers. Average preharvest quality loss was 13.9% of harvested tomatoes. Aggregate postharvest loss from farm through retail was estimated at either 9.1% or 13.4%, depending on whether the calculation used counted crates diverted to designated destinations or participants’ declarations ([Boiteau and Pingali, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170466/)).

That gap is not a statistical nuisance; it reveals a conceptual problem. Tomatoes fed to animals, composted, sold at lower grades, or discarded can be classified differently depending on whether the question concerns human-edible mass, economic value, environmental burden, or total biological use. The same study found that losses were lowest at trader stages and concentrated after harvest before tomatoes left the farm, while season, market conditions, and expected price were associated with loss. Its authors caution that single-point estimates can obscure patterns in crops harvested repeatedly and that the sample and interrupted production limited generalizability ([Boiteau and Pingali, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170466/)).

Together, the Indian and Kenyan evidence supports an established but bounded conclusion: losses emerge from interactions among biology, handling, infrastructure, prices, and market organization. It does **not** show that refrigeration alone would eliminate the reported percentages, nor that every kilogram saved would accrue to farmers or food-insecure consumers.

## Energy, refrigerants, and the risk of solving one problem by worsening another

Refrigeration shifts deterioration risk into energy and equipment risk. A conventional cold room connected to unreliable electricity may require diesel backup; fuel shortages or generator failures can then threaten the whole stored inventory. Solar power, thermal storage, efficient compressors, insulation, and remote monitoring can improve resilience, but each adds design choices, maintenance requirements, and financing needs.

Refrigerant leakage is another concern. The UNEP–FAO report recommends efficient equipment, low-global-warming-potential refrigerants, renewable energy, enforceable performance standards, and national cooling planning rather than uncoordinated equipment deployment ([UNEP and FAO, 2022](https://wedocs.unep.org/items/c53a122d-d424-48a3-8cd6-9fddc3cf0dc2)). “More cooling” is therefore not a sufficient policy objective. The relevant goal is reliable, appropriately sized, low-emission cooling embedded in a complete logistics and market system.

Scale presents a genuine trade-off. Large centralized warehouses may gain efficiency and professional maintenance but require long feeder journeys and favor large-volume clients. Small decentralized units shorten the distance to cooling and may improve access, yet can have higher unit costs, seasonal underuse, and limited repair support. The correct design depends on product mix, harvest calendar, road network, market distance, energy resources, and who can commit sufficient throughput.

## Toward fairer allocation of cold-chain risk

Equity does not require assigning all risk to one actor. It requires matching responsibility to control, making failures observable, and preventing stronger parties from transferring unmanageable exposure to weaker ones.

Several principles follow:

1. **Specify the product, not merely “cold.”** Contracts and operating procedures should define commodity-appropriate temperature, humidity, airflow, packaging, loading, and allowable time outside control. Mixed loads require explicit compatibility decisions.

2. **Assign duties at handoffs.** Records should identify who must precool, verify vehicle readiness, load correctly, monitor conditions, respond to alarms, and decide whether exposed food is safe or marketable.

3. **Preserve evidence.** Calibrated sensors, timestamps, loading records, product-temperature checks, and chain-of-custody documentation reduce disputes. Data ownership and access must also be specified; a monitoring system visible only to the buyer can deepen power asymmetry.

4. **Separate safety from commercial quality.** A cosmetic or shelf-life rejection is not automatically proof that food is unsafe. Secondary markets, processing, donation where legally and safely feasible, animal feed, and composting can prevent an all-or-nothing outcome, although each requires its own safety and economic assessment.

5. **Finance access, not only equipment.** Shared facilities, cooperatives, loan guarantees, public aggregation centers, or pay-per-use services may address capital barriers. Fees, booking priority, and liability rules determine whether smaller producers actually benefit.

6. **Reward preserved value.** If buyers pay no premium for better quality and impose no penalty for avoidable damage by downstream handlers, the party asked to invest may receive too little of the benefit. Pricing and claims systems should recognize quality preservation and remaining shelf life.

7. **Evaluate consumer outcomes directly.** Project assessment should measure not only tonnes cooled but also prices, dietary access, geographic distribution, safety performance, farmer income, service affordability, energy use, refrigerant leakage, and the incidence of rejected loads.

8. **Build redundancy proportionate to consequence.** Backup power, spare parts, maintenance contracts, emergency unloading sites, and insurance are most valuable where one failure can destroy a large aggregated inventory. Aggregation improves efficiency but also concentrates risk.

## Conclusion

The cold chain converts perishability from an immediate biological constraint into a managed portfolio of technical and economic risks. Cooling can slow deterioration, preserve safety, extend market reach, and reduce forced sales. At the same time, it creates dependence on power, machinery, data, standards, finance, and coordinated handoffs.

Who bears spoilage risk is not answered by asking who owns the refrigerator. Producers often bear early quality loss and weak bargaining positions; logistics providers bear equipment, energy, utilization, and some liability risk; wholesalers and retailers manage inventory while often retaining the power to reject; consumers bear consequences through prices, availability, diet quality, and safety; and the public bears infrastructure and environmental costs.

Established evidence supports integrated cold-chain investment where perishability, market access, and loss justify it. The same evidence counsels against treating refrigeration as an isolated technological fix. The fairest and most effective arrangements align control with responsibility, make temperature and ownership histories visible, distribute investment costs according to captured benefits, and ensure that preserved food reaches people who otherwise lack reliable access. The unresolved questions are primarily local: which products, markets, actors, and seasons generate benefits large enough to cover the full financial and environmental cost—and which contractual and governance arrangements keep those benefits from being captured by the strongest participant alone?
