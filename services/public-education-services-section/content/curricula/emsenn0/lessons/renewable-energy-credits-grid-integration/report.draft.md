# Renewable Energy Credits and Grid Integration: Who Benefits, Who Pays?

## Introduction

Renewable Energy Credits (RECs) solve an accounting problem: once electricity enters a shared grid, electricity from a wind turbine or solar array becomes physically indistinguishable from electricity generated elsewhere. A REC separates the renewable attributes of generation from the underlying electrical energy and assigns those attributes to an identifiable owner. In the United States, one REC represents the non-power attributes associated with one megawatt-hour (MWh), or 1,000 kilowatt-hours, of renewable electricity generated and delivered to the grid ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Retail RECs”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/retail-recs)).

That accounting system has important economic consequences. A renewable generator can sell electricity, capacity, grid services, and RECs as distinct products. A utility can retire RECs to satisfy a legal renewable portfolio standard. A business or household can voluntarily buy and retire them to substantiate a renewable-electricity claim. Regulators determine which generators qualify, how compliance is measured, whether costs may be recovered from ratepayers, and how transmission investments are allocated.

Yet a REC is neither electricity nor a physical grid service. It does not deliver power to a particular consumer, ensure that generation occurs when demand is highest, reserve transmission capacity, or by itself pay for storage and network upgrades. This distinction explains the central tension: REC systems can efficiently assign environmental claims and supply renewable projects with revenue, but their prices and eligibility rules do not necessarily reflect the location, timing, reliability value, or community effects of generation.

The answer to “who benefits and who pays?” therefore depends on several linked but legally distinct systems:

1. the REC market;
2. wholesale electricity and capacity markets;
3. utility procurement and retail ratemaking;
4. generator interconnection;
5. regional transmission planning; and
6. the distribution of environmental and economic effects among communities.

This report concentrates on the United States, where REC eligibility and electricity regulation differ substantially across states and regions.

## Prerequisites: electricity, attributes, and grid accounting

An electric generator produces physical energy measured in watt-hours. Power, measured in watts, is the instantaneous rate of generation or consumption; energy is power integrated over time:

\[
E=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}P(t)\,dt.
\]

A 100-megawatt generator operating at full output for one hour produces 100 MWh. If its output varies, annual energy can be approximated by:

\[
E_{\text{annual}}=C \times 8{,}760 \times CF,
\]

where \(C\) is nameplate capacity in megawatts and \(CF\) is the capacity factor. RECs are issued from metered energy, not merely from nameplate capacity. Thus, a hypothetical renewable plant producing 250,000 MWh in an eligible year could generate 250,000 RECs, subject to the applicable program’s verification and eligibility rules.

The physical electricity and the REC may be sold together—a “bundled” transaction—or separately. In an unbundled transaction, the electricity is sold as generic power while the REC is transferred independently. EPA emphasizes that an unbundled REC conveys no physical electricity to its purchaser ([EPA, “Retail RECs”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/retail-recs)). Consequently, a factory in one location may buy ordinary electricity from its utility and separately retire RECs from renewable generation elsewhere.

Electronic tracking systems record certificate creation, ownership transfers, and retirement. A unique identifier is assigned to each certificate so that only one account holder owns it at a time; regulators also use these systems to verify compliance with state standards ([EPA, “Energy Attribute Tracking Systems”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/energy-attribute-tracking-systems)). Retirement removes a REC from circulation and establishes the final owner’s exclusive claim.

This exclusivity matters. The Federal Trade Commission advises that an entity which operates solar panels but sells all associated RECs should not claim that it “uses” renewable energy; the renewable attribute has been transferred to the REC buyer ([FTC, “Environmental Claims: Summary of the Green Guides”](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/environmental-claims-summary-green-guides)). Physical hosting, financial ownership, receipt of electricity, and ownership of its renewable attributes can therefore belong to different parties.

## Two markets with different sources of demand

### Compliance markets

A renewable portfolio standard (RPS) requires covered utilities or retail electricity suppliers to procure a minimum quantity or percentage of eligible renewable electricity. RECs allow a supplier to document that procurement. States differ in their targets, eligible technologies, geographic boundaries, banking rules, alternative compliance payments, and technology-specific requirements ([EPA, “Renewable Portfolio Standards”](https://www.epa.gov/statelocalenergy/energy-and-environment-guide-action-chapter-5-renewable-portfolio-standards)).

A simplified compliance obligation is:

\[
R_{\text{required}}=S_{\text{covered}}\times r,
\]

where \(S_{\text{covered}}\) is covered retail sales in MWh and \(r\) is the required renewable fraction. If a supplier sells 10 million MWh and faces a 30% requirement, it must ordinarily retire 3 million qualifying RECs, subject to program-specific adjustments.

Some programs create carve-outs for particular resources. A solar carve-out reserves part of the total obligation for solar generation, creating demand for Solar Renewable Energy Certificates (SRECs). EPA explains that SREC prices are shaped chiefly by the mandated demand, eligible supply, and the cost of paying an alternative compliance charge instead ([EPA, “State Solar Renewable Energy Certificate Markets”](https://www.epa.gov/greenpower/state-solar-renewable-energy-certificate-markets)).

Compliance demand can give producers a revenue stream in addition to energy sales. If a generator produces \(Q\) MWh and sells its certificates for \(p_{\text{REC}}\) dollars per MWh, gross REC revenue is:

\[
V_{\text{REC}}=Qp_{\text{REC}}.
\]

This revenue can improve a proposed project’s expected cash flow and ability to secure financing. It is not pure profit: the generator still bears development, financing, operating, interconnection, and contractual costs.

Alternative compliance payments can act as an effective ceiling or reference point because an obligated supplier will compare the price of qualifying RECs with the cost and legal consequences of noncompliance. EPA reports that compliance markets tend to have higher prices than broad voluntary markets because eligibility restrictions constrain supply while mandates and penalties strengthen demand ([EPA, “U.S. Renewable Electricity Market”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/us-renewable-electricity-market)).

### Voluntary markets

Voluntary buyers include households, businesses, universities, governments, and other institutions purchasing renewable attributes beyond their legally assigned electricity mix. Nationally available, inexpensive unbundled RECs permit participation even when a buyer cannot contract directly with a local renewable plant. Buyers can also specify technology, location, project age, or generation vintage, although tighter restrictions generally increase prices by reducing the set of eligible certificates ([EPA, “Retail RECs”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/retail-recs)).

Voluntary and compliance claims must remain separate. A REC retired for an RPS cannot also be retired by a company as a voluntary purchase. EPA calls the generation left available beyond a mandate “regulatory surplus” and describes the mandatory market as a policy floor over which voluntary demand can operate ([EPA, “U.S. Renewable Electricity Market”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/us-renewable-electricity-market)).

Voluntary purchasing is not a single economic mechanism. Buying low-priced certificates from existing generators is different from signing a long-term power-and-REC agreement that stabilizes a new project’s future revenue. An EPA-sponsored 2025 analysis estimated that at least 230 voluntary buyers had signed long-term contracts supporting about 72 GW of solar and wind capacity, but it also cautioned that counterfactual attribution is difficult: some estimated deployment might have occurred through other buyers or revenue sources without the voluntary market ([EPA, *Impacts of Voluntary Renewable Energy Demand on Deployment*, pp. 12, 22–23](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2025-01/impacts_voluntary_renewable_energy_demand.pdf)).

That limitation is crucial. REC ownership establishes an accounting claim; it does not automatically establish “additionality”—that the buyer caused a project to be built or caused a specific quantity of emissions to be avoided. EPA explicitly distinguishes RECs from carbon offsets: offsets are denominated in metric tons of carbon-dioxide equivalent and require an additionality test, while RECs are denominated in MWh and do not require project additionality for a renewable-electricity-use claim ([EPA, “Market Instruments”](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/market-instruments)).

## How benefits and costs move among participants

### Clean-energy producers

Renewable generators receive the clearest direct financial benefit: the REC creates a saleable product in addition to energy, capacity, and ancillary services. A scarce compliance-eligible certificate can be much more valuable than a broadly eligible voluntary certificate. Technology carve-outs, geographic restrictions, project-age rules, and long-term procurement requirements can direct this value toward selected technologies or locations.

This mechanism also creates risk. REC prices can fall when supply outruns mandated or voluntary demand. A developer relying on future spot-market certificate sales faces more uncertainty than one with a long-term bundled contract. Program redesign can change eligibility, and a physically productive plant may lose certificate value without losing its ability to generate electricity.

Interconnection costs can absorb much of the prospective benefit. Berkeley Lab found nearly 2,600 GW of generation and storage seeking U.S. transmission access at the end of 2023, more than 95% of it zero-carbon generation or storage. Only 19% of projects, representing 14% of capacity, entering a studied subset of queues from 2000 through 2018 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2023 ([Berkeley Lab, *Queued Up: 2024 Edition*](https://emp.lbl.gov/publications/queued-2024-edition-characteristics)). Queue capacity is therefore evidence of developer interest, not a forecast of construction.

A separate Berkeley Lab synthesis reported that completed wind and solar projects had interconnection costs equal to roughly 6–8% of total project costs, while withdrawn projects faced estimates of roughly 30–37%; broader network upgrades, often charged partly or wholly to developers, were a major driver ([Berkeley Lab, “Grid Connection Barriers to New-Build Power Plants”](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/grid-connection-barriers-new-build-power-plants-united-states)). High anticipated REC revenue may encourage a developer to remain in the queue, but a REC cannot eliminate a binding transmission constraint.

### Utilities and retail suppliers

Utilities and suppliers benefit from a standardized, auditable compliance instrument. Instead of owning every renewable plant or tracing particular electrons to customers, they can procure qualifying certificates and demonstrate that a specified quantity of renewable generation entered the grid.

They also bear procurement and administrative obligations. In vertically integrated systems, a utility may build a plant or sign a long-term power purchase agreement and seek regulatory approval to recover costs. In retail-choice markets, competitive suppliers may buy RECs, bundled products, or make alternative compliance payments. The ultimate burden depends on contracts, market structure, and the state regulator’s treatment of costs.

Utilities can be both buyers and intermediaries. A utility green-pricing program may acquire additional RECs for subscribing customers, who pay a premium. Conversely, the utility may sell the RECs from a renewable asset to another party. If it does, neither the utility nor its ordinary customers should represent that particular generation as their renewable use, even though the electricity remains part of the local physical mix.

### Consumers and ratepayers

Consumers pay through several channels:

- the energy and capacity components of retail electricity rates;
- RPS or clean-energy compliance charges;
- transmission and distribution rates;
- voluntary green-pricing premiums or direct REC purchases; and
- taxes supporting public incentives or administrative programs.

Not every renewable-related cost is a REC cost. A wind farm’s interconnection line, a regional transmission expansion, reserve procurement, distribution upgrades, and a certificate registry are different expenditures. Combining them into one “renewable surcharge” can conceal both causation and benefits.

Berkeley Lab’s 2024 status analysis estimated that RPS compliance costs averaged roughly 4% of retail electricity bills across RPS states, with wide interstate variation and the highest reported values—about 11–12%—in states with solar carve-outs and high SREC prices ([Berkeley Lab, *U.S. State Renewables Portfolio & Clean Electricity Standards: 2024 Status Update*](https://climateprogramportal.org/public/resources/P_misc83.pdf)). These are estimated policy-compliance costs, not a universal measure of the net effect of renewable generation on bills. Renewable procurement may simultaneously alter wholesale energy prices, fuel-price exposure, capacity needs, transmission spending, and environmental damages. Different accounting boundaries can consequently produce different answers.

Cost incidence also differs from formal billing. A utility may initially pay for RECs, but regulators may permit recovery from customers. A developer may formally fund a network upgrade, yet incorporate that expense into the price required under a power purchase agreement. A tax credit may lower a project’s bid while shifting part of its support to taxpayers. Economic burdens can therefore migrate through contracts and rates.

### Regulators and governments

State legislatures define many renewable requirements, while public utility commissions oversee procurement and rate recovery in regulated utility territories. Regulators decide whether spending is prudent, how costs are divided among customer classes, and whether low-income protections or bill credits accompany a program.

At the federal level, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission governs major aspects of interstate transmission and wholesale electricity. FERC Order No. 2023 requires cluster-based interconnection studies, replacing sequential examination with group analysis intended to improve efficiency and allocate shared upgrade costs more systematically; applicants must also satisfy deposits and project-readiness requirements ([FERC, “Fact Sheet: Improvements to Generator Interconnection Procedures and Agreements”](https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/fact-sheet-improvements-generator-interconnection-procedures-and-agreements)). Order No. 2023-A clarified that allocation of certain substation-upgrade costs within a cluster depends on the number and voltage levels of connecting facilities ([FERC, “Explainer on Order No. 2023-A”](https://www.ferc.gov/explainer-interconnection-final-rule-2023-A)).

For longer-term regional investments, FERC Order No. 1920 requires transmission providers to conduct planning over at least 20 years, update it at least every five years, use at least three scenarios, evaluate specified benefits, and propose a default cost-allocation method. Its stated principle is that customers should pay only for projects from which they benefit ([FERC, “Fact Sheet: Building for the Future”](https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/fact-sheet-building-future-through-electric-regional-transmission-planning-and)). Applying that principle remains contentious because reliability, congestion relief, public-policy compliance, resilience, and access to cheaper generation can benefit different territories and customer groups over different time horizons.

## Why REC demand does not by itself integrate renewable generation

Grid operators must continuously balance supply and demand, maintain frequency and voltage, manage line limits, and hold sufficient resources for contingencies. Wind and solar output varies with weather and time of day. Their variability is predictable to a degree but not perfectly controllable.

The main integration options include:

- improved forecasting and shorter scheduling intervals;
- dispatch across larger balancing regions;
- flexible conventional generation;
- demand response and time-varying rates;
- batteries, pumped storage, and other storage;
- transmission expansion;
- advanced conductors, dynamic line ratings, and power-flow controls;
- renewable-plus-storage hybrids; and
- curtailment when production exceeds what can be used or transported.

The Department of Energy’s 2023 National Transmission Needs Study concluded that transmission expansion can connect remote renewable resources to load, reduce curtailment, and help serve electrification-driven demand. It also found that storage, distributed resources, advanced conductors, grid-enhancing technologies, and microgrids can complement conventional transmission, although they do not remove every need for new infrastructure ([DOE, *National Transmission Needs Study*, p. 111](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-12/National%20Transmission%20Needs%20Study%20-%20Final_2023.12.1.pdf)).

A conventional annual REC records energy quantity and attributes, but usually not the system value of the exact hour. Consider two hypothetical 1-MWh outputs:

- Solar plant A generates at noon during abundant solar output and local congestion.
- Wind plant B generates during an evening period of scarce supply.

Each may create one REC, although their wholesale prices, avoided emissions, congestion effects, and contributions to reliability can differ. Likewise, an unbundled REC purchased across the country does not reserve a transmission path from the generator to the buyer.

This creates a weak coupling between certificate incentives and grid needs. A high REC price may stimulate renewable construction, but if eligibility rules ignore deliverability, the projects may cluster where resources and land are inexpensive rather than where the network can absorb output. Curtailment then reduces energy and REC production, while interconnection and transmission costs rise.

Policy designers can tighten the coupling through geographic eligibility, deliverability requirements, time-differentiated certificates, hourly matching, storage incentives, or clean-capacity requirements. Each improvement has trade-offs. More detail can better represent grid value but raises measurement and transaction costs, reduces market liquidity, and may exclude small buyers or generators.

## Distributional fairness and public participation

A program can be cost-effective in aggregate while distributing burdens unfairly. Electricity charges consume a larger share of income for households with low incomes. DOE’s Low-Income Energy Affordability Data tool reports a national average household energy burden of about 6% for households at 0–80% of area median income, although burdens vary geographically and the measure includes electricity, gas, and other household fuels ([DOE, “Low-Income Energy Affordability Data Tool”](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool)). A uniform monthly REC or transmission charge is therefore regressive relative to income unless counterbalanced by discounts, credits, weatherization, or progressive public funding.

Benefits are also uneven. Project owners, landowners receiving lease payments, tax jurisdictions, contractors, and REC sellers may gain, while other communities host transmission corridors, substations, generation sites, or continued fossil facilities. Buying remote RECs can improve a purchaser’s accounting without reducing pollution near communities burdened by nearby combustion. Conversely, replacing local fossil generation may create health benefits not represented in the REC price.

Scale matters as well. Large corporate buyers can negotiate long-term contracts, hire specialists, and manage basis and price risk. Households generally encounter retail REC products or utility green tariffs with less control over project selection. Community solar, targeted procurement, local ownership, and bill-credit structures can spread participation, but none is an automatic property of a REC registry.

Procedural justice concerns who gets meaningful influence before decisions are made. FERC’s current long-term planning framework gives states a role in scenario development and cost allocation; Order No. 1920-B allows states to seek additional scenarios and, under specified procedures, distinguish costs caused by particular state policies ([FERC, “What State Regulators Need to Know About Order No. 1920-B”](https://www.ferc.gov/what-state-regulators-need-know-about-order-no-1920-b)). Formal state involvement, however, is not identical to direct participation by Tribal governments, municipalities, low-income customers, landowners, or environmental-justice communities. Fair process requires accessible data, early engagement, resources for participation, and an explanation of how testimony affected the result.

## A practical framework for evaluating a REC policy

A careful evaluation should ask five groups of questions.

### 1. What claim does the certificate support?

Identify the generator, technology, location, production date, tracking system, eligibility category, owner, and retirement record. Determine whether the claim is compliance-based or voluntary and whether the same generation appears in another mandate, tariff, or disclosure.

### 2. Does the incentive change investment or operation?

Estimate expected REC revenue relative to total project revenue and cost. Examine contract duration, price volatility, financing effects, and the counterfactual: what likely happens without the purchase? Treat additional generation as an empirical question rather than assuming that every retired REC caused new construction.

### 3. Is the project usable by the grid?

Examine interconnection status, deliverability, congestion, expected curtailment, capacity contribution, hourly generation, storage, and necessary transmission. Queue entry alone is not proof that a project will operate.

### 4. Who ultimately pays and benefits?

Trace costs through developers, utilities, wholesale prices, retail riders, taxes, and voluntary premiums. Disaggregate residential, commercial, industrial, and low-income customers. Include energy, capacity, transmission, distribution, reliability, emissions, health, land, employment, and tax effects without pretending they are all already embedded in the REC price.

### 5. How robust is the evidence?

Separate metered facts from modeled forecasts. State assumptions about fuel prices, load growth, technology costs, discount rates, transmission availability, and policy continuity. Report geographic and temporal boundaries. Where benefits cannot be monetized reliably, describe them rather than assigning false precision.

## Conclusion

RECs are best understood as instruments for ownership, accounting, and demand formation. They make renewable attributes tradable, prevent legitimate claims from being assigned twice, help utilities document compliance, and provide renewable producers with an additional source of revenue. They can support investment—especially when embedded in stable mandates or long-term contracts—but certificate retirement alone does not prove that a buyer caused new generation or reduced a known quantity of emissions.

The allocation of money is layered. Producers initially receive REC revenue but may bear substantial development and interconnection costs. Utilities and suppliers procure certificates and administer compliance, frequently recovering approved expenses from customers. Voluntary purchasers pay directly for claims beyond standard service. Regulators define eligibility and determine which utility and transmission costs enter rates. Taxpayers may finance complementary incentives, while host communities experience effects not captured in financial transactions.

Grid integration is governed by another set of mechanisms. Transmission, storage, operational flexibility, demand response, forecasting, and appropriately located capacity determine whether renewable electricity can serve demand reliably. REC rules can influence those decisions through location, timing, and deliverability requirements, but ordinary annual certificates provide only a coarse signal.

The fairest assessment therefore asks not simply whether more RECs were issued, but whether renewable generation was genuinely enabled, whether the grid could use it, whether claims were exclusive and accurately described, whether costs followed benefits, and whether households and communities had both protection and a voice. REC systems are useful infrastructure for the renewable economy; they are not a substitute for grid planning, consumer protection, or distributive policy.
