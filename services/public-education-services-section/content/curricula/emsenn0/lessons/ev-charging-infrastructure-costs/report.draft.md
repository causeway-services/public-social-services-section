# Electric-Vehicle Charging: Who Pays for the Grid and Who Controls Access?

## Introduction

An electric-vehicle charging station is simultaneously an electrical load, a transportation facility, a digital service, a real-estate use, and—sometimes—a regulated utility investment. That combination makes apparently simple questions such as “Who pays for the charger?” surprisingly incomplete. The charger itself may be purchased by a private operator, while taxpayers subsidize construction, utility customers finance an upstream transformer, a property owner supplies parking, and drivers pay electricity, demand, parking, and network fees. Control is similarly divided: utilities determine whether and when a site can be energized; site owners control physical access; charging networks manage authentication and data; regulators decide which costs enter electricity rates; and governments attach conditions to public funding.

The central distributional question is therefore not merely who initially writes a check. It is who ultimately bears costs and risks, who receives the resulting mobility and grid benefits, and who controls essential decisions about location, pricing, reliability, data, and service. Those dimensions can diverge. A ratepayer-funded station may be privately operated; a publicly subsidized charger may sit on privately controlled land; and a charger located in a disadvantaged census tract may chiefly serve highway travelers rather than nearby residents.

This report concentrates on the United States, where utility regulation and infrastructure policy vary substantially by state. It draws chiefly on federal rules and reports, a 2025 Government Accountability Office audit, and California regulatory programs as an illustrative—not nationally representative—case.

## Prerequisites: power, energy, and charging levels

Power and energy must be kept distinct. Power, measured in kilowatts (kW), is the instantaneous rate at which electricity moves. Energy, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), is power integrated over time:

\[
E = \int P(t)\,dt
\]

At constant power, this simplifies to \(E=P\times t\). Supplying 7 kW for four hours therefore transfers 28 kWh before accounting for losses. A battery does not ordinarily draw a charger’s nameplate power throughout an entire session: its acceptance rate depends on state of charge, temperature, vehicle limits, and the charging curve. The Department of Energy (DOE) warns that planning solely from charger nameplate capacity can consequently overstate actual requirements, especially because vehicles at a multi-port station neither arrive simultaneously nor share identical charging profiles ([DOE, *Impact of Electric Vehicles on the Grid*](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Congressional%20Report%20EV%20Grid%20Impacts.pdf)).

In broad terms, Level 1 charging uses ordinary low-voltage alternating-current service and is slow; Level 2 uses higher-power AC equipment suited to homes, workplaces, and long parking dwell times; direct-current fast charging (DCFC) converts power outside the vehicle and is designed for short stops or high-throughput operations. Charging time is not fixed by these labels alone: DOE reports that it can range from less than 20 minutes with some fast chargers to 20 hours or more with Level 1, depending on battery capacity, initial state of charge, equipment output, vehicle capability, and electrical service ([DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center, “Electric Vehicle Charging Stations”](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-stations?lv=true)).

The distinction matters economically. A driver who parks overnight usually needs energy by morning, not maximum instantaneous power. A highway driver values speed and turnover. A bus or truck depot may need many vehicles ready at a fixed departure time. Designing every application around the fastest possible charge would purchase capacity that often sits unused; designing only around average daily energy could leave vehicles unavailable when needed.

## What must actually be built

“Installing a charger” can involve at least four layers:

1. **Charging equipment:** the cable, connector, power electronics, display, payment hardware, and enclosure.
2. **Customer-side or site infrastructure:** electrical panels, switchgear, conduit, trenching, foundations, protective equipment, networking, lighting, signs, and accessible parking configuration.
3. **Utility-side infrastructure:** service extensions, transformers, feeders, substations, and sometimes higher-voltage upgrades.
4. **Operational systems:** network subscriptions, payment processing, maintenance, cybersecurity, customer support, data reporting, and land or parking management.

The term **make-ready infrastructure** usually describes the electrical work necessary to prepare a parking location for charging, although particular programs divide “utility-side” and “customer-side” make-ready work differently. That definition is consequential: a subsidy covering the charging cabinet but not trenching or a transformer may cover only a minority of a difficult site’s cost.

DOE’s current procurement guidance illustrates the cost spread. It reports approximate public-equipment costs of $3,500 per Level 2 connector and $38,000–$90,000 per DC fast connector, with higher output generally costing more. Installation averages about $2,500 per public or workplace Level 2 connector, while DC-fast installation can range from $20,000 to $60,000 per connector. These figures are not turnkey quotations: geography, trenching, electrical upgrades, labor, permitting, site size, and economies of scale all change the result ([DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center, “Procurement and Installation”](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-infrastructure-development)).

“Soft costs” can be material even though they do not appear in a charger catalog. They include site acquisition, design, zoning, permits, inspections, utility applications, easements, financing, and the cost of waiting. DOE reports that even Level 2 interconnection where power is available has ranged from one day to six months, while DC-fast projects may take six months to more than two years; required upgrades, equipment availability, easements, and local administrative processes help explain the variation ([DOE, “Understanding the Soft Costs of EV Charging”](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/vehicles/understanding-soft-costs-ev-charging)).

Grid construction can take still longer. DOE’s 2024 report cites approximate timelines of two to seven years for some distribution upgrades and ten to fifteen years for transmission upgrades, versus six months to more than two years for charging-infrastructure deployment ([DOE, *Impact of Electric Vehicles on the Grid*](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Congressional%20Report%20EV%20Grid%20Impacts.pdf)). These are planning ranges rather than universal lead times, but they explain why utilities want forecasts before vehicles arrive and why charging developers fear paying for assets based on uncertain future use.

## How charging affects the grid

An EV is a flexible load only within limits. A parked vehicle may be able to shift charging within a several-hour window, but the driver ultimately requires a usable battery at departure. Unmanaged charging begins when a vehicle is plugged in without reference to grid capacity or electricity price. Managed charging varies power or timing in response to such constraints while respecting the vehicle’s energy requirement. Federal regulations define **smart charge management** as controlling charger output to meet customer needs while responding to power-demand or pricing signals for load management, resilience, or other grid benefits ([23 C.F.R. Part 680](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)).

This flexibility matters because grids are built around peaks, not merely annual energy. If 100 vehicles each require 40 kWh overnight, they need 4,000 kWh. Charging all of them at 7 kW simultaneously creates a potential 700 kW load; spreading the same energy evenly across ten hours requires an average of 400 kW. Energy is unchanged, but required peak capacity falls by roughly 43 percent in this simplified example. Real systems must account for charger losses, arrival times, minimum vehicle needs, baseline building load, and safety margins, so this arithmetic is illustrative rather than a site design.

Managed charging can reduce demand charges for commercial customers, avoid coincidence with system peaks, use otherwise underutilized grid capacity, and sometimes defer transformers or feeder upgrades. DOE cites California modeling in which distribution investment through 2035 could approach $50 billion if EV needs were met only with conventional utility assets and without dynamic rates or flexible-load strategies. The same federal report also cites research finding 46–61 percent lower projected New York distribution-upgrade costs under managed-charging scenarios. These are model-dependent projections, not realized national savings, and should not be transferred mechanically between jurisdictions ([DOE, *Impact of Electric Vehicles on the Grid*](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Congressional%20Report%20EV%20Grid%20Impacts.pdf)).

Bidirectional charging adds the possibility of sending stored energy from a vehicle to a building or grid. Its theoretical value includes backup power, demand response, and other grid services. Yet value depends on vehicle availability, compatible equipment, interconnection rules, communications, battery-warranty terms, compensation, and the driver’s required reserve. DOE characterizes several sophisticated grid-integration approaches as emerging or still requiring validation, so vehicle-to-grid capability should not be counted as guaranteed capacity merely because a vehicle has a large battery ([DOE, *Impact of Electric Vehicles on the Grid*](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Congressional%20Report%20EV%20Grid%20Impacts.pdf)).

## Who pays—and who assumes risk

### Drivers and site hosts

Drivers pay directly through per-kWh prices, time-based fees, subscriptions, parking charges, or taxes. They also bear non-price costs: detours, queues, unreliable equipment, time spent charging, and the need to maintain accounts or payment instruments. Renters may indirectly pay through higher rent or parking charges even when the property owner receives an installation incentive.

Site hosts may pay for land, parking-space conversion, construction, customer-side electrical work, maintenance, and the nonfederal share of grants. In return, they may collect charging revenue, attract customers, support employees or tenants, meet regulatory obligations, or increase property value. Revenue risk is substantial during early adoption: a station can have high fixed costs but too few charging sessions to cover them.

### Utilities and ratepayers

A utility may pay upfront for service extensions, transformers, make-ready work, or even chargers, then recover approved expenditures through electricity rates. Economically, “utility-funded” usually means **ratepayer-funded over time**, potentially with an authorized return on capital. The rationale is that infrastructure can enable additional electricity sales and public benefits. If charging occurs off-peak, new sales may spread fixed system costs across more kWh and benefit customers generally; if investments are oversized or charging intensifies peaks, non-EV ratepayers may instead bear stranded or avoidable costs. DOE therefore emphasizes the need to balance reliability, investment timing, third-party alternatives, and customers’ ability to pay ([DOE, *Impact of Electric Vehicles on the Grid*](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Congressional%20Report%20EV%20Grid%20Impacts.pdf)).

California demonstrates a strong socialization model. Under the California Public Utilities Commission’s EV Infrastructure Rules, ratepayers cover service-line extensions and associated distribution infrastructure for eligible separately metered or submetered charging. The commission incorporated these utility-side investments into general rate cases rather than requiring a separate charging-program application for every category ([CPUC, “Distribution Infrastructure and Planning”](https://webproda.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/infrastructure/transportation-electrification/distribution-infrastructure-and-planning)). This lowers an individual developer’s connection cost but transfers cost-allocation authority to regulators and utilities.

The distributive concern is that electricity bills are not equivalent to progressive taxation. Lower-income households may contribute through rates even if they cannot afford an EV, lack off-street parking, or live far from subsidized facilities. Conversely, refusing all shared investment can preserve charging scarcity precisely where private utilization and profits are initially too low to justify construction. Equity therefore depends on the package: who pays, where facilities are placed, who can use them, and whether added electricity revenue or avoided pollution returns measurable benefits to contributing communities.

### Taxpayers and public programs

The 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act created the $5 billion National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Formula Program (NEVI) and a $2.5 billion Charging and Fueling Infrastructure discretionary program. NEVI funding runs through fiscal years 2022–2026 and may support acquisition, installation, network connection, operation, maintenance, and data sharing ([U.S. Department of Transportation, “Federal Funding Programs”](https://www.transportation.gov/rural/ev/toolkit/ev-infrastructure-funding-and-financing/federal-funding-programs)).

Federal support does not eliminate local financing. GAO found that the federal share of a NEVI project is 80 percent, leaving a 20 percent nonfederal share; CFI may cover up to 80 percent. GAO’s interviewees also reported that some upstream utility equipment may fall outside eligible project costs, posing particular difficulty where rural sites lack nearby power ([GAO-25-106992](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-106992/index.html)). Thus a nominally 80-percent grant can leave a state, site host, utility, or its ratepayers responsible for both matching funds and expensive enabling infrastructure.

Public-private partnerships divide these roles in many possible ways. A government may own the land and equipment but contract operations; a private firm may own everything after receiving a capital grant; or a utility may build make-ready infrastructure while a competitive charging provider owns the charger. Federal rules allow charging revenue to support debt service, a reasonable private return, operation and maintenance, public-private partnership payments, or other Title 23 purposes ([23 C.F.R. §680.106](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)). This permits private participation but also makes contract terms—ownership duration, price controls, performance penalties, data access, and disposition of public assets—central to evaluating value for money.

## Who controls access

Physical location is the first form of control. A technically public charger may be practically inaccessible if it is behind a gate, limited to business hours, poorly signed, incompatible with trailers, obstructed, or attached to paid parking. Landowners can influence hours and parking enforcement; municipalities control curb use, zoning, permits, and public rights-of-way; utilities control energization; and charging operators control accounts, prices, maintenance, and network availability.

Technical interoperability is another form. Federal rules require covered chargers to use specified vehicle and network communication standards, including ISO 15118 capabilities, Open Charge Point Protocol, and Open Charge Point Interface. They also require network-switching capability without hardware replacement ([23 C.F.R. §§680.108–680.116](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)). These provisions seek to reduce vendor lock-in and enable roaming, mapping, remote management, and “Plug and Charge.” Nevertheless, standards do not by themselves guarantee reasonable prices, good maintenance, universal connector compatibility, or continued service after a network provider fails.

Federally covered stations must clearly display prices and additional fees and provide information on accepted payment methods. Each charging port must maintain average annual uptime greater than 97 percent under a regulatory formula that excludes certain utility interruptions, scheduled maintenance, vandalism, natural disasters, and vehicle-caused failures ([23 C.F.R. §680.116](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)). Because excluded downtime does not count against the metric, 97 percent regulatory uptime is not identical to a driver’s probability of successfully charging. Reliability assessment should also examine derated power, broken payment systems, blocked stalls, queues, and failures that monitoring systems misclassify.

Data control matters as well. Networked chargers can reveal location, time, energy use, vehicle identifiers, and payment information. Federal rules require safeguards for consumer data and standardized reporting, but centralized data can still create privacy, cybersecurity, and market-power risks ([23 C.F.R. Part 680](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)). Public planners need enough information to evaluate reliability and equity, while drivers have legitimate interests in limiting surveillance and commercial reuse.

## Equity: access is more than station count

Home charging remains the dominant and usually most convenient model, but it privileges households with dedicated parking, electrical capacity, and authority to modify property. Renters and residents of multifamily buildings may face landlord approval, split incentives, shared parking, panel constraints, and expensive retrofits. Public charging is therefore not merely a long-distance amenity; it substitutes for unavailable home infrastructure.

Geographic disparities are substantial. GAO reported that in May 2025 the United States had about 219,000 public ports—approximately 162,000 Level 2, 56,000 DC fast, and 800 Level 1—and cited evidence that 60 percent of urban residents lived within one mile of a public charger, compared with 41 percent of suburban and 17 percent of rural residents. GAO also reported that stakeholders saw rural energization as difficult where new electrical lines or equipment could take years ([GAO-25-106992](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-106992/index.html)).

Proximity alone is an incomplete equity metric. Relevant questions include:

- Is charging affordable relative to household income?
- Can nearby renters use it overnight without accumulating parking or idle fees?
- Is the route accessible to disabled drivers and safe at night?
- Does the station accept payment without a smartphone, bank account, or subscription?
- Does it serve locally useful destinations, fleets, transit, or only through-travelers?
- Who receives construction jobs, operating revenue, and improved air quality?
- Are residents able to influence siting, or merely informed after decisions are made?

California’s near-term priority program shows how funding conditions can redistribute both investment and ownership. It required at least 50 percent of each qualifying program’s budget to be spent in underserved communities, consultation with more than one community-based organization, local or Tribal-government support, and outreach and economic-equity provisions. Utility ownership of customer-side make-ready infrastructure and chargers was limited to underserved-community sites, with utility ownership capped at 50 percent of customer-side infrastructure overall ([CPUC, “Near-Term Priority Investments”](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/infrastructure/transportation-electrification/charging-infrastructure-deployment-and-incentives/near-term-priority-investments)). These are procedural and expenditure safeguards, however; they do not automatically prove that residents received affordable, reliable service or long-term wealth.

Federal equity policy has also changed. The August 2025 NEVI interim final guidance reduced plan requirements concerning community engagement and grid integration and rescinded earlier language asking states to target at least 40 percent of benefits toward disadvantaged communities. The same change gave states more flexibility over corridor spacing and when to expand beyond corridors ([USDOT, August 11, 2025 NEVI announcement](https://www.transportation.gov/briefing-room/president-trumps-transportation-secretary-sean-p-duffy-unveils-revised-nevi-guidance)). As of the federal guidance index updated in July 2026, the document remained labeled “Interim Final Guidance” ([FHWA, “Guidance / Regulations”](https://highways.fhwa.dot.gov/iija/guidance-regulations)). This is a change in administrative guidance, not proof that generally applicable civil-rights or accessibility law disappeared: federally funded chargers remain subject to the Americans with Disabilities Act and Title VI requirements identified in the federal rule ([23 C.F.R. §680.118](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title23-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title23-vol1-part680.pdf)).

## What the evidence does—and does not—establish

The strongest established findings are structural. Fast charging generally requires more expensive equipment and site capacity than slower charging; connection cost and time vary greatly by location; coincident demand can trigger grid investment; and managed charging can reduce peaks when vehicle schedules permit it. Public rules and utility tariffs materially determine who pays and who controls the resulting infrastructure.

The magnitude of future costs is less certain. Forecasts depend on EV adoption, vehicle efficiency, driving patterns, charging location and timing, existing feeder headroom, equipment prices, land costs, and the success of load management. Scenario estimates such as California’s potential $50 billion distribution requirement are warnings about a modeled pathway, not invoices already incurred.

Federal program outcomes are also not yet fully measurable. GAO found that only 384 NEVI- and CFI-funded chargers were open as of April 2025 and concluded that FHWA lacked performance goals measuring progress toward some intended outcomes, including improved access. It also found no near-term performance goals for CFI ([GAO-25-106992](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-106992/index.html)). Subsequent policy changes mean the audit cannot resolve the programs’ eventual cost-effectiveness or equity.

A defensible evaluation should therefore follow money, control, and outcomes separately. It should report full lifecycle cost; distinguish taxpayer, ratepayer, host, and driver contributions; publish ownership and contract rights; measure successful charging rather than installed ports alone; compare prices and reliability across neighborhoods; and evaluate who uses the stations and receives local benefits. Without that information, “public,” “utility-funded,” “equitable,” and even “operational” can conceal more than they reveal.

## Conclusion

EV charging redistributes responsibility across two infrastructures that were historically planned separately: roads and electricity. Utilities can socialize grid upgrades, governments can subsidize equipment, private firms can own networks, municipalities can allocate land, and drivers can pay for use—yet none of those arrangements alone determines fairness.

A sound policy matches charging power to actual mobility needs, uses flexible demand where practical, allocates upgrade costs according to transparent principles, and preserves competition and interoperability. An equitable policy goes further: it treats affordability, rental housing, rural service, disability access, payment access, reliability, participation, and local economic benefits as outcomes to be measured rather than promises inferred from a station’s map location.

The enduring question is not whether public or private actors should pay. Both inevitably will. It is whether the institutions assigning those costs also give affected communities meaningful control and can demonstrate that benefits—mobility, cleaner air, resilient service, economic opportunity, and lower system costs—reach the people asked to finance the transition.
