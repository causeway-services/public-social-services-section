## Who this book is for

You already pay for an agentic coding subscription -- Claude Code, Codex,
or something like them -- and you've started running more than one agent
at a time, because one agent working alone leaves obvious throughput on
the table. This book assumes exactly that starting point and nothing
more. It does not teach you what an AI coding agent is, how to write a
prompt, or how to pick a model. It teaches the one thing that almost
nobody teaches explicitly: the operating discipline that keeps two or
more agents from costing you more than they save.

If you have ever had an agent quietly overwrite a colleague's uncommitted
work, watched a "done" report turn out to be wrong once you actually
opened the file, or spent an afternoon untangling what three parallel
agents did to the same directory, this book is about you.

## How this book is organized

Chapters 1 through 6 each teach one discipline: writing a complete brief,
recognizing the fan-out anti-pattern, steering a running agent, recovering
an interrupted commission, receiving a report without accepting an
unverified claim, and using dated receipts as memory between sessions.
Chapter 7 is a single worked case study -- a real, dated incident where
two concurrent agent sessions collided on one shared, uncommitted
repository -- walked from the collision to its resolution. Chapter 8 pulls
the six disciplines into one operating checklist you can actually run day
to day, and Chapter 9 collects twelve common situations into quick
diagnoses you can check a live problem against. Chapter 10 extends the
whole discipline from one operator to a small team. A glossary and an
appendix of five reusable templates -- a brief template, a fan-out
decision checklist, a recovery runbook, a minimal receipt format, and a
coordination protocol for two or more people (or agents) sharing one
repository -- close out the book.

Every chapter includes at least one worked example grounded in an ordinary
software project -- nothing here requires you to understand any
particular company's internal tooling or vocabulary. The case study in
Chapter 7 is real and dated; every other example is a composite built to
be representative rather than a transcript of one specific session.

## A note on honesty

None of the discipline in this book is theoretical advice arrived at by
thinking hard about what agents *should* do. Every rule traces back to a
specific failure: a redone task, a silently overwritten file, a "done"
that wasn't true on disk. The rule is what would have prevented the
failure, written down after the failure happened, not before. Where a
worked example is illustrative rather than a literal transcript, this
book says so. Where it is a real incident, this book says that too.
