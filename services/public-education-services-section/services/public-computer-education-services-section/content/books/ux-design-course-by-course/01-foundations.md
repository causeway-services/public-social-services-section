## What UX is

Watch a member of the Northside Tool Library try to borrow a ladder today.
They find the phone number on a flyer, call on a Tuesday evening, wait on
hold, learn the ladder is out, and are told to call back next week. Nothing
in that is a screen, and all of it is **user experience (UX)**: everything a
person encounters, does and feels while trying to get something done with a
product or service.

**Usability** is the measurable core of it. The international standard
defines usability as the extent to which specified users can achieve
specified goals with **effectiveness**, **efficiency** and **satisfaction**
in a specified context of use [@iso9241-11]. Effectiveness: did they get the
ladder? Efficiency: how much time and effort did it take? Satisfaction: how
did it feel?

**User interface (UI)** design is the design of what people see and touch:
screens, buttons, type, color. UX is broader: it includes the research,
structure and flow that decide what the interface should be. A beautiful
interface can still be a poor experience.

## Who does UX

UX work is shared among specialists:

- **UX designers** shape flows, structure and interactions.
- **UX researchers** study users and test designs.
- **UX writers** write the words in the interface.
- **Visual (UI) designers** set type, color, layout and imagery.
- **Interaction designers** design how elements respond to people.
- **UX engineers** build interfaces in code, bridging design and
  engineering.

Designers work with **product managers** (who decide what to build and why),
**engineers** (who build it) and **stakeholders** (who fund or depend on it).
An entry-level designer often works as a **generalist**, doing some of each,
and grows toward a specialty; many teams value **T-shaped** people, broad
across UX with depth in one area.

## User-centered design and the design process

**User-centered design** puts users' needs at the center of every decision.
The international standard for human-centred design sets out its principles:
design is based on an explicit understanding of users, tasks and
environments; users are involved throughout; design is driven and refined by
evaluation with users; the process is iterative; it addresses the whole
experience; and the team brings multidisciplinary skills [@iso9241-210].

The certificate follows the five modes of **design thinking** taught by
Stanford's d.school [@dschool2010]:

1. **Empathize:** learn about users and their needs.
2. **Define:** state the problem to solve.
3. **Ideate:** generate possible solutions.
4. **Prototype:** make a version people can try.
5. **Test:** try it with users and learn.

The stages loop: testing sends you back to define or ideate. Britain's Design
Council draws the same work as a **Double Diamond**: discover and define (the
first diamond, getting the problem right), then develop and deliver (the
second, getting the solution right), each opening wide and then narrowing
[@design-council2005]. The diamond's shape matters. Teams that close on a
solution too early reproduce the first idea they saw, flaws included, a
tendency researchers call **design fixation** [@jansson-smith1991]. Every
method in this book that says "generate many options before choosing" exists
to delay that closure.

## Why things are easy or hard to use

Don Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things* gives the vocabulary for
explaining an experience [@norman2013]:

- An **affordance** is a relationship between an object and a person that
  makes an action possible: a button affords pressing.
- A **signifier** shows where and how to act: a raised, labeled button, an
  underlined link. Affordances that aren't signified go unused.
- **Mapping** is the relationship between controls and their effects: a
  "Reserve" button next to the tool it reserves.
- **Feedback** tells people what happened: "Reserved for Saturday."
- A **conceptual model** is the person's understanding of how the thing
  works. Good design gives people an accurate one.

Norman names two gulfs a design must bridge: the **gulf of execution** (can
people figure out how to do what they want?) and the **gulf of evaluation**
(can they tell whether it worked?).

## Accessibility and equity

Disability advocates put it plainly: a person using a wheelchair isn't
disabled by the wheelchair but by the staircase. That's the **social model of disability**: a
barrier is a property of the design, so removing it is the designer's job.

Four related approaches:

- **Accessibility:** people with disabilities can perceive, understand,
  navigate and interact with the product. The Web Content Accessibility
  Guidelines organize this under four principles: perceivable, operable,
  understandable and robust [@wcag22].
- **Universal design:** design usable by as many people as possible without
  adaptation. Ronald Mace and colleagues set out seven principles, from
  equitable use to tolerance for error [@mace1997].
- **Inclusive design:** design that considers the full range of human
  diversity, including ability, language, culture, age and circumstance.
- **Equity-focused design:** design that recognizes that people start from
  unequal positions and works with the groups most affected, not only for
  them. Sasha Costanza-Chock's *Design Justice* argues that those who will
  live with a design should help lead it [@costanza-chock2020].

**Assistive technologies** are what people use to reach a product: screen
readers, which speak the interface aloud; screen magnifiers; voice control;
switch devices for people who can't use a pointer; captions and transcripts.
Designing for them from the start is cheaper and better than retrofitting.

Accessibility helps everyone. Curb cuts serve wheelchairs, strollers and
delivery carts; captions serve deaf viewers and people in noisy rooms.

## Platforms

A **platform** is the environment a product runs in: desktop web, mobile web,
phone apps, tablets, watches, voice assistants, televisions. Each has its own
conventions, screen size, input (mouse, touch, voice) and context of use.
Design for the platform people will actually use, and follow its conventions,
because users bring expectations from every other app on it.

## Design sprints

A **design sprint** is a five-day process for answering a big question
through design, prototyping and testing with users. Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky
and Braden Kowitz developed it at Google Ventures [@knapp2016]:

1. **Monday, map:** agree on a long-term goal and pick a target.
2. **Tuesday, sketch:** each person sketches solutions, alone.
3. **Wednesday, decide:** choose the strongest and storyboard it.
4. **Thursday, prototype:** build a realistic prototype.
5. **Friday, test:** watch five customers use it.

The course names its phases understand, ideate, decide, prototype and test;
they're the same days. A sprint needs a **Decider** (who makes the final call)
and a **Facilitator** (who runs the process), a clear question, the right
people for five days, and a room or shared board.

After any sprint or project, a **retrospective** asks what went well, what
didn't and what the team will change.

## Worked example: seeing the tool library through UX eyes

**Usability, measured.** Today's phone system: effectiveness is poor (half
of calls end without a tool), efficiency is poor (two evenings a week, hold
times), satisfaction is low.

**Norman's gulfs.** Execution: members can't tell what's available without
calling. Evaluation: after a reservation by phone, there's no confirmation,
so members aren't sure it worked. Signifier to add: availability shown on
each tool. Feedback to add: a confirmation message.

**Accessibility and equity.** Some members use screen readers, some speak
Spanish at home, some don't have smartphones. The team plans to include them
in research from the start, and to keep a phone option.

**A design sprint.** The volunteers run a five-day sprint on one question:
"Can members find and reserve a tool without calling?" By Friday they've
watched five members try a clickable prototype.

## Answers

1. Effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction, for specified users, goals and
   context.
2. A signifier: it affords pressing but nothing shows that it can be
   pressed.
3. Empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test.
4. Reproducing the first idea or example you saw, including its flaws.
   Generating many options, alone and before judging them, guards against it.
5. From the design of the environment, not from the person.
6. Monday map, Tuesday sketch, Wednesday decide, Thursday prototype, Friday
   test.
7. Any two of: screen readers, screen magnifiers, voice control, switch
   devices, captions.
8. Any three of: UX researcher (studies users and tests designs); UX writer
   (writes the interface's words); visual or UI designer (type, color,
   layout); interaction designer (how elements respond); UX engineer (builds
   interfaces in code).
9. What went well, what didn't, and what the team will change; after a
   sprint or project, or at regular points during one.
