## Designing for social good

**Designing for social good** means using design to address a public or
community need: access to services, health, education, civic participation,
the environment. The tool library is one: it saves members money and keeps
tools out of landfills.

It raises the stakes of everything in this book. The people served often have
the least time, money, connectivity or institutional trust. Equity-focused
design says to involve them as partners from the start, and to let those most
affected help lead [@costanza-chock2020]. In practice: recruit research
participants from the community itself; pay them for their time; share
findings back; keep options for people without smartphones or data plans;
and measure success by what changes for them, not by downloads.

## One service, many devices

People use a service on more than one device, often in the same day. Michal
Levin describes three ways a multi-device experience can be designed
[@levin2014]:

- **Consistent:** the same core experience on each device, adapted to its
  screen and input.
- **Continuous:** a task started on one device continues on another (a
  reservation begun on a laptop, finished on a phone).
- **Complementary:** devices play different roles that work together (a
  phone scans a tool's code while the website shows its manual).

**Dedicated app or responsive web app?** A **dedicated (native) app** is
installed from an app store. It can use the device's features (camera,
notifications, offline storage) and feel fast, but people must choose to
install it. A **responsive web app** runs in the browser on any device, needs
no install and reaches everyone with a browser, with more limited access to
device features. Many services offer both, or start with the web.

**Progressive enhancement** starts from a core experience that works
everywhere, on the oldest browser and the slowest connection, then adds
richer features where the device supports them. **Graceful degradation**
starts from the full experience and makes sure it still works, in a reduced
form, when features are missing. Progressive enhancement is usually the
safer default for public services, because it guarantees the core.

## The portfolio case study

A portfolio shows employers how you work. Each project becomes a **case
study** with a consistent structure:

1. **Overview:** the product, your role, the timeline, the tools, and the
   outcome in one or two sentences.
2. **The problem:** the users and their need, with the problem statement.
3. **Research:** what you did, who you learned from, and the key insights.
4. **Design:** how ideas became wireframes, prototypes and mockups, with the
   decisions and why you made them.
5. **Testing and iteration:** what testing showed and what you changed.
6. **Accessibility:** what you did for it, specifically.
7. **Outcome and next steps:** what you would measure, and what you would do next.

Show your thinking, not only final screens: sketches, rejected ideas and the
reasons, before-and-after comparisons from testing. Keep each case study
skimmable: headings, short paragraphs, captioned images.

The certificate builds three end-to-end projects [@google-ux-2026], each a
case study:

- **A mobile app,** taken from research to a high-fidelity prototype and
  tested; Chapters 1 to 6 prepare you for it.
- **A responsive website,** designed across breakpoints and tested;
  Chapter 7.
- **A cross-device design for social good,** a dedicated mobile app and a
  responsive website for the same service; Chapter 8.

Put your strongest first. Host the portfolio on a site you control
or a portfolio platform, and check that the portfolio itself meets the
accessibility standards you claim.

## Getting ready for work

Job preparation is outside this book's scope, except for three habits that
follow from the design process itself: explain your work in terms of users and
outcomes; show how you handle critique by describing a design that changed
after testing; and keep learning in public, through a portfolio that grows.

## Worked example: the tool library across devices

**Social good.** The research included members without smartphones, found
through the library's front desk, and paid them with free membership. Two of
them joined the volunteers' design reviews.

**Devices.** A responsive web app first, so members can use any device
without installing anything. Consistent core (browse, reserve) everywhere;
continuous reservations across devices through the member's account;
complementary use at the library, where a phone scans a code on the tool to
check it out while the website shows the tool's safety guide.

**Progressive enhancement.** The core (see availability, place a hold)
works as plain web pages with forms, even without scripts. Where supported,
the page updates availability live and sends notifications. Members without
data plans can still reserve by text message.

**The case study.** Overview: "Northside Tool Library: letting members see
and hold tools without calling. Role: sole designer, eight weeks, web app.
Outcome: in testing, five of five members held a tool in under two minutes."
Then the problem statement, the journey map, the insight about weekday names
in the time picker and its fix, the accessibility work (labels, focus order,
contrast), and next steps (measure wasted trips after launch).

## Answers

1. Any two of: recruit participants from the community; pay them for their
   time; share findings back; involve them in design reviews; keep options for
   people without smartphones or data.
2. Continuous.
3. A dedicated app can use device features such as notifications and offline
   storage; a responsive web app reaches anyone with a browser without
   installing anything.
4. Progressive enhancement starts from a core that works everywhere and adds
   features; graceful degradation starts from the full experience and makes
   sure it still works when features are missing.
5. Overview; the problem; research; design; testing and iteration;
   accessibility; outcome and next steps.
6. They show how you think and decide, which is what employers want to see,
   not only the final result.
7. Because it guarantees the core service works for everyone, including
   people with old devices or slow connections, before adding anything.
8. Choose the three that best show the whole process and a range of
   platforms (such as an app, a website and a cross-device design), and put
   the strongest first.
