# Seattle: Climate History and Outlook

## Reading Seattle’s climate correctly

Seattle’s climate is often reduced to a stereotype: mild, gray, and rainy. That description captures part of the experience but obscures the features most important for understanding climate change. Seattle has a strongly seasonal climate, with cool wet winters and comparatively dry summers. Its proximity to Puget Sound moderates temperature, while the Olympic and Cascade mountain ranges influence rainfall, snowpack, wind, and regional water supply. Average annual precipitation in Washington’s Puget Sound Lowlands is about 45 inches, but most falls during the cool season; summer is naturally dry by Pacific Northwest standards ([Washington State Climate Office](https://climate.uw.edu/2021/07/08/climatology-of-was-puget-sound-lowlands-climate-division-3/)).

Climate is not the same as weather. Weather describes short-lived atmospheric conditions; climate describes the statistical distribution of weather over decades. NOAA’s official U.S. climate normals are averages and related statistics calculated over uniform 30-year periods. The current standard normals cover 1991–2020 and include temperature, precipitation, snowfall, and other variables from thousands of stations ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)). A normal is therefore a reference—not a promise about what any particular year will bring.

That distinction matters in a warming climate. A 30-year average can describe the recent climate, but comparison of successive normals is not by itself a complete climate-change analysis. NOAA cautions that station moves, instrument changes, missing-data estimation, and later corrections to historical records can affect comparisons between normal periods. Long-term trend studies generally require homogenized observations designed to separate real climatic change from such non-climatic discontinuities ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

Seattle also lacks a single observation that perfectly represents the whole city. The long-running Seattle–Tacoma International Airport station lies south of Seattle and has more open, paved surroundings than many residential neighborhoods. Elevation, distance from Puget Sound or Lake Washington, vegetation, building density, and exposure to marine air all create local differences. Regional projections are therefore best treated as estimates of a changing probability distribution, not precise forecasts for every block.

## Why Seattle has wet winters and dry summers

Seattle lies in the midlatitudes, where prevailing atmospheric circulation commonly brings Pacific weather systems from west to east during the cool season. Moist air crossing the Olympic Mountains rises and cools, producing heavy precipitation on their windward western slopes. Seattle sits partly in the Olympics’ rain shadow, so it receives substantially less precipitation than the outer Washington coast. The Cascade Range forms another major barrier east of the city.

The Pacific Ocean and inland marine waters limit temperature extremes under ordinary conditions. Water heats and cools more slowly than land, helping keep winters relatively mild and many summer days comfortable. The Washington State Climate Office describes the Puget Sound Lowlands as a region where proximity to water moderates temperature throughout the year ([Washington State Climate Office](https://climate.uw.edu/2021/07/08/climatology-of-was-puget-sound-lowlands-climate-division-3/)).

Summer dryness has a different large-scale cause. During much of summer, the northeastern Pacific subtropical high favors sinking air and steers many storms northward. Clearer skies, long days, and limited rainfall can dry soils and vegetation even when air temperatures are not exceptionally high. This seasonal pattern helps explain why warming has consequences beyond a simple increase in annual average temperature: hotter summers can intensify soil-moisture loss, vegetation stress, water demand, wildfire danger, and human heat exposure during the season when rainfall is already scarce.

The surrounding mountains connect Seattle’s climate to areas well beyond the municipal boundary. Much of the region’s water is naturally stored as winter snow and released during spring and summer. “Snow water equivalent,” or SWE, measures the depth of liquid water contained in the snowpack. April 1 SWE is commonly used as an indicator of spring water storage. Washington projections indicate that Cascade snowpack has already declined, especially at lower elevations, and could fall by roughly 40–60 percent on average by the 2080s under a high-greenhouse-gas scenario ([University of Washington Climate Impacts Group](https://data.cig.uw.edu/picea/mauger/misc/2401006part1.pdf)). The exact loss depends on elevation, location, future emissions, and model behavior; the highest North Cascades are expected to retain snow longer than lower and more southerly terrain.

## What has already changed

Seattle Public Utilities reports that the city’s average annual air temperature has risen approximately 1.3°F since 1895 ([Seattle Public Utilities](https://www.seattle.gov/utilities/protecting-our-environment/community-programs/climate-change)). That value summarizes a long-term trend; it does not imply uniform warming in every year, season, or neighborhood. Natural variability—including El Niño and La Niña, ocean conditions, and shifting atmospheric circulation—continues to produce cool years, cold outbreaks, wet summers, and other departures from the trend.

The newer NOAA normals nevertheless place recent conditions in a broader national pattern. NOAA found that most of the United States was warmer in 1991–2020 than in the preceding 1981–2010 normal period. In the Northwest, the newer July normal was both markedly drier and warmer, although NOAA emphasizes that changes in individual months can reflect recurring circulation patterns as well as the long-term warming signal ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

Historical precipitation presents a less simple story than temperature. Year-to-year and decade-to-decade variation is large, and a clear annual-total trend does not necessarily reveal changes in seasonality or extremes. Seattle’s 2023 climate vulnerability assessment concludes that annual precipitation will remain highly variable while its timing changes: fall and winter are expected to become wetter, summer drier, and more winter precipitation concentrated in extreme rainfall events ([City of Seattle, *Climate Vulnerability Assessment*](https://www.seattle.gov/documents/departments/opcd/seattleplan/seattleclimatevulnerabilityassessmentjuly2023.pdf)). These are projections, not statements that every future winter will be wetter than the one before it.

Sea level supplies another historical measure. Seattle Public Utilities states that sea level in Puget Sound has risen approximately nine inches since 1900 ([Seattle Public Utilities](https://www.seattle.gov/utilities/protecting-our-environment/community-programs/climate-change)). Local relative sea level—the height of water relative to land—combines global ocean expansion and land-ice loss with regional ocean circulation and vertical land movement. Consequently, a global mean sea-level number cannot simply be transferred to Seattle without local adjustment.

## The 2021 heat dome: an event and a warning

The June 2021 Pacific Northwest heat wave demonstrated how far Seattle’s weather can depart from its historical range. A NOAA-affiliated analysis reported that the event exceeded Seattle’s previous record by approximately 3°C and attributed its immediate meteorological severity to a persistent omega-shaped atmospheric blocking pattern. Sinking air warmed through compression, while strong solar heating continued beneath the block ([James Overland, NOAA Institutional Repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/40148)).

The physical mechanism of the event and the influence of long-term climate change are related but distinct questions. The blocking pattern explains why the heat occurred at that time and place. Background warming raises the temperature from which such circulation anomalies begin and can alter the probability that thresholds are exceeded. A single event is not “caused” by one factor in the everyday sense; event attribution instead asks how human influence changed its likelihood or intensity.

The public-health consequences were measurable. Washington’s Department of Health recorded 100 heat-related deaths from June 26 through July 2, 2021, and 157 through August 31. Thirty-four occurred in King County, where Seattle is located. People aged 65 or older accounted for 106 of the 157 statewide deaths, and males accounted for 61 percent. The agency also reported that heat-related emergency-department visits across federal Region 10—Alaska, Idaho, Oregon, and Washington—were 69 times more frequent from June 25–30 than during the corresponding 2019 period ([Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/emergencies/be-prepared-be-safe/severe-weather-and-natural-disasters/extreme-heat/hot-weather-precautions/heat-wave-2021)).

Those numbers require careful interpretation. They cover Washington, not Seattle alone, and certified heat-related deaths may understate total mortality because heat can aggravate cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and other illnesses without being identified on the death certificate. Conversely, statewide totals cannot be assigned to the heat dome’s hottest days without considering delayed deaths and later hot weather. A separate state analysis therefore compared observed deaths with deaths expected from 2011–2019 trends, illustrating why excess-mortality analysis can complement cause-of-death counts ([Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/422243-ExcessDeathsCOVID19HeatDome.pdf)).

The event also revealed a mismatch between past building assumptions and emerging conditions. A region historically moderated by marine air has many homes not designed for prolonged extreme heat. Risk varies within the city: tree cover, shade, impervious surfaces, housing quality, floor level, ventilation, occupational exposure, health status, and access to cooling all affect exposure. A citywide average temperature cannot represent these differences.

## The warming outlook

All credible projection frameworks indicate continued warming in Seattle, but numerical results depend on the baseline, emissions pathway, climate models, downscaling method, geographic domain, season, and temperature metric. A statement about annual mean temperature cannot be substituted for one about the hottest day, nighttime minimum, or summer maximum.

Seattle Public Utilities summarizes current planning information as approximately 5.5°F of additional average annual warming by 2050 ([Seattle Public Utilities](https://www.seattle.gov/utilities/protecting-our-environment/community-programs/climate-change)). The city’s climate vulnerability assessment cites a projected increase of about 6.3°F in maximum summertime air temperature by 2050 ([City of Seattle, *Climate Vulnerability Assessment*](https://www.seattle.gov/documents/departments/opcd/seattleplan/seattleclimatevulnerabilityassessmentjuly2023.pdf)). These figures should not be read as contradictory: they refer to different temperature measures and may use different baselines or modeling products.

The general mechanism is well established. Additional greenhouse gases reduce the rate at which Earth loses energy to space, changing the planet’s energy balance. The oceans absorb much of the added heat, and land temperatures rise in response to the altered balance. Locally, atmospheric circulation, marine influence, soil moisture, vegetation, and urban surfaces shape how global warming is expressed.

Model ensembles are used because no single climate model captures every process or every pattern of natural variability. The University of Washington’s Pacific Northwest projection tool presents output from multiple generations of global models and separates the 2050s, defined as 2040–2069, from the 2080s, defined as 2070–2099. It also distinguishes the Pacific Northwest, western and eastern sides of the Cascades, seasons, and emissions scenarios ([University of Washington Climate Impacts Group](https://cig.uw.edu/resources/analysis-tools/pacific-northwest-climate-projection-tool/)). Ensemble spread represents model disagreement and natural variability, while differences between emissions pathways become increasingly important later in the century.

Even if changes in annual precipitation remain modest relative to natural variability, higher temperature alters the water cycle. Warmer air can hold more water vapor, which can contribute to heavier rainfall when storms have sufficient moisture and lift. At the same time, greater evaporative demand can dry soils and vegetation more rapidly during rain-free periods. Thus heavier winter downpours and more intense summer dryness can occur within the same future climate.

## Rain, flooding, landslides, and infrastructure

Seattle faces three broad flood mechanisms: urban flooding when drainage capacity is exceeded, river or creek flooding, and coastal flooding. City emergency managers identify urban and riverine flooding as the most common today and expect climate-driven sea-level rise and more frequent heavy rainfall to increase future urban and coastal risks ([Seattle Office of Emergency Management](https://www.seattle.gov/emergency-management/disaster-impacts/all-hazards/floods)).

Extreme rainfall matters because damage depends not only on annual totals but also on intensity, duration, antecedent soil moisture, topography, drainage condition, and whether high tides impede discharge. A storm that delivers several inches gradually may have different effects from the same total concentrated in a few hours. When soils are already saturated, additional rain increases runoff and pore-water pressure, potentially contributing to landslides on steep slopes. Urban pavement and rooftops reduce infiltration and route water quickly into drainage systems.

Climate projections do not yet specify the exact rainfall for a future Seattle storm on a particular date. Instead, they indicate shifts in statistical properties such as the frequency or intensity of heavy precipitation. Infrastructure designed using historical intensity–duration–frequency curves may become less reliable if the underlying climate distribution is no longer stationary.

Adaptation therefore involves more than enlarging pipes. Possible measures include detention and storage, restored floodplains, green stormwater infrastructure, permeable surfaces, creek restoration, maintenance of drainage systems, updated design standards, and limits on development in repeatedly inundated areas. Each option has constraints: green infrastructure occupies land and requires maintenance; engineered systems can be expensive; and defenses may redistribute water or create a false sense of security if events exceed their design level.

## Sea-level rise and the Duwamish Valley

Seattle’s sea-level outlook is especially consequential for low-lying shorelines, port and industrial land, wastewater and drainage infrastructure, habitat, and the Duwamish Valley. Seattle Public Utilities reports a 50 percent probability that local relative sea level will rise by at least 0.8 feet by 2050. For 2100, its planning page presents a range of roughly 2.4–5.2 feet, based on Washington-specific projections derived from climate-model and sea-level research ([Seattle Public Utilities, “Projections & Maps”](https://seattle.gov/utilities/protecting-our-environment/community-programs/climate-change/projections-and-maps)).

Those figures are probabilistic scenarios, not a single forecast. Future emissions, the response of Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, ocean dynamics, and vertical land motion all contribute uncertainty. The upper end is particularly sensitive to processes affecting ice-sheet stability. Risk management may appropriately use different percentiles for different assets: a park with a short design life may tolerate more risk than a wastewater facility or transportation corridor expected to operate for many decades.

Sea-level rise raises the baseline on which tides and storm surge act. Seattle’s mapping convention uses Mean Higher High Water, the average of each day’s higher high tide, referenced to the North American Vertical Datum of 1988. The city notes that with approximately three feet of sea-level rise, monthly high tides, king tides, and storm surge would reach progressively higher levels above today’s reference elevation ([Seattle Public Utilities, “Projections & Maps”](https://seattle.gov/utilities/protecting-our-environment/community-programs/climate-change/projections-and-maps)). This illustrates why an increase in average level can produce a much larger change in flood frequency: water need not wait for an unprecedented storm to reach elevations that previously flooded only rarely.

The Duwamish Valley combines physical exposure with social and environmental burdens. The city’s 2023 assessment maps a 2050 high-end scenario incorporating approximately 3.1 feet of sea-level rise plus storm surge and shows inundation affecting several blocks along the river ([City of Seattle, *Climate Vulnerability Assessment*](https://www.seattle.gov/documents/departments/opcd/seattleplan/seattleclimatevulnerabilityassessmentjuly2023.pdf)). That map is a scenario for planning, not a prediction that every shaded parcel will be permanently underwater in 2050. Actual flooding will depend on tides, storms, river flow, groundwater, barriers, drainage, and future adaptation.

## Water supply, ecosystems, smoke, and marine change

Seattle’s drinking-water security depends partly on mountain hydrology. Warmer winters cause more precipitation to fall as rain rather than snow, and earlier melting shifts runoff toward winter and spring. Lower summer snow storage does not automatically mean shortage—reservoir operations, conservation, demand, and annual precipitation matter—but it reduces one form of natural seasonal storage. The projected 40–60 percent decline in average spring snowpack by the 2080s under a high-emissions scenario is therefore a planning challenge rather than a direct forecast of tap-water availability ([University of Washington Climate Impacts Group](https://data.cig.uw.edu/picea/mauger/misc/2401006part1.pdf)).

Warmer, drier summers can also lower streamflow and raise water temperature. Cold-water species such as salmon face compounded stress from high temperature, low dissolved oxygen, habitat fragmentation, and altered flow timing. The city’s assessment expects warming water, declining summer flow, and reduced riparian shade to intensify stress in shallow waterways, while acknowledging that site-specific habitat responses remain uncertain ([City of Seattle, *Climate Vulnerability Assessment*](https://www.seattle.gov/documents/departments/opcd/seattleplan/seattleclimatevulnerabilityassessmentjuly2023.pdf)).

Wildfire is usually not an urban ignition problem within central Seattle, but regional fires can expose the city to smoke. The precise future frequency of smoke episodes depends on fire weather, fuels, ignition, land management, and wind direction. Climate-driven summer warming and drying can create more favorable burning conditions, yet a seasonal climate projection cannot specify how many smoke days Seattle will experience in a given year.

Puget Sound also experiences interacting changes in temperature, oxygen, and carbonate chemistry. Atmospheric carbon dioxide enters seawater and alters its carbonate system, lowering pH and reducing carbonate availability. Local biological production, respiration, freshwater inputs, nutrients, and circulation modify this global signal. Research in Puget Sound seagrass habitats shows that seasonal metabolism can change buffering capacity and accelerate the timing at which ecological thresholds are crossed ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency research record](https://assessments.epa.gov/risk/document/%26deid%3D362518)). It would be misleading to attribute every low-pH episode solely to global carbon dioxide, but equally misleading to treat ocean acidification as only a local pollution problem.

## What is established, and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions are well supported:

- Seattle has warmed over the observational record, and continued warming is expected under every major emissions pathway.
- Extreme heat will become more important even though marine influence will continue to moderate many days.
- Rising relative sea level will increase the frequency and reach of coastal flooding.
- A larger share of mountain precipitation will fall as rain, spring snowpack will decline, and seasonal runoff will shift.
- Winters are expected to favor heavier precipitation, while summers become hotter and generally drier.
- Exposure and adaptive capacity vary sharply among neighborhoods and populations.

Important uncertainties remain. The magnitude of late-century warming and sea-level rise depends strongly on future global emissions. Models disagree about changes in regional circulation, individual storms, and year-to-year precipitation. Neighborhood-scale heat depends on land cover and urban development that are themselves changeable. Future mortality depends not only on temperature but also on cooling access, housing, public-health systems, demographics, acclimatization, and behavior. Adaptation can substantially alter impacts without altering the underlying hazard.

Seattle’s outlook is therefore neither a forecast of uniform catastrophe nor a reason for complacency. The city’s historically mild climate is shifting, but the most consequential outcomes will emerge from interactions among physical hazards, infrastructure, ecosystems, and unequal social vulnerability. The appropriate lesson from climate projections is not that every future detail is known. It is that the direction of several major changes is clear enough to justify planning across a range of plausible futures.
