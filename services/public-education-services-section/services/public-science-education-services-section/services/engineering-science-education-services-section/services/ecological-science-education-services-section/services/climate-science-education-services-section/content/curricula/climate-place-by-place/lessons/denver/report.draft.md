# Denver: Climate History and Outlook

## A high, dry city shaped by mountains

Denver’s climate cannot be understood from latitude alone. The city lies near 40°N on the western edge of the Great Plains, immediately east of the Rocky Mountains, and roughly one mile above sea level. Denver International Airport—the current official climate station—is at 5,414 feet (1,650 meters), according to the station metadata in the [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s 1991–2020 climate normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003017).

This setting produces a semi-arid continental climate with large temperature swings, relatively little annual precipitation, abundant winter sunshine, warm summers, and periodic intrusions of both very cold and very warm air. Elevation helps make nights cooler than they would be at lower elevations, while the nearby mountains strongly influence wind, clouds, and precipitation. Air descending the eastern slope can warm and dry rapidly; under other weather patterns, easterly “upslope” flow can lift moist air against the terrain and produce rain or heavy snow.

Denver therefore sits at a climatic boundary. It is a Plains city whose weather is repeatedly rearranged by mountain topography. It receives much less precipitation than the high country to its west, yet much of its water supply originates in those wetter, snowier mountains. The distinction between local weather and regional water climate is essential: rain falling within Denver can help vegetation and reduce irrigation demand, but it generally does not replenish the mountain reservoirs on which the metropolitan water system depends. Denver Water states that its supply is entirely surface water drawn from about 4,000 square miles of watersheds on both sides of the Continental Divide, principally through rivers, reservoirs, and streams fed by mountain snowmelt ([Denver Water, 2026 water-quality report](https://www.denverwater.org/your-water/water-quality/water-quality-reports/2026-report)).

## How to read Denver’s climate record

A climate normal is not a prediction or an unchanging ideal. It is a statistical description of a specified reference period. NOAA’s current conventional U.S. normals cover 1991–2020; the agency recommends that period for most present-day comparisons and explains that official normals are recalculated after each decade ending in zero ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)). A temperature “above normal” consequently means above the 1991–2020 average, not necessarily above the average of the nineteenth century or of a future climate.

The station history also requires care. Denver’s official observations came from the Downtown City Office from 1872 through December 1949, Stapleton Airport from January 1950 through February 1995, and Denver International Airport from March 1995 onward, according to the [National Weather Service’s documentation of Denver climate extremes](https://www.weather.gov/bou/Denver_Monthly_Extremes?lv=true). These sites differ in elevation, surrounding development, exposure, and distance from the urban center. NOAA applies quality-control and homogenization methods to major climate datasets, but users should still avoid treating the composite Denver series as if one unchanged instrument had occupied one unchanged location since 1872.

Snowfall is especially sensitive to observing location and procedure. Official snowfall observations did not move to Denver International Airport until the 2007–2008 snow season, leaving too little time for a 30-year snowfall normal at that site; the National Weather Service instead gives a 1991–2020 seasonal normal of 47.0 inches based on its documented Denver snowfall series ([NWS snowfall history](https://www.weather.gov/bou/SeasonalSnowfall?lv=true)). Comparisons of individual snowstorms across centuries are therefore informative, but less clean than a continuous record from a single site would be.

These qualifications do not make the record unusable. They define what can responsibly be inferred from it. Monthly airport normals describe recent conditions near the current official station. The long composite record describes the broader history of officially observed Denver weather. Statewide gridded data and climate-model ensembles are better suited to identifying regional trends and future changes that extend beyond one station.

## Denver’s recent baseline climate

At Denver International Airport, the 1991–2020 normal annual mean temperature is 51.2°F (10.7°C). Normal daily maximum and minimum temperatures averaged across the year are 65.1°F and 37.2°F, respectively ([NOAA monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003017)).

January is normally the coldest month, with a mean of 31.7°F, an average daily high of 44.6°F, and an average daily low of 18.7°F. July is normally the warmest, with a mean of 75.1°F, an average high of 89.9°F, and an average low of 60.2°F. These averages conceal substantial variability: during a typical year, the airport records about 46 days with highs of at least 90°F, approximately 156 nights at or below freezing, and about six nights at or below 0°F ([NOAA monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003017)).

Normal annual precipitation is only 14.48 inches (368 millimeters). May is normally the wettest month, with 2.16 inches, followed closely by July at 2.14 inches and June at 1.94 inches. December and January are the driest months by liquid-equivalent precipitation, at 0.35 and 0.38 inches, respectively. Measurable precipitation of at least 0.01 inch occurs on roughly 80 days per year, but days receiving at least one inch average only about two per year ([NOAA monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003017)).

The annual cycle reflects several mechanisms. Spring brings stronger storm systems and can produce Denver’s largest snowfalls because the atmosphere contains more moisture than in midwinter while surface temperatures can still support snow. Warm-season precipitation is often delivered by thunderstorms rather than prolonged steady rain. Colorado’s precipitation is influenced by interacting large-scale and local processes, including the polar jet stream, the North American monsoon, El Niño–Southern Oscillation, upslope and downslope flow, and localized circulation near the urban corridor; this complexity is one reason future precipitation projections remain much less certain than temperature projections ([Denver Water climate-adaptation overview](https://www.denverwater.org/your-water/water-supply-and-planning/climate-change)).

Denver can consequently be snowy without being wet. Snow depth measures the physical thickness of snow, whereas snow-water equivalent, or SWE, measures the liquid water contained in it. Ten inches of powder and ten inches of dense spring snow can have very different SWE. Colorado’s water managers therefore emphasize SWE because it is more directly connected to runoff and reservoir inflow. The state climate assessment reports that SWE is monitored at automated SNOTEL stations and manually sampled snow courses, most of which are in the mountains rather than in Denver itself ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 3](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/3_water.html)).

## What has already changed?

The clearest established change is warming. The 2024 *Climate Change in Colorado* assessment reports that statewide annual average temperature increased by about 2.3°F from 1980 through 2022, with the greatest seasonal warming in autumn. Relative to the 1971–2000 baseline, Colorado had already warmed by approximately 1.5°F by the decade ending in 2022, although the precise value depends on the calculation method ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 2](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/2_temp_precip.html)).

Denver’s local record is consistent with that regional warming, but the exact local trend should not be inferred simply by connecting the first and last raw station observations because the official station moved twice. Denver Water’s synthesis of the local record reports that only three years since 2000 were cooler than Denver’s 1971–2000 annual average ([Denver Water climate-adaptation overview](https://www.denverwater.org/your-water/water-supply-and-planning/climate-change)). That is evidence that recent conditions are predominantly on the warm side of the late-twentieth-century distribution, not proof that every year must be warmer than the previous one.

Temperature extremes have changed asymmetrically across Colorado. Hot days and heat waves have become more common, while cold nights and cold waves have generally become less common. The observed decline in four-day cold waves from 1951 to 2020 was more modest than the rise in heat waves and was not statistically significant in any of the assessment’s climate divisions. By contrast, increases in extreme heat were significant across most of the state ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)). A warming climate therefore does not eliminate severe cold or snowstorms; it shifts their probabilities while increasing the background temperature on which individual weather systems act.

The assessment defines one heat-wave metric using four consecutive days: the mean temperature for each day is

\[
T_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{T_{\max}+T_{\min}}{2},
\]

and a heat wave occurs when the four-day average exceeds the threshold that was reached, on average, once per year during 1971–2000. This relative definition allows meaningful comparisons among Colorado’s hot plains, cooler mountains, and urban corridors. It differs from an absolute definition such as “four days above 95°F,” so numerical results from the two definitions should not be interchanged ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)).

Precipitation history is less straightforward. Colorado’s statewide precipitation varies greatly from year to year and has not exhibited a change comparable in clarity to its temperature trend. There are indications that heavy and extreme rainfall has increased, but results depend on the indicator and period examined; the evidence is not yet as consistent as it is in some other U.S. regions ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)). This distinction matters: confidence in warming does not imply equal confidence in the future annual precipitation total.

Hydrologic changes are clearer than annual precipitation alone because warming affects what happens after precipitation falls. Across Colorado’s major river basins, April 1 SWE during the twenty-first century has averaged 3% to 23% below the 1951–2000 average. Since 2000, annual streamflow has averaged 3% to 19% below the earlier baseline, and published modeling studies have attributed as much as half of the decline since 1980 in some Colorado basins to warming temperatures ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 3](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/3_water.html)). Precipitation deficits have also contributed; warming is not the only cause.

## Why warming changes water even if precipitation does not

Water supply depends on a chain of processes rather than precipitation alone:

\[
\text{precipitation}
\rightarrow \text{snow storage}
\rightarrow \text{snowmelt}
\rightarrow \text{soil and vegetation}
\rightarrow \text{streamflow}
\rightarrow \text{reservoir storage}.
\]

Warming can alter every arrow. More cool-season precipitation may fall as rain instead of snow at marginal elevations. Snow can melt earlier. Warmer air increases atmospheric demand for moisture, raising evaporation from water and soil and transpiration from plants when water is available. Drier soil can absorb more snowmelt, leaving less water to enter streams. These effects explain why stable annual precipitation does not guarantee stable runoff.

Colorado’s mountain snowpack acts as a natural seasonal reservoir. It stores about 15 million acre-feet of water around its spring peak, and roughly 60%–80% of annual streamflow in rivers with headwaters above 8,000 feet originates as snowmelt. About 70%–80% of annual runoff in most snow-fed Colorado streams occurs from April through July ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 3](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/3_water.html)). Earlier melting can therefore shift water toward late winter and spring, away from the hotter months when urban landscaping, agriculture, and ecosystems often need it most.

Denver’s engineered system partly compensates for this mismatch. The utility collects precipitation and snowmelt across approximately 4,000 square miles and stores spring runoff for later use. In an average year, its system captures about 290,000 acre-feet—approximately 94 billion U.S. gallons—and conveys water through rivers, tunnels, canals, reservoirs, and treatment facilities ([Denver Water, “Where does your water come from?”](https://www.denverwater.org/tap/where-does-your-water-come)). Reservoirs provide resilience, but they do not remove climate risk: very low snowpack, earlier runoff, dry soils, high evaporative demand, wildfire damage, population, and customer demand can interact.

The extreme 2025–2026 water year illustrates this interaction, but one year must not be mistaken for a long-term trend by itself. Denver Water reported that snowpack in its Colorado River collection area peaked on March 18, 2026, at 58% of normal, while the South Platte collection area peaked on March 17 at 42% of normal. The utility described both peaks as more than a month earlier than normal and reported reservoir storage at 67% of capacity on September 28, compared with a normal 91% for that date ([Denver Water supply update, September 28, 2026](https://www.denverwater.org/tap/denver-water-snowpack-and-water-supply-update)). These are observations of an exceptional drought year. Their relevance to climate lies in the mechanisms—heat, early melt, and high evaporative demand—not in an assertion that every future year will resemble 2026.

## The outlook: what is robust and what remains uncertain

### Temperature and heat

Continued warming is the most robust conclusion. Under the medium-low emissions pathways RCP4.5 and SSP2-4.5, model ensembles project Colorado’s statewide annual temperature for 2035–2064 to be about 2.5°F–5°F warmer than 1971–2000 in CMIP5 and about 3.5°F–5°F warmer in the central 80% of CMIP6 results. Because approximately 1.5°F of that warming had already occurred by 2022, these ranges correspond to roughly another 1°F–3.5°F in CMIP5 and 2°F–3.5°F in CMIP6 relative to the assessment’s recent climate ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 2](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/2_temp_precip.html)).

Those are statewide projections, not a precise Denver forecast. As an educational analogue, the assessment estimates that another 2°F of warming would give Denver a seasonal temperature regime resembling present-day Pueblo; another 4°F would resemble present-day Lamar. The authors explicitly warn that this comparison concerns temperature only, not precipitation or every other feature of those places ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 2](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/2_temp_precip.html)).

Heat waves are expected to change more dramatically than annual averages suggest. Under RCP4.5, the median model result raises the occurrence of what was historically a once-per-year four-day heat threshold to about ten occurrences per year by the 2060s—roughly 40 days annually meeting that historical heat-wave definition. All projections analyzed showed significant long-term increases, though their magnitudes varied ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)). Urban surfaces can compound exposure by storing heat, while trees, shade, building quality, air conditioning, occupation, health, and income affect who is most vulnerable. Thus, regional warming may be similar across a metropolitan area while human risk is not.

### Precipitation, storms, and floods

Annual precipitation is substantially less predictable. For 2050 under RCP4.5, the Colorado assessment’s models range from approximately 7% less to 7% more annual statewide precipitation than the late-twentieth-century average; projections centered on 2070 are similar ([Climate Change in Colorado executive summary](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/exec_summ.html)). This range should not be summarized as “no change.” It means the sign of the annual change is uncertain.

Meanwhile, heavier rainfall can intensify even if the annual total changes little. A warmer atmosphere can contain more water vapor, but storm formation also requires suitable moisture transport, instability, and lifting. Colorado’s topography and varied storm types make local outcomes complex. The state assessment assigns medium confidence to future increases in extreme precipitation and flooding, but low confidence to changes in tornadoes, windstorms, and several thunderstorm hazards. It also notes that land cover, drainage infrastructure, burn scars, and development influence flood damage independently of rainfall ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)).

### Snowpack and streamflow

Future snow and runoff losses are more likely than the ambiguous precipitation projection might suggest. Most modeled projections show Colorado April 1 SWE declining by 5%–30% by mid-century relative to 1971–2000. The snowpack’s seasonal peak is projected to occur several days to several weeks earlier, depending on warming and precipitation. Most modeled basin projections likewise show annual streamflow falling by 5%–30%, with summer and autumn flows declining as runoff shifts one to four weeks earlier ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 3](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/3_water.html)).

These figures are ranges, not promises. A sufficiently large increase in cool-season precipitation could offset some warming-driven loss, and individual wet years will continue. Yet in the hydrologic ensemble used by the assessment, 65%–80% of simulations across each major basin produced lower mid-century streamflow even though most underlying climate simulations produced slightly more annual precipitation. That result demonstrates the importance of evaporation, soil moisture, snow storage, and runoff efficiency ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 3](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/3_water.html)).

### Drought, wildfire, and air quality

Warming increases drought severity by raising evaporative demand and reducing the benefit of precipitation. The assessment therefore concludes with high confidence that Colorado droughts will become more frequent or severe even though future precipitation remains uncertain. Severe droughts are not new—the state experienced major episodes in 2002, 2012, 2018, and 2020—but heat now adds stress to precipitation shortages ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)).

Colorado has also experienced more large fires and greater burned area since 2000, with fires occurring at higher elevations and greater intensity than in the late twentieth century. Climate is only part of the causal system: fuel accumulation, past fire suppression, land management, ignition sources, and expansion into fire-prone landscapes also matter. Nevertheless, higher temperature accelerates fuel drying, and multiple studies support further increases in large fires and annual area burned, especially in forests ([Climate Change in Colorado, Chapter 4](https://climatechange.colostate.edu/chapters/4_hazards.html)). For Denver, risk includes not only fires at the metropolitan fringe but smoke transported from fires elsewhere and post-fire erosion within source-water watersheds.

Hot, sunny conditions also favor formation of ground-level ozone from precursor pollutants, although emissions, winds, clouds, and atmospheric chemistry determine actual concentrations. The Denver–northern Front Range has a documented ozone problem, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency notes that ozone production is generally more limited under cool, cloudy, rainy, or windy conditions ([EPA, weather-adjusted ozone trends](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/trends-ozone-adjusted-weather-conditions)). Warming can therefore make air-quality management harder without being the sole cause of pollution.

## Living with a moving baseline

Denver’s outlook is not adequately described as simply “hotter and drier.” “Hotter” is well established. “Drier” depends on the variable: annual precipitation may rise, fall, or remain similar, while snowpack, summer soil moisture, and streamflow can decline because warming changes storage and water loss. Extreme rainfall may intensify even as drought risk rises. Snowstorms will remain possible in a warmer climate, yet the fraction of precipitation stored as snow and the timing of melt can change.

Adaptation must consequently address several interacting systems. Denver Water identifies diversified surface and recycled supplies, possible aquifer storage and recovery, improved decision-support tools, watershed monitoring, efficiency, and adaptive planning as parts of its climate program ([Denver Water climate-adaptation program](https://www.denverwater.org/your-water/water-supply-and-planning/climate-change)). Urban heat measures such as shade, trees, reflective materials, efficient buildings, and accessible cooling can reduce exposure, but their performance depends on maintenance, water requirements, neighborhood conditions, and equitable access. Flood planning must consider drainage and impervious surfaces as well as future rainfall. Wildfire resilience extends upstream into the forests and watersheds that supply the city.

Important evidence gaps remain. Station relocation complicates precise estimates of Denver’s century-scale local trend. Climate models resolve broad temperature change better than neighborhood-scale thunderstorms, hail, or tornadoes. Hydrologic projections depend on emissions, climate models, downscaling, watershed models, vegetation, and management decisions. Urban growth and adaptation could either magnify or reduce impacts. Projections should therefore be used as ranges of plausible conditions for stress-testing decisions, not as exact calendars of future weather.

The central lesson is nonetheless clear: Denver’s climate is already moving beyond the late-twentieth-century baseline. Future warming is highly likely; increasing heat extremes are strongly supported; declining snowpack, earlier runoff, greater evaporative demand, and lower streamflow are probable; and annual precipitation, severe thunderstorms, and some forms of flooding remain more uncertain. Sound planning preserves these distinctions instead of forcing every hazard into one simple narrative.
