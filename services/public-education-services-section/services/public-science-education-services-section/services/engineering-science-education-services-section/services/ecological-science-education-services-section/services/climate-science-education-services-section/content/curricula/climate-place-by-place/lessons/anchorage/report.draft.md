# Anchorage: Climate History and Outlook

## A city between ocean and mountains

Anchorage occupies a climatic transition zone in south-central Alaska. The developed “Anchorage Bowl” lies on a glaciated lowland between Cook Inlet and the Chugach Mountains; Knik Arm borders it to the north and west, Turnagain Arm lies to the south, and steep terrain rises immediately to the east. The broader municipality also includes Chugiak–Eagle River, mountain watersheds, and the wetter maritime environment around Girdwood. Consequently, no single weather station describes every neighborhood or elevation. Municipal planning documents characterize the Bowl itself as roughly 100 square miles, bounded by the inlet, military lands, and Chugach State Park, with numerous streams draining toward Cook Inlet ([Municipality of Anchorage coastal-management plan](https://www.muni.org/Departments/OCPD/Planning/Publications/Anc%20Coastal%20Management%20Plan/CZM_Chapters.pdf)).

This geography gives Anchorage a subarctic climate moderated by nearby ocean water but constrained by mountains. Its winters are substantially milder than those of Alaska’s continental interior, while its summers are normally cool. The Chugach Mountains force air to rise, redistribute precipitation, and produce strong local contrasts: the airport, eastern hillside, Eagle River, and Girdwood can experience quite different snowfall, wind, and rainfall during the same storm. Cold air can also collect over low terrain, while downslope or gap winds can cause abrupt warming and drying.

Understanding Anchorage’s climate therefore requires three kinds of evidence:

1. **Weather observations**, which describe individual days, storms, and seasons.
2. **Climate normals and trends**, which summarize typical conditions and long-term change.
3. **Model projections**, which estimate possible futures conditional on greenhouse-gas emissions, natural variability, and model assumptions.

These categories should not be conflated. A record hot day is an observation, not by itself a trend. A 30-year normal is a descriptive baseline, not an unchanging natural standard. A projection is a conditional estimate rather than a prediction of the weather in a particular future year.

## Reading climate records correctly

The principal long-running reference station is Anchorage International Airport, identified by NOAA as station USW00026451 at 61.1689°N, 150.0278°W and about 120 feet above sea level. NOAA’s current standard normals cover 1991–2020. Such normals are averages and related statistics calculated over a standard 30-year period; NOAA recommends the 1991–2020 release for most present-day applications ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

Normals answer questions such as “What is an ordinary January like?” They do not describe all possible Januaries, and they can conceal meaningful variation among neighborhoods. Nor should a difference between two successive normal periods automatically be treated as a pure climate trend. Changes in station exposure, instruments, observing practices, and data-processing methods can matter, although NOAA applies quality controls and adjustments when producing its datasets. For rigorous trend analysis, researchers normally analyze the underlying annual or monthly time series rather than merely subtracting one normal from another.

A temperature anomaly is conventionally expressed as

\[
T' = T_{\text{observed}} - T_{\text{baseline}},
\]

where both quantities refer to the same place, season, and temperature metric. An anomaly of \(+4^\circ\text{F}\), for example, means four Fahrenheit degrees warmer than the specified baseline—not a temperature of \(4^\circ\text{F}\). Temperature *differences* convert according to

\[
\Delta T_{\!^\circ C}=\frac{5}{9}\Delta T_{\!^\circ F}.
\]

Thus, \(4^\circ\text{F}\) of warming is about \(2.2^\circ\text{C}\). Absolute Fahrenheit temperatures require the additional offset: \(T_{\!^\circ C}=(T_{\!^\circ F}-32)\times 5/9\).

## The late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century normal

At Anchorage International Airport, the 1991–2020 annual normal temperature is **37.6°F (3.1°C)**. The normal daily maximum averaged across the year is 44.1°F, while the normal daily minimum is 31.0°F. January is normally the coldest month, with a mean of 16.9°F, a daily maximum of 22.7°F, and a daily minimum of 11.0°F. July is normally the warmest, with a mean of 59.6°F and average high and low temperatures of 66.2°F and 52.9°F ([NOAA’s station-specific 1991–2020 monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00026451)).

These values illustrate the maritime influence. Anchorage is cold enough for a persistent winter snow season, but Cook Inlet helps restrain both the deepest winter cold and summer heat. At the airport, the normal monthly mean remains below freezing from November through March. April’s mean is 37.5°F, while October’s is 36.3°F, placing the spring and autumn “shoulder seasons” close to the freezing threshold. This is important because a modest change in mean temperature near 32°F can cause a disproportionate change in precipitation type, road icing, snowpack, and freeze–thaw frequency.

Normal annual liquid-equivalent precipitation at the airport is about **16 inches**, with late summer and early autumn generally wetter than late winter and spring. Airport totals should not be generalized across the entire municipality: mountain lifting makes the eastern uplands and Girdwood appreciably wetter, while gauges also tend to undercatch wind-driven snow. The municipality’s older 1981–2010 table, for example, reported August and September as its wettest airport months, at 3.3 and 3.0 inches respectively ([2019 Anchorage Climate Action Plan](https://www.muni.org/Departments/Assembly/ResilientAnchorage/Documents/2019%20Anchorage%20Climate%20Action%20Plan_ADOPTED.pdf)).

The 1991–2020 normal snowfall at Anchorage International Airport is **77.9 inches (1.98 m) per year**. December has the largest monthly normal, 18.2 inches, followed by February at 13.4 inches, November at 12.6 inches, January at 12.4 inches, and March at 11.0 inches. Snow can occur during the shoulder seasons—the normals include 5.6 inches in October, 4.0 inches in April, and small amounts in September and May—but essentially none in June through August ([NOAA station normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00026451)).

An important subtlety is that a warmer climate does not guarantee a smooth decline in every season’s snowfall. Cold air can still coexist with moisture-rich storms, producing exceptional snow years even as the long-term snow season shortens. Midwinter snowfall can remain large—or sometimes increase—where temperatures stay cold enough for snow, while autumn and spring precipitation increasingly falls as rain. Snowfall totals, snow-water equivalent, duration of ground cover, and frequency of rain-on-snow events are therefore different variables and may not change in parallel.

## What the historical record establishes

The clearest regional finding is sustained warming. The Fifth National Climate Assessment concludes that detectable warming across Alaska began in the 1970s. From 1971 onward, annual average warming ranged from 2.4°F in Southeast Alaska to 6.2°F in northern Alaska, as much as 2.6 times the rate in the contiguous United States. An attribution study summarized by the assessment found that greenhouse-gas forcing explained about 75% of Alaska’s annual-average warming from 1950 to 2017 ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Chapter 29](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)).

Those figures are statewide or regional, not an Anchorage-specific trend estimate. They establish the broader climatic setting but should not be presented as if every Anchorage neighborhood warmed by the same amount. Local trends are affected by Cook Inlet temperatures, atmospheric circulation, snow cover, urbanization, elevation, and station history. Natural variability associated with the North Pacific can make individual years or decades depart substantially from the longer trend.

The change in Anchorage is nevertheless visible in how rare events are being experienced. On July 4, 2019, Anchorage International Airport reached **90°F (32.2°C)**, breaking the prior all-time station record by 5°F. Anchorage then recorded six consecutive days at or above 80°F, twice the previous record-length run. NOAA attributed the immediate event to an unusually persistent high-pressure ridge and clear skies, while placing it against the background of long-term human-caused warming; the possible contribution of that year’s weak El Niño was considered uncertain ([NOAA Climate.gov event analysis](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/high-temperatures-smash-all-time-records-alaska-early-july-2019)).

This distinction between cause and context is essential. The ridge was the proximate meteorological cause of the heatwave. Long-term warming raised the baseline on which that circulation pattern operated, making extreme warmth more likely or more intense than it would have been in an earlier climate. A single event generally has multiple causal layers.

The 2019 episode also demonstrated compound risk. Hot, dry conditions contributed to extensive wildfire activity elsewhere in Alaska, and smoke reached Anchorage. NOAA reported the city’s first dense-smoke advisory, while residents faced a conflict between opening windows for cooling and closing them to reduce smoke exposure ([NOAA Climate.gov](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/high-temperatures-smash-all-time-records-alaska-early-july-2019)). The national assessment identifies that heat–smoke tradeoff as a broader Alaska health concern because air-conditioning is uncommon and access to filtration or healthcare is unequal ([Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)).

## Why high-latitude warming matters locally

Greenhouse gases reduce Earth’s loss of infrared energy to space, warming the climate system globally. High northern latitudes experience additional feedbacks. When snow and ice retreat earlier, darker land or water absorbs more solar energy. Warmer air holds more water vapor, itself a greenhouse gas. Changes in clouds, atmospheric heat transport, sea ice, and ocean conditions further modify regional warming. Anchorage is south of the Arctic Circle and lacks the extensive sea ice of northern Alaska, but it is connected to this larger high-latitude system through atmospheric circulation, nearby ocean temperatures, snow cover, ecosystems, and the statewide economy.

Winter change is especially consequential. The freezing point creates a physical threshold rather than merely another point on a thermometer. Near 32°F, a small warming can change snow to rain, soften or eliminate snowpack, increase slush, and produce water that refreezes when temperatures fall. A rain-on-snow event may load roofs, create ice layers, obstruct drainage, and leave hard crusts that affect wildlife access to food. Alternating thaw and freeze can damage pavement and complicate walking and driving even if episodes of severe cold become less frequent.

Statewide observations already show that snowfall has declined in autumn and spring while increasing in parts of Alaska during midwinter. The national assessment projects a shorter snowfall season and, under the scenarios it reviewed, a **20%–60% decrease in water stored in snowpack by the 2050s** across most southern and western parts of the state; by the 2080s, the projected decline is **40%–90%** under a higher-emissions future. Colder, high-elevation areas may respond differently, including possible midwinter snowfall increases ([Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)). These are broad regional ranges, not direct forecasts for an airport gauge or a particular Anchorage watershed.

## The outlook: conditional, not predetermined

The 2019 municipal climate plan used downscaled modeling to estimate that Anchorage’s annual mean temperature could be **4°F–5°F warmer by the 2040s** than its historical reference climate. It projected warming in every month, with the greatest increases in winter; average March temperature was expected to approach the freezing point, and normal July daily highs to approach 70°F. It also anticipated fewer January days with mean temperature below freezing, more winter rain-on-snow events, and increasing average precipitation later in the century ([Anchorage Climate Action Plan](https://www.muni.org/Departments/Assembly/ResilientAnchorage/Documents/2019%20Anchorage%20Climate%20Action%20Plan_ADOPTED.pdf)).

These local numbers are useful but require caution. The plan predates the latest national assessment, draws on a particular generation of models and downscaling, and does not always attach an emissions scenario or confidence interval to every public-facing number. Its “2040s” estimate should therefore be read as a planning-scale scenario, not a precise prediction that Anchorage will be exactly 4°F–5°F warmer in a named year.

The newer national assessment provides scenario-explicit statewide projections. For 2081–2100 relative to 1981–2010, Alaska’s annual average surface temperature is projected to rise **8.1°F (4.5°C)** under the intermediate SSP2-4.5 scenario and **14.2°F (7.9°C)** under the very-high SSP5-8.5 scenario. Statewide annual precipitation is projected to rise **20.6%** and **35.8%**, respectively, under those same scenarios ([Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)).

The scenario labels encode different future greenhouse-gas concentrations and socioeconomic pathways. They are not probabilities assigned by the assessment. Differences between them grow with time, so late-century outcomes depend strongly on global emissions. Near-term projections are less separated because climate-system inertia and emissions already released exert substantial influence, while natural variability can dominate the exact sequence of individual years.

More total precipitation does not necessarily mean more usable water or deeper lasting snow. A warmer atmosphere can produce heavier precipitation, but higher temperatures increase evaporation and shift precipitation toward rain. Faster spring melt can leave soils and streams drier later in summer. For Anchorage, the hydrologically important questions include not only annual totals but also storm intensity, precipitation type, snow-water equivalent, melt timing, and the capacity of culverts, storm drains, streams, and roofs to handle water.

Evidence for historical precipitation change is less geographically uniform than evidence for temperature change. Alaska’s annual and seasonal precipitation generally has increased, but the magnitude and statistical significance vary by dataset and region. Since 1957, increases in one- and five-day maximum precipitation are significant in some Alaska climate divisions but not uniformly across the state ([Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)). It would therefore overstate the evidence to assign a precise historical extreme-rainfall trend to Anchorage without a dedicated station or watershed analysis.

## Consequences for Anchorage

### Snow, ice, and transportation

Anchorage’s built environment assumes recurrent snow and freezing conditions. Warming may reduce the duration of continuous snow cover while increasing difficult mixed-phase events—wet snow, freezing rain, slush, and refreezing meltwater. Winter maintenance may shift from moving dry snow toward managing heavier snow, drainage, and ice. Roads and runways may cross the freezing point more often even as the number of intensely cold days falls.

Heavy snow will remain possible. Indeed, a warmer atmosphere can carry more moisture, and individual storms can deposit exceptional snow whenever the vertical temperature profile stays below freezing. Climate adaptation must therefore prepare for both a declining average snow season and occasional severe snow loads.

### Heat and smoke

Historically mild summers have limited the use of household cooling. That becomes a vulnerability when heatwaves coincide with wildfire smoke. Opening windows may reduce indoor heat but admit fine particulate matter; keeping them closed can allow indoor temperatures to rise. The 2019 heatwave and smoke emergency showed that this is already a practical Anchorage problem, not merely a distant forecast ([NOAA Climate.gov](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/high-temperatures-smash-all-time-records-alaska-early-july-2019)).

Risk is not evenly distributed. Older adults, infants, outdoor workers, people with cardiovascular or respiratory illness, and residents without effective filtration or cool indoor space face greater exposure. Adaptation can include clean-air shelters, heat-response plans, better building envelopes, filtered ventilation, shade, and public communication that addresses heat and smoke together.

### Ecosystems and water

Earlier snowmelt, warmer streams, and altered summer flow can affect salmon habitat and urban watersheds. Across Alaska, the national assessment links climate change with reduced summer streamflow, higher water temperature, and lower oxygen in some freshwater systems, although outcomes vary among basins ([Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/downloads/NCA5_Ch29_Alaska.pdf)). Applying that conclusion to a particular Anchorage stream requires local measurements of flow, groundwater input, riparian shade, and barriers.

Permafrost is not as dominant in the Anchorage Bowl as in northern or Interior Alaska. A USGS synthesis specifically reported that Anchorage groundwater supplies were not materially constrained by permafrost, while describing the Pacific coastal region as nearly free of it ([USGS report on groundwater in Alaska’s permafrost regions](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0696/report.pdf)). Broad statements that permafrost thaw is the city’s principal climate hazard would therefore be misleading. Local frozen-ground conditions may still matter in colder uplands or isolated sites, but snow, drainage, slope stability, wildfire, and ecosystem change are more defensible citywide concerns.

### Coast and relative sea level

Global sea level rises as ocean water warms and land ice melts, but coastal impacts depend on **relative sea level**—the height of the sea compared with the moving land. Alaska is unusually complex because earthquake deformation, tectonics, sediment processes, and glacially driven uplift vary sharply by location.

NOAA’s older Anchorage tidal benchmark documentation warns that Cook Inlet has anomalous relative-sea-level behavior because land-level change is substantial, requiring local treatment of tidal datums rather than a simple transfer of national averages ([NOAA Anchorage benchmark sheet](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/benchmarks/benchmarks_old/9455920.html)). The Anchorage gauge record is comparatively short and its estimated trend has large uncertainty. Accordingly, it is not justified to take a global sea-level projection and add it directly to today’s Anchorage shoreline. Local vertical land motion, tides, storm surge, sediment, and updated gauge data must all be included in site-specific planning.

## What is established—and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions are well supported:

- Anchorage has a cold, snow-dominated seasonal climate moderated by Cook Inlet, with strong local variation caused by terrain.
- Alaska has warmed substantially since the 1970s, and human greenhouse-gas emissions explain most of the long-term statewide warming assessed for 1950–2017.
- Anchorage is already capable of heat extremes well outside its late-twentieth-century experience, as demonstrated by the 90°F record in 2019.
- Continued warming is expected under every major emissions pathway, with winter and freezing-threshold effects especially important.
- The snow season is likely to shorten, even though major snowstorms will remain possible.
- Heat, smoke, rain-on-snow, drainage, and freeze–thaw events can interact to create risks larger than those from any single variable.

Other questions remain less certain:

- The exact Anchorage-specific rate of historical precipitation change depends on dataset, period, station, and metric.
- Future snowfall totals are harder to project than temperature because they depend jointly on moisture, storm tracks, and whether air layers remain below freezing.
- Neighborhood-scale outcomes differ across the Bowl, hillside, Eagle River, and Girdwood.
- Relative sea-level change in Cook Inlet cannot be inferred reliably from the global mean alone.
- Decadal natural variability will continue to produce cold winters, cool summers, heavy snow years, and other temporary departures from the long-term warming trajectory.

The practical lesson is not that Anchorage is simply becoming a warmer version of itself. Thresholds and interactions matter. A few degrees of warming can reorganize whether precipitation falls as rain or snow, when meltwater enters streams, how often pavement refreezes, and whether homes can safely be cooled during smoke events. The most useful outlook therefore combines long-term emissions scenarios with local observations, updated engineering thresholds, and explicit uncertainty.
