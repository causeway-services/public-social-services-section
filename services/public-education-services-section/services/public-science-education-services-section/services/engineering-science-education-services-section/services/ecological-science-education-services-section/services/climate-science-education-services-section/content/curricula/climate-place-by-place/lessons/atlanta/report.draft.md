# Atlanta: Climate History and Outlook

## Reading Atlanta’s climate correctly

Atlanta’s climate is best understood at three different scales. First is **weather**: the temperature, rainfall, wind, or storm occurring on a particular day. Second is a **climate normal**, a statistical description of recent conditions, conventionally calculated over 30 years. Third is **climate change**, a persistent shift in the distribution of weather—including averages, extremes, seasonality, and variability—over many decades.

These distinctions matter. A cold winter morning does not disprove long-term warming, and one flood does not by itself establish a trend. Conversely, a 30-year “normal” is not an unchanging natural standard. NOAA updates U.S. normals every decade precisely because the recent climate changes; the current official period is 1991–2020. NOAA also applies quality-control, estimation, and consistency procedures, so station normals are more carefully constructed than simple arithmetic averages of whatever observations happen to be available ([NOAA, “Understanding Climate Normals”](https://www.noaa.gov/explainers/understanding-climate-normals); [NCEI, U.S. Climate Normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

“Atlanta” also needs a geographic definition. Conditions at a rural station, a forested suburban neighborhood, a dense commercial district, and an airport runway can differ on the same afternoon. The principal long-term station used here is NOAA station USW00013874 at Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport, approximately 10 miles south of downtown. It lies at 33.63°N, 84.44°W and about 308 meters—1,010 feet—above sea level. Its daily record begins in 1930, although Atlanta’s broader official climate record incorporates earlier observations from other locations ([NOAA station metadata](https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/cdo-web/datasets/GHCND/stations/GHCND%3AUSW00013874/detail)). Airport observations are indispensable for tracking regional climate, but they should not be mistaken for a complete map of neighborhood exposure.

## The physical setting

Atlanta lies well inland in the Piedmont, the rolling upland between the Appalachian Mountains and the Atlantic Coastal Plain. Georgia’s subtropical latitude and proximity to moisture from the Gulf of Mexico and Atlantic Ocean help produce long, humid summers and generally short, mild winters. Elevation moderates Atlanta’s summer afternoons relative to lower-elevation cities farther south in Georgia, while continental air masses can still bring freezes and occasional snow or ice in winter ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

Several interacting processes shape the city’s weather:

- In summer, strong solar heating and abundant atmospheric moisture favor heat, humidity, thunderstorms, and heavy local downpours.
- In the cool season, passing midlatitude storm systems often provide widespread rainfall.
- Tropical cyclones or their remnants can carry exceptional moisture far inland, even though Atlanta is hundreds of miles from the coast.
- Multiweek rainfall deficits can still cause drought because high temperatures increase evaporation from soil and transpiration from plants.
- Buildings, pavement, waste heat, and reduced vegetation create urban microclimates that can remain warmer than greener surroundings.

This combination explains an important feature of Atlanta’s climate: it is humid and comparatively rainy, yet it remains vulnerable to drought. Annual rainfall alone does not determine water availability. Timing, storm intensity, soil storage, vegetation demand, reservoir operations, and evaporation all matter.

## Atlanta’s recent climate normal

NOAA’s 1991–2020 normal for Hartsfield gives an annual mean temperature of **63.6°F (17.6°C)**, calculated from a normal daily maximum of 73.2°F and minimum of 54.1°F. January is the coldest month, with a mean of 44.8°F, while July is the warmest at 80.9°F. Normal July conditions include an average high of 90.1°F and low of 71.8°F. January averages a high of 54.0°F and low of 35.6°F ([NOAA’s Hartsfield monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataTypes=MLY-TMAX-NORMAL%2CMLY-TMIN-NORMAL%2CMLY-TAVG-NORMAL%2CMLY-PRCP-NORMAL%2CMLY-SNOW-NORMAL&dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&format=pdf&stations=USW00013874)).

The airport normally records about **47 days per year with a maximum of at least 90°F**, but fewer than one day per year at or above 100°F. Nights at or below freezing occur on about 36 days annually; an entire day failing to rise above 32°F occurs only about once per year. These threshold counts reveal more than the annual mean: Atlanta’s characteristic heat is prolonged summer warmth, including warm nights, rather than frequent readings above 100°F ([NOAA’s Hartsfield monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataTypes=MLY-TMAX-NORMAL%2CMLY-TMIN-NORMAL%2CMLY-TAVG-NORMAL%2CMLY-PRCP-NORMAL%2CMLY-SNOW-NORMAL&dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&format=pdf&stations=USW00013874)).

Normal annual precipitation is **50.43 inches (1,281 millimeters)**. Rainfall occurs throughout the year: monthly normals range from 3.28 inches in October to 4.75 inches in July. NOAA estimates about 116 days annually with at least 0.01 inch of precipitation, 34 days with at least half an inch, and 15 days with at least one inch. Normal snowfall is only **2.2 inches per year**, concentrated in winter and subject to large year-to-year variation ([NOAA’s Hartsfield monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataTypes=MLY-TMAX-NORMAL%2CMLY-TMIN-NORMAL%2CMLY-TAVG-NORMAL%2CMLY-PRCP-NORMAL%2CMLY-SNOW-NORMAL&dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&format=pdf&stations=USW00013874)).

A normal is not a promise. NOAA’s precipitation table, for example, gives a median annual total of 44.88 inches but an arithmetic mean of 50.43 inches. The difference reflects precipitation’s skewed distribution: a few very wet periods can pull the mean upward. Thus “below average” rainfall need not mean an exceptionally dry year, and a wet annual total can conceal a damaging dry spell.

## Historical change: a nonlinear warming story

Georgia’s temperature history is not a smooth upward line. Statewide temperatures were high in the 1920s and 1930s, cooled by almost 2°F into the 1960s, and subsequently rose by about 3°F. The 1990s and 2000s returned to approximately 1930s levels, while 2015–2020 established a distinctly higher five-year record. Over the full period since the beginning of the twentieth century, Georgia warmed by about **0.8°F**, roughly half the corresponding warming reported for the contiguous United States ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

The mid-century cooling is sometimes misread as evidence against human-caused warming. It instead illustrates why regional climate is noisier than the global average. Atmospheric circulation, ocean variability, land use, aerosols, and observational sampling can temporarily reinforce or oppose the long-term greenhouse-gas influence. A state or city can therefore experience several cool decades within a longer warming trajectory.

The statewide series should not be treated as an exact Atlanta trend. Atlanta has additional local influences, including urban growth and land-cover change, while a single airport station may be affected by changes in instrumentation, surroundings, or observation practices. NOAA’s longer-term climate products use quality-control and homogenization methods to reduce artificial discontinuities, but no observing system eliminates every uncertainty ([NCEI’s description of U.S. Climate Normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

Individual years demonstrate the range around the trend. In 2020, Atlanta’s annual mean temperature was 64.8°F—2.3°F above the then-operative 1981–2010 normal—and the year ranked sixth warmest in the local record available at that time. Annual precipitation reached 66.99 inches, 17.28 inches above that older normal, making 2020 the sixth-wettest Atlanta year then recorded. Yet June and July were both drier than normal within that very wet year ([National Weather Service 2020 climate summary](https://www.weather.gov/ffc/2020AnnualClimateSummary)). The example cautions against describing an entire year with only one number.

## Extreme heat and the city within the climate

Regional warming and the **urban heat-island effect** are related but distinct. Greenhouse gases alter Earth’s energy balance and raise temperatures across broad regions. An urban heat island arises locally because dark, impervious materials absorb solar energy; buildings restrict airflow and radiative cooling; vehicles and air conditioners release waste heat; and sparse vegetation provides less shade and evaporative cooling. The two processes can compound one another.

At the statewide scale, NOAA estimates that Atlanta historically averaged about seven days per year above 95°F and four “very warm nights” per year, fewer hot afternoons than lower-elevation Macon or Columbus but more warm nights than mountain locations. NOAA identifies urban heat as an added concern because high temperatures and humidity can produce dangerous heat-index conditions ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

Direct measurements show that exposure also varies sharply inside Atlanta. A Georgia Tech study deployed a dense sensor network across the university’s urban campus and examined how built surfaces and vegetation affected near-surface air temperature. During summer 2017, monitored locations experienced between **7 and 56 hot days**, meaning people in the warmer portions encountered almost two additional months of extreme conditions compared with cooler locations. Tree-canopy area was statistically associated with lower maximum urban-heat intensity, although the authors cautioned that heat-island magnitude depends on spatial scale, time of day, and the definition used ([Mallen and colleagues, *Urban Climate*, 2020](https://urbanclimate.gatech.edu/files/2020/09/Mallen-TCN_2020.pdf)).

That study supports a mechanism—shade and vegetation can reduce local heat—but it does not establish a single “Atlanta heat-island temperature.” It covered one campus, one network design, and a limited time period. Surface temperatures measured by satellite, air temperatures measured at pedestrian height, daily maxima, and overnight minima answer different questions. Extrapolating one number to every neighborhood would exceed the evidence.

Heat risk is also more than temperature. A simplified relationship is

\[
\text{risk} \approx \text{hazard} \times \text{exposure} \times \text{vulnerability}.
\]

This is a conceptual framework, not a predictive equation. “Hazard” includes temperature and humidity; “exposure” includes time spent outdoors or in an uncooled building; and “vulnerability” includes age, health, housing quality, income, electricity security, and access to transportation or care. The Fifth National Climate Assessment concludes with very high confidence that increasing heat and other climate-related environmental changes are affecting health in the Southeast, while also noting limited regional data for some outcomes, including pollen, vector-borne disease, and mental health ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)).

## Heavy rain, flooding, and drought

A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapor—approximately 7% more per 1°C of warming under the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship if relative humidity is otherwise similar. That thermodynamic capacity can intensify heavy precipitation when storms have sufficient moisture and lifting. It does **not** imply that every place or season becomes wetter, because circulation, storm tracks, soil conditions, and the spacing between storms also change.

For Atlanta, intense rain creates both riverine and urban flooding. Impervious surfaces route rainfall rapidly into drains and streams, while undersized or obstructed drainage systems can be overwhelmed. Flood outcomes therefore depend on development and infrastructure as well as meteorology. A record illustrates event magnitude without proving a trend: on October 10, 2020, Atlanta measured 4.55 inches of rain, breaking the previous daily record for that date ([National Weather Service 2020 climate summary](https://www.weather.gov/ffc/2020AnnualClimateSummary)).

Drought can coexist with heavier downpours. If more annual rain arrives in short bursts separated by longer dry intervals, much of it may run off rather than replenish soil moisture. Higher temperature also increases atmospheric demand for water, accelerating evaporation when moisture is available. NOAA therefore warns that even unchanged annual precipitation could produce more intense dry spells through greater soil-moisture loss and increased competition for water ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

The Fifth National Climate Assessment finds increasing heavy-rain frequency and lengthening dry spells at some Southeastern locations, while describing longer droughts as apparently becoming more severe but not more frequent. It explicitly assigns greater uncertainty to drought trends than to temperature trends because drought integrates precipitation, heat, soil properties, vegetation, and human water use ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)). This is an important boundary of knowledge: confidence in future warming is much stronger than confidence in the precise timing of Atlanta’s future droughts.

## Severe storms and tropical systems

Atlanta can experience tornadoes, damaging thunderstorm winds, hail, lightning, and flooding from tropical-cyclone remnants. Climate change does not affect all of these hazards in the same way, and a trend in financial losses may reflect population growth and development as well as changes in the hazard itself.

For tropical cyclones, Atlanta’s inland position removes direct storm surge from the city’s risk profile but not wind, tornado, or rainfall risk. The remnants of a storm can interact with fronts and terrain, producing heavy rain well away from the original landfall. Attributing a particular Atlanta storm to climate change requires event-specific analysis. Broad physical expectations—such as greater rainfall potential in a warmer, moister atmosphere—should not be converted into unsupported claims that every hurricane, tornado, or flood was “caused” by warming.

Likewise, evidence for changing tornado frequency is substantially less straightforward than evidence for rising temperature. Tornado records are influenced by changing detection, population, radar coverage, and reporting practices. Responsible planning can account for severe-weather risk without pretending that every component has an equally certain climate trend.

## The outlook: what is robust and what remains uncertain

The strongest conclusion is that Atlanta will become warmer as global greenhouse-gas concentrations rise. NOAA’s state-level model ensemble projects Georgia’s end-of-century annual temperature at **2.3–7.9°F above the 1901–1960 baseline under a lower-emissions pathway**, compared with **6.4–13.3°F under a higher-emissions pathway**. These are ranges across model simulations, not precise Atlanta forecasts. The pathways represent alternative emissions futures; the difference between them becomes especially large later in the century ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

Several consequences follow with relatively high confidence:

1. Average heat will rise, and intense heat events will become more severe.
2. Warm nights will become more common, reducing overnight relief.
3. Freezing conditions and disruptive cold events will generally become less frequent, though they will not disappear.
4. Cooling-energy demand will increase while heating demand declines.
5. Heat stress will grow for outdoor workers, athletes, people without reliable cooling, and residents of hotter neighborhoods.

The Fifth National Climate Assessment describes projections of future heat and extreme-precipitation risk in the Southeast as particularly robust and assigns high confidence to associated economic risks. It also stresses that impacts worsen with each additional increment of global warming rather than appearing only after a single threshold ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)).

Precipitation is less certain. Under the higher-emissions scenario used in NOAA’s state summary, most of Georgia shows a modeled mid-century increase in annual precipitation of **0–5%** relative to the late twentieth century, with 5–10% in a narrow eastern area. The assessment warns, however, that these projected changes are small relative to Georgia’s natural variability and are not statistically robust across most of the state ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)). “Slightly wetter annually” should therefore not be translated into “less drought” or “uniformly more rain.”

A useful way to express the outlook is:

\[
\text{future local climate}
=
\text{forced change}
+
\text{natural variability}
+
\text{local land-use effects}.
\]

The forced component is dominated by greenhouse-gas warming and depends increasingly on future emissions. Natural variability continues to create hot, cool, wet, and dry years around the changing baseline. Local land use can amplify or moderate exposure through tree canopy, pavement, building form, drainage, and waste heat.

## Implications for Atlanta

For public health, the most immediate climate concern is compound heat: high air temperature, humidity, solar exposure, and limited nighttime cooling. Adaptation measures include reliable warning systems, accessible cooling centers, weatherization, efficient air conditioning, shaded transit stops, worker protections, and targeted outreach. Trees can contribute shade and evapotranspirative cooling, but they require space, water, maintenance, suitable species, and long-term protection. The Georgia Tech measurements support canopy as a local cooling intervention while also showing that its performance is context-specific ([Mallen and colleagues, 2020](https://urbanclimate.gatech.edu/files/2020/09/Mallen-TCN_2020.pdf)).

For water and infrastructure, the city must prepare for both extremes of the hydrologic cycle. Detention and retention systems, restored floodplains, permeable surfaces, stream buffers, and upgraded drainage can reduce runoff from heavy rain. Water conservation, leakage control, diversified supplies, drought planning, and soil-moisture retention address dry periods. These strategies are complementary because a city designed only for average rainfall is poorly prepared for greater variability.

For buildings and energy, rising cooling demand can stress household budgets and the electrical system during heat waves. Passive measures—shade, insulation, air sealing, reflective roofs, ventilation, and efficient equipment—can reduce exposure and peak demand. Yet interventions need careful distribution: citywide average improvements can coexist with severe risk in households unable to afford equipment or electricity.

Mitigation and adaptation solve different parts of the problem. Adaptation reduces harm from climate change already occurring or now unavoidable. Mitigation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions and therefore limits how far future warming proceeds. NOAA’s projection ranges show why both matter: Atlanta must prepare for additional heat, but the magnitude of late-century change remains strongly dependent on collective emissions choices ([NOAA’s Georgia State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ga/)).

## What can and cannot be concluded

The evidence supports several established findings. Atlanta has a warm, humid, year-round-rainfall climate; Georgia has warmed overall since the early twentieth century despite pronounced mid-century cooling; recent years have reached unprecedented statewide five-year warmth; urban form produces substantial neighborhood-scale heat differences; and continued greenhouse-gas emissions will bring further warming and more dangerous heat.

Other conclusions remain conditional. The exact temperature of Atlanta in 2050 or 2100 depends on emissions, model response, natural variability, and local development. Annual precipitation may increase modestly, but the change is uncertain relative to natural variability. The timing and location of future floods and droughts cannot be predicted from annual averages. Evidence does not justify assigning every local storm, flood, drought, or hot day exclusively to climate change.

The practical lesson is not uncertainty about whether Atlanta will warm; that result is robust. The uncertainty concerns **how much**, **how quickly**, **how rainfall will be distributed**, and **who will bear the greatest harm**. Those distinctions turn climate information from a collection of alarming numbers into a sound basis for education and planning.
