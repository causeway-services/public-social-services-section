# New York City: Climate History and Outlook

## Introduction

New York City’s climate story is shaped by three interacting realities: a naturally variable midlatitude climate, a dense urban environment, and human-caused global warming. The city already experiences hot, humid summers, cold winters, heavy rain, drought, coastal storms, and large year-to-year swings. Climate change does not replace that variability; it shifts the conditions in which it occurs. Average temperatures rise, very hot conditions become more common, a warmer atmosphere can produce heavier downpours, and rising seas allow ordinary tides and storm surges to reach farther inland.

The central lesson is therefore not that every year will be hotter or wetter than the one before it. It is that the probability distribution is changing. Cold conditions are becoming less common, heat is intensifying, extreme rainfall is becoming more consequential, and the baseline water level around the city is rising. At the same time, the consequences of these physical changes depend on buildings, infrastructure, health, income, access to cooling, and where people live.

The principal sources for this report are observational records maintained by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the peer-reviewed fourth assessment of the New York City Panel on Climate Change (NPCC4), and New York State’s 2024 Climate Impacts Assessment. These sources support a clear conclusion: warming and sea-level rise are established observations, while the scale of future change—especially late in the century—depends on emissions, ice-sheet behavior, adaptation, and other uncertain factors.

## Reading climate evidence correctly

Weather describes atmospheric conditions over hours to days. Climate describes the statistical character of weather over longer periods: averages, extremes, seasonal cycles, and the frequency of events. A cold winter day does not disprove long-term warming, just as one heat wave does not by itself establish a climate trend.

A **climate normal** is a standard multidecadal reference, not a forecast and not an unchanging definition of “normal.” NOAA recommends its 1991–2020 normals for most present-day applications and provides temperature, precipitation, snowfall, degree-day, and freeze statistics for thousands of stations ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)). Researchers studying long-term change may instead use an earlier or fixed baseline so that the reference does not move as the climate warms.

That distinction matters because the figures in this report use several baselines:

- NOAA’s current climate normals cover 1991–2020.
- NPCC4 temperature and precipitation changes are measured relative to 1981–2010.
- NPCC4 sea-level projections are measured relative to 1995–2014.

Projected changes must always be interpreted against the baseline stated by the source. A sea-level projection of 25 inches relative to 1995–2014, for example, does not mean 25 inches above today’s tide on a particular date.

Observations and projections also answer different questions. A weather station records what occurred at one location. A climate model estimates how distributions may change under specified physical assumptions and emissions pathways. Projections are conditional statements—“if the world follows these conditions, this range is plausible”—rather than exact predictions of a particular year.

## Place, water, and the urban environment

New York occupies a coastal estuary where the Hudson River meets the Atlantic Ocean. Its five boroughs contain islands, tidal shorelines, low-lying neighborhoods, hills, wetlands, filled land, and intensely developed surfaces. These differences create meaningful climate variation within the city.

Buildings and pavement absorb solar energy during the day and release stored heat after sunset. Limited vegetation reduces shade and evaporative cooling. Waste heat from vehicles, equipment, and buildings adds energy locally. The resulting **urban heat-island effect** is often most important at night, when densely built neighborhoods cool more slowly than greener surroundings. NPCC4 emphasizes that surface and air temperatures differ across the city with time of day, season, and the characteristics of the underlying built environment ([NPCC4 climate-risk assessment](https://climateassessment.nyc/assessment/nyc-climate-risk-information-2022-observations-and-projections/)).

The city is also exposed to several distinct forms of flooding:

- **Coastal flooding** occurs when tides, storm surge, and waves raise water along the shoreline.
- **Pluvial flooding** occurs when intense rain falls faster than streets, soils, and drainage systems can absorb or convey it.
- **Fluvial flooding** occurs when rivers or streams overflow.
- **Groundwater flooding** can occur when rising groundwater reaches basements, tunnels, or the land surface.

These mechanisms can compound. Rainfall may impede drainage while high coastal water prevents stormwater from discharging efficiently. A power failure during a heat wave may eliminate air conditioning and elevator service precisely when people are least able to cope.

## The historical climate record

Central Park provides New York City’s best-known long observational record, but it is not a complete representation of all five boroughs. Station surroundings, instruments, observation practices, and local land cover can change over time. Comparisons among Central Park, LaGuardia Airport, John F. Kennedy International Airport, and neighborhood measurements help reveal spatial differences.

The NOAA annual series nonetheless shows a clear long-term warming signal amid large variability. Central Park’s annual mean temperature was 56.5°F in 1999, 57.3°F in 2020, and 57.9°F in both 2023 and 2024; colder individual years remain possible, and these examples should not be mistaken for a trend calculation by themselves ([National Weather Service Central Park temperature record](https://www.weather.gov/media/okx/Climate/CentralPark/monthlyannualtemp.pdf)). The stronger evidence comes from systematic trend analysis: NPCC4 found that, since 1995, daily minimum temperatures increased by about 0.73°F per decade at Central Park, 0.74°F at LaGuardia, and 0.68°F at JFK, while daily maximum temperatures increased by approximately 0.55°F, 0.74°F, and 0.74°F per decade, respectively ([NPCC4 extreme-temperature analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11438572/)).

New York State’s longer quality-controlled record supplies regional context. From 1901 through 2022, statewide annual mean temperature increased by approximately 0.21°F per decade, compared with 0.17°F per decade across the contiguous United States. The state assessment found statistically significant warming in all seasons and a winter trend roughly twice as steep as the trends in other seasons ([New York State Climate Impacts Assessment](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15240)). Statewide numbers are not interchangeable with city-specific trends, but they demonstrate that New York City’s warming belongs to a broader regional pattern.

The cold side of the distribution is changing as well. NPCC4 reports that the annual number of days with minimum temperatures below freezing has declined steadily since 1900 ([NPCC4 climate-risk assessment](https://climateassessment.nyc/assessment/nyc-climate-risk-information-2022-observations-and-projections/)). This does not eliminate severe winter weather. It means that freezing conditions occur within an increasingly warm background climate.

Precipitation is more variable than temperature. Central Park recorded 38.82 inches in 1985, 80.56 inches in 1983, 65.55 inches in 2018, and 59.73 inches in 2021, illustrating the enormous year-to-year range in annual totals ([National Weather Service Central Park precipitation record](https://www.weather.gov/media/okx/Climate/CentralPark/monthlyannualprecip.pdf)). Such variability makes short periods unreliable guides to long-term change. It also explains why projected changes of several percent in annual precipitation can coexist with much larger differences among individual years.

## Why human-caused warming changes local hazards

Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation, reducing the rate at which Earth loses heat to space. Human activities—especially fossil-fuel combustion, along with industrial processes, land-use change, and agriculture—have raised greenhouse-gas concentrations. New York State’s assessment summarizes the broader attribution evidence: the planet is about 2°F, or 1.1°C, warmer than in the late nineteenth century, and known natural processes cannot account for the observed long-term warming ([New York State Climate Impacts Assessment](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15240)).

Several mechanisms translate global warming into New York City risk:

1. **Higher background temperature shifts heat extremes.** A hot spell superimposed on a warmer average more readily crosses health and infrastructure thresholds.

2. **Warm air can hold more water vapor.** When moisture and atmospheric dynamics align, storms can produce heavier precipitation. This does not imply that every storm intensifies or that rain falls more frequently in every season.

3. **Ocean warming and melting land ice raise global sea level.** Local relative sea level also reflects land movement, ocean circulation, gravity, and regional water-density changes.

4. **Urban form modifies exposure.** Dark surfaces, low tree cover, limited ventilation, building materials, and indoor conditions can magnify heat.

5. **Social conditions modify vulnerability.** Health, age, income, housing quality, occupation, energy security, and access to cooling affect the harm caused by the same outdoor weather.

NPCC4 summarizes risk as

\[
R = H \times E \times V,
\]

where \(H\) is hazard, \(E\) is exposure, and \(V\) is vulnerability ([NPCC4 climate-risk information](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15116)). This is a conceptual relationship, not a literal instruction to multiply three universally measured numbers. Its value lies in showing that adaptation can reduce risk by limiting exposure or vulnerability even when the physical hazard grows.

## Heat: the clearest emerging hazard

NPCC4 projects increases in New York City’s annual mean temperature, relative to 1981–2010, of:

| Period | Projected increase |
|---|---:|
| 2030s | 2.7–3.9°F |
| 2050s | 4.0–6.0°F |
| 2080s | 5.6–9.8°F |

These are ensemble ranges, not annual forecasts, and the widening range toward the 2080s reflects both model differences and divergent future forcing. The same assessment projects that hot days and nights will increase by 15–52 days per year by the 2050s, while heat waves become two to four times as frequent and up to 50% longer than in the reference climate ([NPCC4 extreme-temperature analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11438572/)).

Humidity makes these temperatures more dangerous because evaporation of sweat becomes less effective as the surrounding air grows more humid. The heat index combines temperature and humidity to approximate human-perceived heat under specified conditions. New York historically experienced about 38 days per year with a heat index above 85°F and six above 95°F during the NPCC4 baseline; the median projection for days above 95°F reaches 37 per year by the 2050s ([NPCC4 extreme-temperature analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11438572/)).

Heat is already a major health burden. The New York City health assessment estimated that hot weather—defined in that analysis as days reaching at least 82°F—causes about 352 deaths per year on average. That estimate combines roughly seven annually reported heat-stress deaths during 2012–2021 with approximately 345 annual **heat-exacerbated deaths** during 2016–2020, in which heat worsened cardiovascular, respiratory, or other underlying conditions ([NPCC4 health assessment](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15115)). Death certificates substantially undercount this broader burden, which is why epidemiological estimates exceed directly coded heat deaths.

Outdoor measurements alone do not capture residential exposure. In New York homes without air conditioning, indoor temperature can be up to 10°F higher than outdoors, and excess heat may persist for as long as three days after outdoor conditions cool. Although more than 90% of city households have air conditioning, access varies by more than fourfold among neighborhoods; lower-income and Black New Yorkers are less likely to have home cooling ([NPCC4 health assessment](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15115)). These disparities reflect energy costs, housing conditions, tree cover, neighborhood temperature, and the cumulative effects of structural inequality.

Adaptation therefore requires more than weather alerts. Effective measures include affordable residential cooling, reliable electricity, weatherization that limits unwanted heat gain, shaded streets, trees where they can survive and deliver benefits, cool roofs, accessible cooling spaces, workplace protections, and outreach through trusted community and health networks. Mechanical cooling can save lives, but it also raises electricity demand and can emit waste heat; its climate benefit depends partly on building efficiency and the carbon intensity of electricity.

## Rainfall, drainage, and drought

NPCC4 projects annual precipitation increases of approximately 2–7% by the 2030s, 4–11% by the 2050s, and 7–17% by the 2080s relative to 1981–2010. The panel cautions that these changes are small compared with year-to-year variability, while models generally indicate larger relative increases in extreme rainfall frequency ([NPCC4 climate-risk assessment](https://climateassessment.nyc/assessment/nyc-climate-risk-information-2022-observations-and-projections/)).

A warmer, wetter average does not eliminate drought. Higher temperatures increase evaporative demand, precipitation can arrive in fewer and heavier events, and long dry intervals may occur between storms. Water-supply risk also depends on consumption, reservoir operations, snowpack, watershed conditions, and seasonal timing rather than annual rainfall alone.

The metropolitan drought of record occurred in the early-to-mid-1960s and remains the central stress test for regional reservoir operations. Tree-ring reconstructions suggest a moderate probability of droughts with similar characteristics recurring, while modeling indicates that summer precipitation may remain similar or decline slightly and short-duration droughts may become more frequent ([NPCC4 climate-risk information](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15116)). NPCC4 explicitly identifies a knowledge gap: drought vulnerability should be assessed using changing demand across sectors as well as climatic supply.

Extreme rainfall presents a different challenge. On September 1, 2021, remnants of Hurricane Ida produced 3.15 inches of rain at Central Park in one hour, between 8:51 and 9:51 p.m. Eastern time. Rainfall rates of three to five inches per hour caused deadly flash and urban flooding across the metropolitan area and severely disrupted New York City transit ([NOAA Hurricane Ida Service Assessment](https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/Hurricane_Ida_Service_Assessment.pdf)). This event illustrates the importance of rainfall intensity: the same total spread over a day would create a different drainage problem.

No projection can specify exactly when another Ida-like event will occur. Nor can citywide annual precipitation projections be translated directly into a block-level flood depth. Local outcomes depend on storm tracks, drainage capacity, topography, impervious surfaces, clogged inlets, sewer design, and the presence of basements. NPCC4 notes that urban pluvial modeling remains less mature than traditional coastal or river-flood modeling, though New York City released its first citywide extreme-rainfall flood projections in 2021 ([NPCC4 climate-risk information](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15116)).

## Sea-level rise and coastal flooding

Relative sea level near New York City has risen because global ocean volume is increasing while regional and local processes modify the result. New York State reports that the long-term rise at New York City averaged about 0.11 inch per year from 1865 to 2022 and accelerated to approximately 0.16 inch per year over the most recent 40 years analyzed ([New York State observed and projected climate report](https://dec.ny.gov/sites/default/files/2026-01/observedprojectedclimatechangereport.pdf)).

NPCC4’s city projections, relative to 1995–2014, are:

| Period | 10th percentile | 25th percentile | 75th percentile | 90th percentile |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2030s | 6 in | 7 in | 11 in | 13 in |
| 2050s | 12 in | 14 in | 19 in | 23 in |
| 2080s | 21 in | 25 in | 39 in | 45 in |
| 2100 | 25 in | 30 in | 50 in | 65 in |
| 2150 | 38 in | 47 in | 89 in | 177 in |

The table combines three scenarios with equal weights. Its percentiles describe the projection distribution used by NPCC4; they are not simple confidence limits and should not be interpreted as precise odds independent of the chosen scenarios and models ([NPCC4 climate-risk information](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nyas.15116)).

The late-century and 2150 ranges are especially broad because future emissions and ice-sheet behavior become increasingly important. The very high 2150 value represents a tail risk rather than the most likely outcome. Low probability does not make such an outcome irrelevant where infrastructure must last many decades and failure would be catastrophic.

Sea-level rise does not need to create a new storm to increase damage. It raises the platform on which tides, waves, and storm surge operate. Water levels that were once reached only during unusual storms can therefore be reached more often, and a storm of the same meteorological intensity can flood a larger area.

Hurricane Sandy demonstrates the city’s exposure, though a single storm cannot be attributed wholly to climate change. Sandy struck on October 29, 2012, killed 44 city residents, damaged more than 69,000 residential units, and caused an estimated $19 billion in damage and lost economic activity ([New York City Sandy recovery record](https://www.nyc.gov/site/cdbgdr/hurricane-sandy/hurricane-sandy.page)). Sea-level rise increases the likelihood that future coastal storms will begin from a higher water baseline.

## What is established, and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions are strongly supported:

- New York City and the surrounding region have warmed.
- Freezing days have declined while hot days and nights are increasing.
- Relative sea level is rising and its rate has accelerated over recent decades.
- Heavy rainfall already exceeds urban drainage capacity during some storms.
- Heat mortality and flood damage depend strongly on housing, infrastructure, health, and inequality.

Other questions retain substantial uncertainty:

- Late-century warming depends heavily on global emissions.
- High-end sea-level rise depends on difficult-to-model ice-sheet processes.
- Individual hurricane tracks and landfalls cannot be projected decades in advance.
- Neighborhood flood depths require more detailed information than citywide climate projections provide.
- Annual rainfall projections are less certain than temperature projections.
- Adaptation, migration, building replacement, energy systems, and population change will alter future exposure and vulnerability.

These uncertainties do not imply ignorance. Near-term warming and sea-level rise occur across a wide range of scenarios, while uncertainty grows farther into the future. A practical response is **adaptive planning**: make robust investments now, monitor observations, establish decision points, and preserve the ability to strengthen defenses as new evidence emerges.

## Outlook

New York City’s future climate will remain recognizably variable, but it will operate from a warmer and higher-water baseline. The most defensible expectation is not a smooth sequence of worsening years. It is a growing likelihood of severe heat, warmer nights, intense rainfall, tidal flooding, and damaging combinations of hazards.

The city’s outcome is not determined by climate physics alone. Emissions choices influence the eventual magnitude of warming and sea-level rise. Local choices determine who has cooling, which buildings can tolerate power loss, whether drainage systems handle intense rain, where new development occurs, and whether coastal protection transfers risk elsewhere. The physical hazard may be regional or global, but exposure and vulnerability are built—and can be changed—place by place.
