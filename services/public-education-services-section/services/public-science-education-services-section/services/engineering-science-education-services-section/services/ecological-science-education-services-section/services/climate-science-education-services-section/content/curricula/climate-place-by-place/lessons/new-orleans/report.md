# New Orleans: Climate History and Outlook

## Introduction: climate in a city between water and water

New Orleans has a humid, subtropical climate shaped by the Gulf of Mexico, the Mississippi River, Lake Pontchartrain, and the city’s low-lying deltaic setting. Summers are long, hot, humid, and rainy; winters are comparatively mild; and tropical cyclones can produce destructive wind, rainfall, and storm surge. Yet those familiar descriptions capture only part of the city’s climate risk. New Orleans is also a heavily engineered landscape where levees limit natural drainage, pumps move rainfall out of populated basins, and floodwalls and surge barriers reduce—but cannot eliminate—coastal flooding.

The city’s future therefore depends on interactions among several processes:

- Global warming raises temperatures and intensifies some forms of extreme precipitation.
- Ocean warming and melting land ice raise global sea level.
- Local land subsidence adds to the rise of water relative to the ground.
- Coastal wetland loss changes the landscape through which storm surge travels.
- Tropical-cyclone rainfall and the strongest hurricane intensities are expected to increase, although projections of storm frequency and local landfalls remain uncertain.
- Infrastructure condition, land use, social inequality, evacuation capacity, and adaptation choices determine how physical hazards become human consequences.

This report distinguishes three kinds of evidence. **Observations** describe what instruments have recorded. **Climate projections** are conditional model results based on future warming or emissions. **Planning scenarios** are deliberately chosen possibilities used to test decisions; they are not forecasts of what will occur in a particular year.

## Reading the evidence: weather, climate, normals, and projections

Weather is the state of the atmosphere over hours to days. Climate describes statistical patterns—averages, variability, seasonality, and extremes—over much longer periods. The distinction matters because an individual freeze, hurricane, or exceptionally wet summer cannot by itself establish a climate trend.

A **climate normal** is a standardized multidecadal average used as a reference for present-day conditions. NOAA’s current U.S. normals cover 1991–2020 and include data from more than 15,000 precipitation stations and more than 7,300 temperature stations; they are intended to describe a typical recent climate, not to predict each year and not, by themselves, to explain why climate changed ([NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, “U.S. Climate Normals”](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

A **projection** asks what climate could be like under stated assumptions about greenhouse gases, global warming, models, and natural variability. It is conditional: “if these assumptions hold, this range of outcomes is plausible.” A **forecast** instead attempts to predict the most likely conditions at a specified time. Louisiana’s coastal-planning documentation explicitly warns that its lower and higher scenarios are possibilities for stress-testing decisions, not forecasts of the coast in a named future year ([Louisiana Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority, Master Plan Data Access Portal](https://mpdap.coastal.la.gov/dataset/)).

Finally, climate numbers are sensitive to scale. An airport station, a city neighborhood, the Louisiana coast, and the entire Southeast are not interchangeable. Urban surfaces, proximity to open water, station location, elevation, and observation practices can all affect measured conditions. Regional projections are valuable for understanding direction and mechanism, but they should not be presented as precise neighborhood forecasts.

## The recent baseline climate

At Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport, NOAA’s 1991–2020 normals give an annual average high of **78.5°F**, average low of **62.5°F**, daily mean of **70.5°F**, and annual precipitation of **63.35 inches**. January averages are 62.5°F for the high and 46.1°F for the low, while July and August average highs are approximately 91°F and lows about 76.5°F. Rainfall occurs year-round, with especially wet normals in June, July, and August ([National Weather Service New Orleans/Baton Rouge, 1991–2020 normals](https://www.weather.gov/lix/newnormals)).

Those averages conceal meaningful local variation. For 1991–2020, New Orleans Lakefront Airport had an annual mean temperature of **70.8°F** and precipitation of **60.12 inches**, compared with 70.5°F and 63.35 inches at the international airport. The Lakefront station’s normal July and August lows were 78.2°F and 78.6°F, respectively, illustrating how warm nights can be near the urbanized lakeshore ([National Weather Service New Orleans/Baton Rouge](https://www.weather.gov/lix/newnormals)). Differences between stations should not automatically be interpreted as long-term climate change; they also reflect location and exposure.

The seasonal pattern results from basic energy and moisture relationships. Strong summer solar heating, warm Gulf waters, and abundant atmospheric moisture favor high humidity and frequent convection. Humidity reduces the efficiency with which sweating cools the body, so physiological heat stress can be much greater than air temperature alone suggests. Warm, humid nights are particularly consequential because they reduce nighttime relief for people without reliable air conditioning. The Fifth National Climate Assessment concludes with high confidence that extreme heat in the Southeast is increasing and that projected heat extremes disproportionately threaten urban and energy-burdened communities ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)).

Winter is mild on average, but “mild” does not mean frost-free. Cold-air outbreaks can still reach the Gulf Coast, and rare freezes can disrupt water systems, transportation, vegetation, and buildings designed for warmth rather than prolonged cold. Climate change is reducing the intensity and frequency of many cold-season events across the Southeast, but declining frequency does not imply zero future occurrence ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)).

## What the historical record shows—and what it does not

The National Weather Service’s comparison of consecutive 30-year normals provides a compact picture of recent change. At the international airport, the annual normal for daily mean temperature increased from **69.7°F in 1981–2010 to 70.5°F in 1991–2020**. The average annual low increased from 61.1°F to 62.5°F, while the average high rose from 78.2°F to 78.5°F. Normal annual precipitation increased from 62.45 to 63.35 inches ([National Weather Service New Orleans/Baton Rouge](https://www.weather.gov/lix/newnormals)).

These comparisons are consistent with warming, especially in minimum temperatures, but they are not a clean independent trend calculation. The two normal periods overlap for twenty years; only the 1980s leave one window while the 2010s enter the other. Station moves, instruments, land-cover changes, and natural variability may also influence a single-station comparison. A rigorous trend analysis would use the complete quality-controlled annual series, account for discontinuities, and quantify uncertainty.

Regional evidence strengthens the broader conclusion. The Fifth National Climate Assessment finds increasing climate stress from extreme heat, extreme precipitation, sea-level change, and tropical cyclones across the Southeast, while disruptive cold-season events generally decline ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)). That assessment synthesizes multiple observational datasets and peer-reviewed studies rather than relying on a single New Orleans station.

The precipitation record requires particular care. New Orleans is naturally wet and variable, so a change in annual average rainfall need not track changes in short, intense downpours. A climate can experience longer dry spells and more extreme rain events at the same time. The national assessment projects increases in some seasonal, annual, and extreme precipitation measures across the Southeast, with much of the increase driven by very heavy events such as days receiving at least three inches in 24 hours. The magnitude and exact location of change remain model- and warming-dependent ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter PDF](https://nca5.climate.us/downloads/NCA5_Ch22_Southeast.pdf)).

The physical mechanism is well established: warmer air can hold more water vapor, providing additional moisture for intense rainfall when storms have the necessary lift and circulation. That thermodynamic tendency does not mean every storm becomes wetter, because rainfall also depends on storm tracks, atmospheric stability, wind patterns, and moisture transport. Consequently, confidence is stronger in the general increase of heavy-rain potential than in the timing of a particular New Orleans event.

## Why water creates several different flood hazards

“Flooding” in New Orleans is not one phenomenon. At least four mechanisms should be separated.

### Pluvial or rainfall flooding

Intense rain can accumulate faster than streets, catch basins, canals, and pumps can convey it away. The Sewerage and Water Board reports that New Orleans’ drainage network includes more than 68,000 catch basins, 1,500 miles of lateral underground pipes, 200 miles of canals, and 120 pumps in 24 drainage-pump stations. Its traditional operating benchmark is about one inch of rain in the first hour and roughly one-half inch per hour thereafter; storms exceeding an inch per hour are likely to outpace the system temporarily ([Sewerage & Water Board of New Orleans, “Drainage System Facts”](https://www.swbno.org/Stormwater/Facts)).

This benchmark is not a promise that every neighborhood will drain identically. Water must first reach the pumps by gravity through surface and subsurface conveyances. Clogged inlets, local topography, canal levels, pump availability, and power supply can change outcomes. Moreover, an average hourly amount may hide brief bursts with much higher intensity.

### River flooding

The Mississippi River can rise because of precipitation and snowmelt far upstream, making river flood risk partly independent of local weather. River levees separate much of the city from the river’s natural floodplain. This greatly reduces frequent river flooding but also means residents experience a managed hydraulic landscape rather than an unaltered delta.

### Storm surge

A hurricane can push Gulf water toward the coast, through sounds and waterways, and toward Lake Pontchartrain and the city’s perimeter. Surge depends on storm size, intensity, track, forward speed, pressure, coastal bathymetry, and local topography. Wind category alone is therefore an inadequate measure of surge risk. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers emphasizes that different combinations of hurricane size, intensity, and track can generate the same annual-exceedance level of surge ([USACE, “100-Year Level of Risk Reduction”](https://www.mvn.usace.army.mil/Missions/HSDRRS/Risk-Reduction-Plan/100-Year-Level-Protection/)).

### Compound flooding

Hazards can coincide. High coastal water may impede drainage at the same time that extreme rainfall produces runoff. Surge, waves, river levels, rain, and infrastructure outages can interact nonlinearly, making total consequences greater than consideration of any one hazard alone. The Fifth National Climate Assessment identifies compound coastal flooding as an increasing concern along the Atlantic and Gulf Coasts as rising sea levels elevate the baseline on which storms operate ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Climate Trends chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/downloads/NCA5_Ch2_Climate-Trends.pdf)).

## Katrina as a historical event, not a template for every future storm

Hurricane Katrina made Louisiana landfall on August 29, 2005. The National Hurricane Center’s post-storm analysis found surge of approximately **15–19 feet in eastern New Orleans, St. Bernard Parish, and Plaquemines Parish**, and **10–14 feet in western New Orleans along southern Lake Pontchartrain**. Levees and floodwalls were overtopped or breached, and about 80% of New Orleans flooded, with depths reaching roughly 20 feet in some locations ([National Hurricane Center, *Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Katrina*](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL122005_Katrina.pdf)).

Katrina is essential climate history, but several distinctions matter. It was one storm, not a statistical projection. Its disaster resulted from the interaction of a powerful cyclone, surge pathways, levee and floodwall failures, exposure, evacuation constraints, and social vulnerability. Climate change did not create those institutional and engineering conditions. Conversely, the existence of non-climatic contributors does not make climate irrelevant: higher background sea level and potentially wetter or more intense storms can load the same human systems more severely.

It is also misleading to ask only whether climate change will produce “more Katrinas.” Hurricanes differ in size, track, speed, rainfall, and surge. The stronger scientifically supported statements are probabilistic. With additional warming, North Atlantic hurricanes are expected to produce more rainfall, a larger fraction are expected to reach at least Category 4 intensity, and rapid intensification is expected to become more common; sea-level rise will worsen surge inundation when storms occur. The expected change in total Atlantic hurricane number and the frequency of landfall at a particular place remain more uncertain ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter PDF](https://nca5.climate.us/downloads/NCA5_Ch22_Southeast.pdf)).

## Relative sea-level rise: ocean rise plus sinking land

For New Orleans, the relevant quantity is **relative sea level**: the height of the water relative to the land and built structures. A simplified expression is

\[
\Delta R \approx \Delta S - \Delta L,
\]

where \(\Delta R\) is relative sea-level change, \(\Delta S\) is ocean-surface change, and \(\Delta L\) is land-elevation change. If the ocean rises and the land sinks, \(\Delta L\) is negative, so the two contributions add.

Global sea level rises primarily because seawater expands as it warms and because land ice loses mass. Local departures from the global average arise from vertical land motion, ocean circulation, gravitational effects of ice loss, and regional atmospheric and oceanographic variability. Tide gauges measure the combined result at their locations, not ocean change alone. NOAA explicitly notes that tide-gauge water levels incorporate both ocean fluctuations and vertical motion of the land ([NOAA Tides & Currents, Louisiana sea-level trends](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/sltrends/sltrends_states.shtml?region=la)).

Subsidence in coastal Louisiana has multiple causes and varies sharply by location. Delta sediments compact naturally; groundwater or hydrocarbon withdrawal can contribute in some settings; drainage and oxidation of organic soils can lower land; and large structures settle. Because rates vary across short distances and through time, a single “New Orleans subsidence rate” can be misleading. Louisiana’s 2023 Coastal Master Plan used spatially varying, data-informed shallow and deep subsidence assumptions and acknowledged that local variation can exceed regional values ([Louisiana CPRA, *Determining Subsidence Rates for Use in Predictive Modeling*](https://coastal.la.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Subsidence-Rates_Mar2021.pdf)).

The Fifth National Climate Assessment reports that tide gauges across the Southeast recorded about **six inches of relative sea-level rise from 1970 to 2020**, with substantial geographic variation. Across the Southeast, its low-to-high scenarios project an average **16–23 inches of rise by 2050 relative to 2000**, and **2.2–7.3 feet by 2100**. These are regional scenario ranges, not precise forecasts for a New Orleans address; local land motion and future global emissions can shift actual outcomes ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter PDF](https://nca5.climate.us/downloads/NCA5_Ch22_Southeast.pdf)).

Even a modest rise matters because it raises the starting level for tides and surge. A water level formerly reached only during a rare storm can be exceeded more frequently once the baseline rises. Higher receiving-water levels can also complicate gravity drainage and increase the need for pumping.

## Coastal land loss and the changing buffer

New Orleans’ risk is tied to the broader Louisiana coast. The state estimates that coastal Louisiana lost more than **2,000 square miles of land between 1932 and 2015**. It attributes this change to multiple interacting causes, including Mississippi River levees that reduce sediment delivery to wetlands, navigation and oil-and-gas canals, and climate change ([Louisiana CPRA, 2023 Coastal Master Plan Data Viewer](https://mpdv.coastal.la.gov/)).

Wetlands are not a simple wall that removes hurricane risk. Their surge-reduction effect varies with water depth, vegetation, storm characteristics, landscape continuity, and the location of channels and levees. Nevertheless, wetland deterioration changes friction, wave behavior, habitats, and the geography separating communities from open water. Coastal restoration should therefore be understood as one layer in a risk-reduction portfolio, not a substitute for structural protection, evacuation, or building-level measures.

Louisiana’s planning models illustrate the scale and uncertainty of future change. Without implementation of the 2023 plan, the state’s viewer reports modeled losses of about **1,100 square miles under its lower environmental scenario and 3,000 square miles under its higher scenario over 50 years**. With the plan, modeled avoided losses are approximately 310 and 230 square miles, respectively ([Louisiana CPRA, 2023 Coastal Master Plan Data Viewer](https://mpdv.coastal.la.gov/)). These figures are scenario-model results, not observed future facts.

The underlying scenarios assume **0.5 meter of eustatic sea-level rise over 50 years in the lower case and 0.77 meter in the higher case**, together with different subsidence and tropical-storm-intensity assumptions. The modeled rate of sea-level change is not linear ([Louisiana CPRA, *Planning Tool Methods & Results*](https://coastal.la.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/G1_PlanningToolMethodsResults_Mar2023_v4.pdf)). Results therefore inherit uncertainties in sea level, subsidence, storm behavior, sediment, vegetation, project performance, and future maintenance.

## Protection, probability, and residual risk

After Katrina, federal and local partners constructed or upgraded the Greater New Orleans Hurricane and Storm Damage Risk Reduction System. The system includes levees, floodwalls, gates, armoring, and major pump and closure structures. The Corps describes it as providing a **100-year level of storm-surge risk reduction**, meaning reduced risk from surge with a **1% probability of occurrence or exceedance in any year** ([USACE, “100-Year Level of Risk Reduction”](https://www.mvn.usace.army.mil/Missions/HSDRRS/Risk-Reduction-Plan/100-Year-Level-Protection/)).

“100-year” does not mean “once every 100 years,” nor does it guarantee safety for a century. If annual events were independent and the probability stayed fixed at \(p=0.01\), the chance of at least one exceedance in \(n\) years would be

\[
P(\text{one or more exceedances})=1-(1-p)^n.
\]

Over 50 years, that is \(1-0.99^{50}\), or about **39.5%**. Moreover, the probability may not remain fixed as relative sea level, storms, land elevation, and infrastructure condition change.

The system is therefore accurately described as risk reduction, not absolute protection. Its design considered sea-level rise, settlement, subsidence, and possible storm changes, but continued performance depends on inspection, maintenance, upgrades, and the evolving hazard ([USACE, “100-Year Level of Risk Reduction”](https://www.mvn.usace.army.mil/Missions/HSDRRS/Risk-Reduction-Plan/100-Year-Level-Protection/)). Structural defenses can reduce frequent and severe losses while leaving residual risks from exceedance, failure, rainfall inside protected basins, and events outside design assumptions.

## Outlook for heat, rain, hurricanes, and the coast

The most robust outlook is continued warming. Average temperatures will rise with global warming, hot days and humid-heat episodes will become more dangerous, and warm nights will become more common. The exact number of future days above a chosen temperature threshold depends on the emissions pathway, time period, climate model, and local urban conditions. Neighborhood-scale heat can also vary with tree canopy, pavement, building density, housing quality, and access to cooling.

Heavy-rain risk is also expected to increase, although annual rainfall totals are less certain than the thermodynamic tendency toward more intense downpours. This distinction is vital for New Orleans: drainage failure is governed by rainfall intensity and duration over particular basins, not simply annual accumulation. Infrastructure designed using historical intensity-frequency-duration relationships may become less reliable if those relationships shift. The national assessment notes that many existing Southeast design estimates do not include future precipitation projections ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter PDF](https://nca5.climate.us/downloads/NCA5_Ch22_Southeast.pdf)).

For hurricanes, confidence is high that warmer oceans and atmospheres can support heavier tropical-cyclone rainfall and that sea-level rise will increase coastal inundation for an otherwise comparable storm. Evidence also supports an increasing share of the strongest storms and greater rapid-intensification risk. Confidence is lower in future total storm counts, individual tracks, and New Orleans landfall frequency. Preparedness must therefore accommodate a wide range of storms rather than assume a simple increase in every hurricane metric.

Relative sea-level rise is effectively certain to continue for decades, but its magnitude late in the century depends strongly on global emissions, ice-sheet response, and local land motion. For practical planning, this means flexible pathways are preferable to a single precise endpoint: monitor change, preserve options, and design measures that can be elevated, expanded, or replaced.

## Adaptation as a layered system

No single intervention resolves New Orleans’ climate risk. A defensible strategy combines:

1. **Global mitigation**, which limits the long-term magnitude of warming and sea-level rise.
2. **Regional coastal restoration**, including sediment and wetland projects where evidence indicates durable benefits.
3. **Surge defenses**, maintained and reassessed as relative sea level and storm statistics evolve.
4. **Urban water management**, including pumps, power reliability, catch-basin maintenance, storage, green infrastructure, and surfaces that retain or slow runoff.
5. **Heat adaptation**, such as shade, trees suited to future conditions, reflective materials, efficient buildings, cooling access, and worker protections.
6. **Building and neighborhood measures**, including elevation, floodproofing, insurance, emergency planning, and strategic decisions about where repeated rebuilding remains sustainable.
7. **Social capacity**, including accessible warnings, evacuation assistance, medical continuity, tenant protection, and resources for households unable to finance adaptation independently.

Equity is not separate from climate science. The same physical event produces unequal harm when residents differ in housing quality, health, mobility, insurance, savings, access to vehicles, and ability to evacuate or recover. The Fifth National Climate Assessment finds that adaptation resources in the Southeast are often concentrated in wealthier coastal and metropolitan areas, while under-resourced communities face greater constraints ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southeast chapter](https://nca5.climate.us/chapter/22)). Evaluating adaptation therefore requires asking not only how much expected damage is avoided, but who receives protection, who bears costs, and what happens if infrastructure is exceeded.

## What can be concluded—and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions are strongly supported. New Orleans already has a hot, humid, wet climate; recent normals are warmer than the preceding overlapping normal period; extreme heat is increasing regionally; heavy-rain potential is growing; relative sea level is rising; and subsidence compounds coastal exposure. Higher sea levels will amplify future surge and compound-flood hazards. Strong hurricanes are expected to become wetter and, as a fraction of all tropical cyclones, more common under additional warming.

Important uncertainties remain. Available regional projections do not determine the exact temperature, rainfall intensity, or flood depth for every neighborhood. Future emissions remain a societal choice. Ice-sheet behavior introduces widening late-century sea-level uncertainty. Subsidence is spatially variable. Hurricane landfall timing and tracks cannot be projected for particular years. Coastal and flood models depend on assumptions about ecological response, structural reliability, maintenance, population, and property distribution.

The correct educational conclusion is neither that New Orleans is inevitably lost nor that its defenses make climate change manageable without further action. The evidence supports a more demanding position: the city’s risk is already engineered and climate-sensitive; climate change will raise the load on that system; and outcomes will depend substantially on emissions, maintenance, adaptation, public institutions, and the distribution of protective resources.
