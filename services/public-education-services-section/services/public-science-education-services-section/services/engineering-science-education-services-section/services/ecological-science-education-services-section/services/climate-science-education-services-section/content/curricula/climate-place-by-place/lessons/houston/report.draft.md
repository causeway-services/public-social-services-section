# Houston: Climate History and Outlook

## A humid Gulf Coast climate in a low, rapidly changing landscape

Houston’s climate cannot be understood from temperature and rainfall alone. The city sits on a nearly level coastal plain beside Galveston Bay and the Gulf of Mexico, is crossed by slow-moving bayous, and contains extensive paved and built surfaces. Its hazards emerge from interactions among Gulf moisture, tropical cyclones, intense thunderstorms, urban development, drainage capacity, groundwater-driven land subsidence, and rising relative sea level. The result is a place exposed both to extreme heat and to several distinct kinds of flooding.

“Climate” means the statistical distribution of weather over decades: averages, variability, seasonal cycles, and extremes. A climate normal is not a prediction for a particular year, nor does it describe every part of a metropolitan area. It is usually a 30-year average calculated at a specified weather station. Houston’s large urban region contains multiple stations with different surroundings, records, and microclimates, so “Houston’s temperature” or “Houston’s rainfall” is never entirely independent of location.

For example, the National Weather Service’s commonly cited Houston record is associated with George Bush Intercontinental Airport, north of central Houston, while William P. Hobby Airport lies southeast of downtown and closer to Galveston Bay. Hobby has a continuous observational record beginning in 1931, according to the [National Weather Service’s station history](https://www.weather.gov/hgx/houclimate). Comparisons through time should ideally use the same station, quality controls, and definitions; comparisons between stations can otherwise mix genuine climate differences with elevation, land-cover, instrumentation, and record-length effects.

## The observed seasonal climate

At Bush Intercontinental Airport, the 1991–2020 normal annual mean temperature was **70.5°F (21.4°C)**. Normal monthly means ranged from **53.8°F in January** to **85.2°F in August**. Normal daily highs reached **94.5°F in July** and **94.9°F in August**, while normal daily lows remained near **75–76°F** in those months. Annual normal precipitation was **51.84 inches (1,317 millimeters)**, distributed through the year rather than confined to a single wet season; June was the wettest normal month at 6.00 inches, but May, August, September, October, and December each averaged at least about four inches. These figures come from the National Weather Service’s [1991–2020 Houston normals](https://www.weather.gov/hgx/climate_iah_normals_summary).

Those averages conceal enormous variability. At the same station, the historical record high was **109°F**, reached on September 4, 2000, and August 27, 2011; the record low was **5°F** on January 18, 1930. The station’s greatest calendar-day rainfall was **16.07 inches on August 27, 2017**, during Hurricane Harvey, according to the same [National Weather Service record](https://www.weather.gov/hgx/climate_iah_normals_summary). Such extremes do not mean that every location in Houston experienced identical conditions. Rainfall in tropical systems can vary sharply over short distances, and a station record is a point measurement rather than a metropolitan-area total.

The seasonal pattern follows Houston’s geography. The Gulf supplies abundant water vapor, supporting humid summers, thunderstorms, and heavy rainfall. In winter, the relatively flat central United States permits occasional continental or Arctic air masses to reach Texas. Consequently, severe cold remains possible even in a warming climate. The relevant climate-change finding is not that cold weather disappears, but that the probability distribution shifts: hotter conditions become more common while extremely cold conditions generally become less common. Across Texas, average temperature has risen by roughly **1.5°F since the beginning of the twentieth century**, and the number of very warm nights was especially high during the 2010s, according to NOAA’s [Texas State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/tx/).

## How Houston’s climate has been changing

Houston’s 2020 climate assessment examined daily observations at 11 long-term stations and evaluated 25 indicators. It concluded that all seasons had warmed, hot days and heavy-precipitation events had become more frequent, and heat waves and hot days had intensified. It also found little historical change in annual precipitation totals but a shift toward more intense extreme rainfall and more dry days. These are distinct results: a location can have almost no change in annual rainfall while experiencing longer rain-free intervals and heavier downpours when rain does occur. The conclusions and their scope are summarized in the city’s primary technical report, [*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf).

The distinction between annual totals and event intensity is fundamental. Imagine two years that each receive 50 inches of rain. One could receive frequent moderate rain, while the other receives much of its total in a few exceptional storms separated by long dry periods. The second pattern creates greater flash-flood risk during storms and can still produce soil-moisture stress between them. NOAA similarly finds that future changes in Texas’s annual average precipitation are uncertain and generally projected to be small, while increases in extreme precipitation are likely and higher temperatures are expected to intensify moisture loss during dry spells ([NOAA Texas State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/tx/)).

Houston’s warming also has an important nighttime component. High nighttime temperatures reduce the opportunity for people, buildings, and infrastructure to cool after hot days. Urban materials absorb solar energy and release it after sunset, while limited vegetation reduces evaporative cooling. This urban heat-island effect is not the same process as global greenhouse warming, but the two can compound one another. NOAA specifically notes that urban heat islands have increased occurrences of very warm nights in Texas city centers ([NOAA Texas State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/tx/)).

## Harvey as an extreme event—and an attribution case study

Hurricane Harvey illustrates how weather dynamics, exposure, and a changing climate combine without being interchangeable. Harvey’s center stalled over or near the Texas coast for four days in August 2017, and the storm produced more than 60 inches of rain in southeastern Texas. The National Hurricane Center classified the resulting flooding as catastrophic and described Harvey as the second-costliest U.S. hurricane after adjustment for inflation at the time of its report, behind Hurricane Katrina ([National Hurricane Center tropical cyclone report](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL092017_Harvey.pdf)).

Climate change did not by itself “cause” Harvey. A tropical cyclone requires a particular atmospheric circulation, ocean state, and storm track. Event-attribution science instead asks a counterfactual question: how did human influence alter the probability or magnitude of an event compared with a climate without that influence?

One observation-based statistical study accounted for both global warming and El Niño–Southern Oscillation. It estimated that human-induced climate change increased Harvey’s rainfall in the most affected Houston-area locations by **at least 18.8%**, with a best estimate of **37.7%**, and increased the probability of the observed accumulations by at least a factor of **3.5**. The authors emphasized that these were estimated lower bounds under their method and called for additional dynamical modeling ([Risser and Wehner, *Geophysical Research Letters*](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0k28z28b)).

A separate study using observations and climate-model ensembles estimated that warming made Harvey’s precipitation about **15% more intense**, with an uncertainty range of **8%–19%**, or made such an event roughly **three times as likely**, with a reported range of **1.5–5 times**. That study also found an observed regional increase of about **12%–22%** in extreme-precipitation intensity since 1880 ([van Oldenborgh and colleagues, Oxford University Research Archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aa087d425-e958-4fb2-b73d-c83986a06297)).

These estimates differ because the studies used different datasets, models, statistical assumptions, spatial domains, and event definitions. Their agreement in direction is stronger than their agreement on an exact number. The defensible conclusion is that warming increased Harvey’s rainfall intensity and likelihood; no single percentage should be treated as a perfectly measured property of the storm.

The physical basis is understandable. Warmer air can contain more water vapor—approximately 6%–7% more per degree Celsius near typical lower-atmospheric temperatures under the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship—but storm rainfall also depends on moisture transport, circulation, vertical motion, storm duration, and location. Both Harvey studies found changes that could exceed simple moisture-capacity scaling, underscoring that the 7%-per-degree approximation is a useful baseline rather than a complete storm model ([Risser and Wehner](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0k28z28b); [van Oldenborgh and colleagues](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aa087d425-e958-4fb2-b73d-c83986a06297)).

## Why rainfall becomes flooding

Extreme rainfall is a meteorological hazard; flood damage depends on where water goes and what lies in its path. Houston’s impervious roofs, roads, and parking areas reduce infiltration and accelerate runoff. The Fifth National Climate Assessment identifies metropolitan Houston’s extensive impervious surfaces as a factor increasing the likelihood of widespread flooding ([Southern Great Plains chapter](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/26/)). Bayous and engineered channels move water through terrain with small elevation gradients, but a sufficiently large, long, or spatially extensive storm can exceed local drainage and storage capacity.

Several flood mechanisms should be distinguished:

- **Pluvial flooding** occurs when rainfall accumulates faster than it can infiltrate or drain, including outside mapped river floodplains.
- **Fluvial flooding** occurs when bayous, rivers, or channels exceed their banks.
- **Coastal flooding** results from storm surge, tides, waves, or elevated mean sea level.
- **Compound flooding** occurs when mechanisms coincide—for example, heavy rainfall and high coastal water levels that impede drainage.

Land subsidence increases these risks by lowering the ground relative to waterways and the sea. In the Houston–Galveston region, groundwater withdrawal reduces pore-water pressure in aquifers, allowing fine-grained sediments to compact. Much of that compaction is effectively irreversible on human timescales. The U.S. Geological Survey reports that groundwater withdrawal is the primary cause of regional subsidence, with oil and gas extraction also contributing; subsidence has increased the frequency and extent of flooding and damaged infrastructure ([USGS Harris County subsidence study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sim/3097/)).

The historical magnitudes were substantial. USGS estimated that more than **9 feet—and possibly as much as 10 feet—of subsidence** occurred in the Pasadena area from 1906 to 1978. Changes in pumping and increased use of surface water subsequently raised groundwater levels in some southeastern areas, while declines continued in parts of western Houston ([USGS groundwater and subsidence report](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr82571)). Regulation and water-source conversion therefore reduced subsidence in some places, but the historical loss of elevation remains part of today’s flood landscape.

## Relative sea level: ocean rise plus land movement

For local planning, **relative sea level** matters more than global mean sea level. It measures the sea surface relative to the adjacent land:

\[
\Delta RSL = \Delta S_{\text{ocean}} - \Delta Z_{\text{land}},
\]

where \(\Delta RSL\) is relative sea-level change, \(\Delta S_{\text{ocean}}\) is change in ocean height, and \(\Delta Z_{\text{land}}\) is vertical land movement, defined as positive upward. If the ocean rises while the land subsides, both terms increase relative sea level.

At Galveston Pier 21, NOAA’s 1904–2024 tide-gauge record gives a linear relative mean-sea-level trend of **6.65 ± 0.21 millimeters per year**, equivalent to **2.18 ± 0.07 feet per century**, where the uncertainty is NOAA’s 95% confidence interval for the fitted trend ([NOAA linear sea-level trends](https://prod.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/sltrends/data/USStationsLinearSeaLevelTrends.pdf)). Multiplying the historical rate by a century describes the fitted past trend; it is not a projection that the next century must follow the same straight line. Future ocean warming, land-ice loss, circulation, and local vertical land motion can change the rate.

Higher mean water levels allow storm surge and high tides to begin from an elevated baseline. Consequently, a coastal water level that was once rare can be reached more often even if storm characteristics do not change. NOAA assesses that warming is also likely to increase hurricane rainfall rates, the intensity of the strongest hurricanes, and surge heights through sea-level rise, while the future number of Texas landfalling hurricanes remains difficult to project ([NOAA Texas State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/tx/)). This is a carefully bounded finding: confidence is greater for rising coastal water levels and heavier tropical-cyclone rainfall than for the future frequency of landfalls at Houston.

## Houston’s projected climate

Houston’s city assessment used an ensemble of 22 CMIP5 global climate models, statistically downscaled to 11 local weather stations. It compared observations for 1971–1990 and 2001–2020 with three future 20-year periods: 2021–2040, 2051–2070, and 2081–2100. Its lower scenario was RCP4.5 and its higher scenario was RCP8.5 ([*Houston’s Climate Future*, methods](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)).

These scenarios are conditional pathways, not forecasts with assigned probabilities. RCP4.5 represents lower radiative forcing than RCP8.5; neither should be read as a precise prediction of future policy, population, technology, or emissions. Moreover, the assessment was published in 2020 and uses the CMIP5 generation of climate models. It remains a valuable place-specific analysis, but newer model ensembles and updated emissions pathways could yield different numerical ranges.

### Heat

At Hobby Airport, the assessment projects about **12 days per year above 100°F under RCP4.5** and **23 under RCP8.5** in 2051–2070. For 2081–2100, those values rise to approximately **14 and 54 days**, respectively. Nights above 80°F are projected to average **22 or 50 per year** by mid-century and **30 or 96** late in the century under the lower and higher scenarios ([*Houston’s Climate Future*, summary findings](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)).

The average hottest day of the 1971–1990 period was 99.4°F. The report projects approximately **104°F under the lower scenario and 106°F under the higher scenario by mid-century**, and **104°F or 109°F by late century**. Its temperature-based definition of summer also expands: relative to 1971–1990, summer is projected to be **41 or 55 days longer by mid-century** and **47 or 81 days longer by late century**, depending on scenario ([*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)).

These estimates describe 20-year model averages, not every year. Individual summers will remain cooler or hotter because of natural variability. The widening gap between scenarios later in the century illustrates a central point: near-term warming is influenced heavily by past emissions and climate-system inertia, while late-century outcomes depend much more on future global emissions.

Cooling degree days translate temperature into an approximate measure of building-cooling demand. For a daily mean temperature \(T_d\), a common base-65°F definition is

\[
CDD_d=\max(0,T_d-65^\circ\mathrm{F}),
\]

with daily values summed over a season. Houston’s assessment projects summer cooling degree days to increase relative to 1971–1990 by **351 under RCP4.5 or 487 under RCP8.5 by mid-century**, and by **396 or 729 by late century**. Those values are climatic indicators, not direct electricity forecasts: actual demand also depends on population, building efficiency, thermostat behavior, technology, and conservation ([*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)).

### Rain, drought, and extremes

The assessment found no significant projected change in Houston’s annual precipitation total, but it projected slightly drier summers, wetter falls, and more rain-free days. Hobby averaged **266 dry days per year in 1971–1990**; projected values were **271–273 by mid-century** and **270–277 late in the century**, depending on scenario. Its drought index also declined, indicating increased risk of extended drought as higher temperatures enhance atmospheric demand for moisture ([*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)).

At the same time, the average precipitation in the wettest three-day event was projected to rise from **7.8 inches historically to 8.6 inches by mid-century** under both scenarios and to **9 inches late in the century under the higher scenario**. Days exceeding four inches also increased slightly in the modeled 20-year periods ([*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)). Thus, “greater drought risk” and “heavier extreme rainfall” are not contradictory: they describe different parts of the precipitation distribution.

A “100-year storm” does not mean a storm occurring exactly once every century. Under a stationary climate, it denotes an annual exceedance probability of 1%, so \(p=1/T=0.01\) for return period \(T=100\) years. Even then, occurrences can cluster. Climate change further violates the stationarity assumption because the underlying probability changes over time. Houston’s assessment found large model-to-model and location-to-location differences in exceedances of historical 100-year rainfall thresholds. Almost all 22 models produced at least one future threshold exceedance at the Houston National Weather Service office, but projections for Hobby showed fewer models doing so ([*Houston’s Climate Future*](https://www.houstontx.gov/mayor/Climate-Impact-Assessment-2020-August.pdf)). That spatial and model spread is evidence of substantial local uncertainty, not a reason to assume zero risk.

## What is well established—and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions are strongly supported across observations, physical understanding, and models:

1. Houston and Texas have warmed, and further warming is expected under every assessed emissions pathway.
2. Extreme heat, warm nights, longer hot seasons, and cooling demand are expected to increase.
3. Heavy rainfall can intensify even if annual precipitation changes little.
4. Relative sea level has risen rapidly along the nearby coast, with land subsidence contributing to local exposure.
5. Houston’s flood consequences depend strongly on development, drainage, topography, subsidence, infrastructure, and social vulnerability—not climate alone.

Other questions retain wider uncertainty. Models do not agree closely on Houston’s annual or seasonal rainfall totals, the frequency and tracks of future landfalling hurricanes, or the exact local recurrence of rare rainfall events. Downscaling adds detail but cannot eliminate limitations inherited from global models or create certainty at neighborhood scale. Scenario uncertainty grows toward 2100, and an extreme-event return period estimated from a limited historical record can be especially unstable.

Finally, this report does not present a complete climate-risk assessment. It does not quantify neighborhood-level heat exposure, public-health outcomes, future population and land use, drainage projects, building vulnerability, insurance, or the performance of proposed coastal defenses. Its observational sea-level evidence extends through 2024, while the Houston projections come from a 2020 CMIP5-based assessment. Those boundaries should be kept visible when the figures are used in planning or teaching.

The broad outlook is nevertheless clear: Houston is likely to become hotter, with much more frequent dangerous heat and warm nights; its rainfall will probably become more concentrated into intense events even if annual totals remain broadly similar; and rising relative sea level will raise the baseline for coastal flooding. How severe the late-century changes become depends partly on global emissions, while the damage produced by any given hazard will also depend on choices about land, water, infrastructure, buildings, emergency management, and protection of vulnerable residents.
