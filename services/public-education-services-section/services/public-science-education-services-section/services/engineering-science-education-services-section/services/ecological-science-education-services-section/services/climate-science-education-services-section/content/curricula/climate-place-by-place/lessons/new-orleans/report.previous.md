# New Orleans: Climate History and Outlook

*Research date: September 20, 2026. Historical observations and future scenarios retain their original dates and geographic scope.*

New Orleans’ climate story begins with a question: where can the water go? Rain falls inside the city, the Mississippi carries water from far upstream, and hurricanes can push coastal water toward the metropolitan area. Meanwhile, the elevation of the land and the height of the sea change over time. Warming adds another challenge: prolonged heat can disrupt daily life even when streets remain dry. Understanding the outlook requires following these different processes, rather than treating every hazard as the same kind of flood.

## A warm, wet place with an uneven climate history

Louisiana receives both warm, moist air from the Gulf and continental air arriving from the north. That combination produces hot summers, relatively mild winters, and precipitation throughout the year. It also allows substantial variation between seasons and years. NOAA’s *Louisiana State Climate Summary 2022*, whose principal observations extend through 2020, describes an uneven temperature history: warmth early in the twentieth century, a cooler interval around the middle of the century, and renewed warming afterward. State temperatures rose more than 2°F from the cool period of the 1970s. Very warm nights increased particularly strongly in the recent decades covered by that assessment. These are Louisiana-wide findings, not a reconstructed thermometer record for every New Orleans neighborhood. The same assessment anticipates further warming and stronger heat waves, while acknowledging substantial uncertainty in seasonal rainfall changes. It also explains why drought remains possible in a generally wet state: higher temperatures can intensify moisture loss during dry spells. [NOAA, Louisiana State Climate Summary 2022](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/la/)

For a specifically local baseline, the National Weather Service publishes 1991–2020 normals for New Orleans Lakefront Airport. January’s average temperature was 54.0°F; August’s was 84.7°F. The August average daily high was 90.8°F, while the average low was 78.6°F. Annual precipitation averaged 60.12 inches. Those warm nighttime readings help describe a summer climate in which evening brings limited cooling. But a normal is a thirty-year average, not a promise about any particular summer or storm. The Lakefront record also represents one observing location. The weather service publishes a separate series for New Orleans International Airport, and the two should not be silently combined. Nor does a change between overlapping thirty-year normal periods provide a complete, independent estimate of the city’s long-term warming trend. [National Weather Service, 1991–2020 climate normals](https://www.weather.gov/lix/newnormals)

## How building the city changed the ground

The landscape was never a fixed platform. River channels shifted, deposited sediment, and left natural ridges across the Mississippi delta plain. In an October 2005 account of the region’s geological history, USGS scientist S. Jeffress Williams described how early New Orleans occupied relatively high natural levees near the river. Later expansion depended on draining lower, wetter land. Canals and pumps made additional areas available for development, but lowering the water table also dried organic soils. Compaction and oxidation then contributed to lost elevation. This history explains a central tension: removing water can make land usable while changing the land itself. It also explains why “New Orleans is below sea level” is an incomplete description. Natural ridges, reclaimed areas, and lower basins have different elevations and histories. Williams’ testimony is useful evidence about those historical processes; its twenty-year-old numerical forecasts should not substitute for newer sea-level assessments. [USGS, geological history and human alteration of New Orleans, October 20, 2005](https://www.usgs.gov/congressional/statement-s-jeffress-williams-coastal-marine-geologist-usgs-woods-hole-science-center)

A separate USGS publication from 2003 illustrates how subsidence has been measured. Land-elevation observations from five survey periods between 1951 and 1995 indicated average sinking of about five millimeters per year within the surveyed, leveed metropolitan areas. That is a historical average over a defined set of measurements. It is not a verified present-day rate for the entire city, and multiplying it forward indefinitely would conceal differences between sites and periods. The essential distinction is between the ocean rising and the ground descending. Both can increase water height relative to land. Measurements of local ground movement therefore matter alongside tide gauges and ocean projections; a global sea-level number alone cannot describe the elevation challenge facing a particular neighborhood. [Burkett, Zilkoski, and Hart, USGS, 2003](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/sea-level-rise-and-subsidence-implications-flooding-new-orleans-louisiana)

Changes extend far beyond the city boundary. A 2017 USGS analysis estimated that coastal Louisiana lost approximately 2,006 square miles of land between 1932 and 2016, with an uncertainty of about 171 square miles. This is a coast-wide historical estimate, not land lost from New Orleans itself. The researchers also cautioned against mistaking a short-term apparent increase in mapped land for recovery: water levels, storms, and measurement differences affect what satellite imagery classifies as land. Their analysis used twenty-one estimates across eighty-four years to distinguish longer-term change from that variability. The regional landscape surrounding New Orleans therefore needs to be understood as changing, with a record that must be interpreted carefully rather than reduced to a single dramatic rate. [USGS, coastal Louisiana land-change findings, 2017](https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/usgs-louisianas-rate-coastal-wetland-loss-continues-slow)

## Heat is already part of the local record

The summer of 2023 provides a concrete example of extreme heat. At New Orleans International Airport, the National Weather Service recorded 105°F on August 27, setting a new maximum for that station’s record at the time. August included fifteen days reaching at least 100°F, and its mean temperature was 89.1°F. The same monthly summary reports only 2.97 inches of rain, compared with an August normal of 6.91 inches. These observations show that exceptional heat and unusually low rainfall can coincide in the metropolitan area. They do not establish what happened on every block, nor does one summer by itself determine a climate trend. Their value is more immediate: they show the kinds of conditions local buildings, workers, transport, and public services have already had to confront. Future assessments must consider repeated hot days and persistently warm nights, not only the highest afternoon temperature. [National Weather Service, summer 2023 observations](https://www.weather.gov/lix/2023summerheat)

## Rainfall flooding and hurricane flooding are different

For ordinary stormwater, New Orleans relies on a sequence of collection and conveyance. Rain runs from roofs and streets into catch basins, then travels through pipes and canals to pumping stations. Pumps cannot remove water that has not yet reached them. The Sewerage and Water Board explains that the system’s historical design estimated removal of roughly half an inch per hour after underground storage fills. It warns that intense rain exceeding an inch in an hour will likely outpace the system and cause street flooding until drainage catches up. These are broad descriptions of system behavior, not a guaranteed capacity at every address. The practical point is that pump operation, available storage, and the movement of water through the whole network all matter. A heavy downpour can overwhelm drainage without a hurricane or an incoming coastal surge. [Sewerage and Water Board of New Orleans, drainage system facts](https://www.swbno.org/Stormwater/Facts)

Hurricane Katrina demonstrated a different pathway. The National Hurricane Center’s account of the 2005 season describes Katrina’s Louisiana landfall near Buras on August 29 as Category 3, followed by another landfall near the Louisiana–Mississippi border. It records the breaching or overtopping of levees and floodwalls around New Orleans and flooding across about eighty percent of the city. The location matters: landfall near Buras is not landfall in downtown New Orleans. The mechanism matters too: the scale of the disaster cannot be read directly from the storm’s category, which describes wind intensity. Katrina remains a defining event in the city’s history, but its occurrence alone does not establish how much human-caused warming changed that individual storm. Historical disaster description and climate attribution answer different questions. [National Hurricane Center, final 2005 Atlantic season summary](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/archive/2005/tws/MIATWSAT_nov_final.shtml)

After Katrina, the Hurricane and Storm Damage Risk Reduction System changed the metropolitan area’s defenses. The Army Corps of Engineers describes a design based on combinations of storm size, track, intensity, surge, and waves. Its “100-year” level refers to a surge event with a one-percent chance of occurring or being exceeded in a given year; it does not mean one event scheduled every century. The Corps also emphasizes that the system reduces risk rather than eliminating it. This distinction prevents two mistakes: assuming the city’s defenses are unchanged since 2005, or assuming the completed works make future flooding impossible. The design description is not a current inspection of every levee reach. Continued performance depends on changing conditions and the infrastructure itself. It also should not be confused with the separate task of removing intense rainfall from within the defended area. [U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, HSDRRS explanation](https://www.mvn.usace.army.mil/Missions/HSDRRS/)

## What the outlook does—and does not—tell us

The 2022 federal sea-level assessment provides a useful regional scale. NOAA’s explanatory toolkit summarizes anticipated Gulf Coast rise of approximately fourteen to eighteen inches between 2020 and 2050. This is a regional estimate over a specified thirty-year interval, not fourteen to eighteen additional inches starting in September 2026. It is also not a forecast of water depth inside every New Orleans home. Regional sea-level change reflects ocean processes and vertical land movement; converting it into local inundation requires information about elevation, connectivity, and defenses. NOAA explains that warming ocean water expands and melting land ice adds water, while local processes can make relative sea level differ from the global average. For a city planning long-lived infrastructure, the lesson is to evaluate a changing water level through time rather than assume today’s relationship between land and sea will persist. [NOAA, sea-level rise and regional projections](https://prod-01-asg-toolkit-climate.woc.noaa.gov/sea-level-rise)

Hurricane projections require similar care. NOAA’s Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory concludes that warming is expected to increase tropical cyclone rainfall rates and average intensity globally. More atmospheric moisture is one reason rainfall can intensify. These findings do not imply that every future hurricane will be stronger, or establish a reliable timetable of future landfalls around New Orleans. Changes in total storm numbers and in the paths storms follow involve additional uncertainty. Coastal impacts can also worsen because a storm begins from a higher background sea level, even without a dramatic change in its wind category. The useful outlook is therefore a set of changing hazards: wetter storms, potential increases in intensity, and higher coastal water levels. It is not a claim that a particular named hurricane will strike the city in a particular year. [NOAA GFDL, global warming and hurricanes](https://www.gfdl.noaa.gov/global-warming-and-hurricanes/)

Louisiana’s 2023 Coastal Master Plan is an example of planning across uncertain futures. CPRA describes the legislatively approved plan as guidance for restoration and flood-risk reduction investments. Its public technical presentation used lower and higher environmental scenarios, including approximately 1.6 and 2.5 feet of sea-level rise by the model’s fiftieth year. Those assumptions belong to the 2023 planning framework; they are not interchangeable with NOAA’s 2020–2050 regional estimate. Nor are the projects in a master plan automatically completed construction. The purpose of comparing scenarios is to test how choices might perform as conditions change. For New Orleans, regional coastal planning and neighborhood drainage operate at different scales, so evidence of progress in one cannot stand in for evidence about the other. [CPRA, 2023 Coastal Master Plan](https://coastal.la.gov/our-plan/2023-coastal-master-plan/); [CPRA, 2023 scenario presentation](https://coastal.la.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/MPPublicHearing_PPT.pdf)

## Adaptation is visible in specific places and decisions

Some local measures have documented completion. In April 2021, the city and the New Orleans Redevelopment Authority announced completion of one hundred household stormwater projects through the Community Adaptation Program in Gentilly. Measures included permeable paving, infiltration trenches, rain gardens, rain barrels, and planting. The stated purpose was to hold water near where it fell and release it more gradually into drainage. This is a concrete example of adapting the built landscape rather than relying exclusively on bigger pumps. However, that announcement establishes a historical milestone, not the present condition of all those installations or a measured citywide reduction in flood losses. Its scale is residential properties within Gentilly. Keeping that scale clear makes it possible to recognize an actual intervention without presenting it as a solution to every form of flooding. [City of New Orleans, Community Adaptation Program milestone, April 8, 2021](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/LANOLA/bulletins/2cba21f/)

The city’s 2022 Climate Action Plan addresses both emissions and adaptation. Its published targets include cutting carbon emissions by fifty percent by 2035 and reaching net zero by 2050. Proposed actions cover cleaner electricity, building energy use, transportation choices, waste, tree canopy, green infrastructure, and more resilient water and energy systems. These are goals and strategies, not evidence that their promised outcomes have already occurred. The announcement also explains a meaningful revision: the interim emissions-reduction target moved from 2030 in the earlier plan to 2035. Dates should therefore be attached to the version being discussed. Reducing emissions addresses the drivers of future warming; adaptation addresses risks facing residents as conditions change. The city’s own plan places both within its response. [City of New Orleans, announcement of the 2022 Climate Action Plan](https://nola.gov/next/mayors-office/news/articles/2022/november-2022/2022-12-13-release-climate-plan/)

Heat planning provides a particularly current example. As accessed on September 20, 2026, the city’s Heat Action Plan development page describes work to map hotter areas, understand who is most affected, and identify cooling measures. It documents a March 30, 2026 public meeting and a survey that closed May 1. The page anticipated completion by mid-2026, but still displays the final recommendations task as not yet started. That page alone therefore does not establish adoption of a completed plan. What it does establish is the city’s recognition that heat exposure differs between neighborhoods and affects residents unevenly, including older people, children, and outdoor workers. Evaluating the eventual response will require looking beyond the existence of a plan to whether cooling resources and neighborhood improvements reach the people facing the greatest exposure. [City of New Orleans, Heat Action Plan development](https://nola.gov/next/sustainability/adaptation-nature-solutions/heat-action-plan-development/)

New Orleans’ future is not captured by one temperature, one levee, or one sea-level projection. The central questions are practical and local: how quickly can rainfall leave a neighborhood; how are land elevations changing; how do defenses perform under evolving coastal conditions; and who can find relief during sustained heat? Answering them together connects the city’s environmental history to decisions residents will experience in their homes, streets, and daily routines.
