# Los Angeles: Climate History and Outlook

## Reading Los Angeles climate correctly

Los Angeles does not have one uniform climate. The city, county, and broader metropolitan region span a cool Pacific coast, a densely urbanized basin, warmer inland valleys, steep mountains, and dry high-desert terrain. Marine air and coastal fog can hold summer temperatures down near the ocean while, on the same day, valleys beyond the coastal hills experience dangerous heat. Elevation, slope orientation, vegetation, pavement, building density, and distance from the sea all affect local conditions. The state’s climate-adaptation framework therefore defines a larger “Los Angeles region”—urban Los Angeles, Orange County, southern Ventura County, and western portions of San Bernardino and Riverside Counties—whose ecosystems range from coast to mountains and desert interior ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)).

This geography matters when interpreting averages. A regional mean can describe broad change without representing the temperature on a particular block, beach, hillside, or inland worksite. Even the phrase “Los Angeles” may refer to the City of Los Angeles, Los Angeles County, the urbanized basin, or the wider assessment region. This report states the relevant scale wherever the source permits.

A second prerequisite is the distinction between weather and climate. Weather describes short-term conditions; climate describes the statistical distribution of weather—its means, variability, seasonal cycle, and extremes—usually over several decades. A single storm, drought, or fire cannot by itself prove or disprove climate change. Climate change can nevertheless alter an event’s probability or intensity by changing its background conditions. Likewise, an individual weather station provides a valuable local record but cannot, without additional analysis, stand in for the whole metropolitan area.

The strongest general conclusion is clear: Los Angeles is warming and will continue to warm. The details are less uniform. Future temperature depends strongly on global greenhouse-gas emissions, particularly after mid-century; precipitation totals remain highly variable and comparatively uncertain; and damages depend not only on physical hazards but also on where and how people build, the condition of infrastructure, access to cooling and insurance, emergency preparedness, and underlying social vulnerability.

## The inherited climate: dry summers, episodic rain, and strong gradients

Los Angeles has a Mediterranean-type seasonal regime: summers are generally dry, while most rain arrives during the cooler part of the year. The wider region’s climate is described by the State of California as having hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)). “Mediterranean,” however, does not mean uniformly mild. Coastal moderation weakens inland, and the basin’s mountains both redirect airflow and separate sharply different temperature regimes.

Rainfall is unusually episodic. The Fourth California Climate Change Assessment’s Los Angeles report found that approximately five storms supply half of the region’s annual precipitation in a typical year. It also found strong year-to-year variability, influence from modes such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation, and no clear historical trend in regional annual precipitation through the period it assessed ([Los Angeles Region Report](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg%20Report-%20SUM-CCCA4-2018-007%20LosAngeles_ADA.pdf)). Thus, “average annual rainfall” conceals the central fact of Los Angeles hydroclimate: long dry intervals can be punctuated by a small number of powerful storms.

This concentration has practical consequences. A wet year does not necessarily imply steady water availability, and a near-normal seasonal total can still contain damaging downpours. Conversely, several missed storms can produce an exceptionally dry year. Reservoir operations, groundwater storage, imported water, stormwater capture, and flood-control infrastructure must therefore manage both scarcity and sudden excess.

Regional water dependence extends far beyond local rainfall. Historically, snow-derived water from the Sierra Nevada and Colorado Rockies has helped meet Southern California’s residential, industrial, agricultural, and ecological demands ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)). Los Angeles climate risk consequently includes changes occurring outside the basin: warmer winters can reduce snow accumulation, accelerate melt, and increase evaporative demand across distant source watersheds.

Santa Ana winds are another essential part of the system. These dry, often powerful offshore winds descend toward coastal Southern California. Compression during descent warms and dries the air, while terrain can channel and accelerate winds through passes and canyons. Santa Ana winds are natural, recurrent weather phenomena rather than products of climate change. Their consequences become more dangerous when they coincide with dry vegetation, low humidity, ignition, exposed development, and an extended dry season.

## What the historical record establishes

California’s statewide instrumental record shows persistent warming since 1895, with faster warming beginning around the 1980s. In the state analysis available in 2018, minimum temperatures—generally associated with nighttime—had increased by about 2.3°F per century, compared with about 1.3°F per century for maximum temperatures. The same analysis identified the South Coast as the fastest-warming of four California climate regions examined ([California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment](https://oehha.ca.gov/epic/changes-climate/overview-changes-climate); [technical temperature report](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/epic/downloads/annualairtemptech2018.pdf)).

Nighttime warming is important because health risk depends partly on whether the body and buildings can cool after sunset. Higher minimum temperatures reduce overnight relief during heat waves. Urban materials compound this problem by absorbing solar energy during the day and releasing heat after dark, while limited tree cover and evapotranspiration can leave some neighborhoods substantially hotter than greener surroundings. Urbanization is not a substitute explanation for greenhouse warming: the urban heat-island effect changes the local expression of heat, whereas regional and global climate analyses account for broad-scale warming across many settings. In Los Angeles, the two influences can operate together.

Individual weather stations illustrate variability but must be interpreted carefully. The National Weather Service’s long record for downtown Los Angeles begins in the nineteenth century, while airport, valley, and foothill stations have different periods of record and distinct exposure. The official records show, for example, a downtown all-time maximum of 113°F in 2010 and a Woodland Hills maximum of 120°F in 2020, underscoring the sharp coastal-to-inland gradient rather than identifying one temperature as representative of the entire city ([National Weather Service climate records](https://www.weather.gov/boi/cliplot_records?wfo=lox)). Station moves, instrument changes, urban development, and incomplete years must be considered in formal trend analysis; raw record tables are best used to illustrate local extremes, not to calculate an unadjusted regional warming rate.

Historical precipitation presents a different picture. The regional assessment found no clear trend in annual totals, even though temperature has risen ([Los Angeles Region Report](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg%20Report-%20SUM-CCCA4-2018-007%20LosAngeles_ADA.pdf)). This is not contradictory. Climate change can intensify evaporation, soil and vegetation drying, extreme rainfall rates, or the alternation of wet and dry conditions without producing a simple monotonic trend in annual precipitation.

Sea level provides a comparatively direct long-term indicator. California’s environmental indicators report gives a relative sea-level trend at the Los Angeles tide gauge of approximately **1.03 millimeters per year**, or **0.04 inch per year**, over 1923–2020. Santa Monica’s reported trend was about **1.54 millimeters per year** over 1933–2020 ([OEHHA sea-level indicator](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/epic/downloads/03sealevelrise.pdf)). “Relative” means measured against the adjacent land, so the value includes both ocean change and local vertical land motion. A historical linear trend is useful evidence of past change, but NOAA cautions that it should not simply be extrapolated for planning because future sea-level rise is expected to accelerate and depends on emissions and ice-sheet behavior ([NOAA Sea Level Trends and Extremes](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/trends-and-extremes/index.html)).

## Why the climate is changing

The governing mechanism is the enhanced greenhouse effect. Carbon dioxide, methane, and other greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation. Increasing their atmospheric concentrations creates a planetary energy imbalance, warming the surface and lower atmosphere. The U.S. climate assessment concluded that greenhouse-gas emissions from human activity are the only set of influences capable of accounting for the observed warming over the last century; natural factors alone do not provide a credible explanation for the magnitude and pattern of change ([Fourth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2018.globalchange.gov/chapter/2)).

Several connected mechanisms translate global warming into Los Angeles hazards:

1. **Higher background temperatures shift the whole distribution.** If daily temperatures vary around a warmer mean, thresholds formerly reached only in rare heat waves are crossed more often. Changes in variability, humidity, winds, and land cover can further alter actual exposure.

2. **Warmer air and vegetation increase atmospheric moisture demand.** All else equal, warming promotes evaporation and evapotranspiration, drying soils and live or dead fuels more rapidly. This can intensify drought impacts even where annual rainfall changes little.

3. **A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapor.** When the circulation supplies moisture and uplift, this can support heavier precipitation. It does not mean every storm becomes stronger or that every year becomes wetter; circulation, storm tracks, ocean conditions, and local topography remain crucial.

4. **Ocean warming and land-ice loss raise sea level.** Thermal expansion of seawater and melting glaciers and ice sheets increase global mean sea level. Local water levels additionally reflect tides, waves, storms, ocean circulation, and vertical land movement. California’s indicator report notes that heat-driven ocean expansion contributed roughly half of observed global rise over the past century ([OEHHA sea-level indicator](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/epic/downloads/03sealevelrise.pdf)).

5. **Hazards combine.** Heat may increase electricity demand while degrading equipment performance; intense rain following fire can mobilize ash and debris; sea-level rise can impede drainage during coastal storms; and a wet growing season followed by exceptional drying can produce abundant, combustible vegetation.

## The temperature outlook

The Fourth California Climate Change Assessment used downscaled global climate models under Representative Concentration Pathways RCP4.5 and RCP8.5. An RCP is not a prediction but a prescribed trajectory of radiative forcing associated with different greenhouse-gas futures. Downscaling translates coarse global simulations into finer regional patterns, but it does not remove uncertainty from model structure, future emissions, natural variability, or local land-use change.

For the wider Los Angeles assessment region, the modeled historical annual average daily maximum was **72.5°F** for 1976–2005. Under RCP4.5, model-average values rose to **74.8°F** for 2006–2039, **76.7°F** for 2040–2069, and **77.8°F** for 2070–2100. Under RCP8.5, corresponding averages were **75.1°F**, **78.2°F**, and **80.9°F**. The report also supplied wide model and year-to-year ranges rather than representing these central values as exact forecasts ([Los Angeles Region Report](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg%20Report-%20SUM-CCCA4-2018-007%20LosAngeles_ADA.pdf)).

These figures imply two lessons. First, substantial warming is expected under both pathways through the first part of the century because of existing greenhouse forcing and inertia in the climate system. Second, the scenarios separate markedly later in the century: under RCP4.5, forcing stabilizes more than under RCP8.5, producing less late-century warming. Emissions choices therefore matter most strongly to the magnitude of long-term change, not to whether the next few decades are warmer than the late twentieth century.

Extreme heat changes more dramatically than the annual mean. Historically, Los Angeles International Airport experienced fewer than 15 days per year at or above 90°F; under the assessment’s RCP8.5 projections, it could experience roughly **50–90 such days per year by late century**. Many land locations were projected to gain **60–90 days per year at or above 95°F**, while the coast and highest elevations showed much smaller increases ([Los Angeles Region Report](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg%20Report-%20SUM-CCCA4-2018-007%20LosAngeles_ADA.pdf)). These are model projections under a high-forcing pathway, not forecasts for a specific year.

A separate state legislative synthesis, using a relative local definition of extreme heat—the historical 98th-percentile maximum for the calendar date—reported that Los Angeles County averaged about four extreme-heat days per year in 1961–1990. Models projected approximately nine per year in 2035–2064 and twelve per year in 2070–2099 ([California Legislative Analyst’s Office](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/4575)). That smaller count is not inconsistent with the airport figures: the studies use different thresholds, places, baselines, and metrics.

## Water: drought and flood are not opposites

Average annual precipitation is among the more uncertain elements of the Los Angeles outlook. The regional modeling assessed in 2018 showed considerable overlap among scenarios and models, consistent with the region’s large natural variability ([Los Angeles Region Report](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg%20Report-%20SUM-CCCA4-2018-007%20LosAngeles_ADA.pdf)). It would therefore be misleading to present one precise percentage change in annual rainfall as settled.

Confidence is stronger about several related processes. The state projects greater oscillation between dry and wet extremes, more frequent or severe heavy-rain events, and a possible doubling in the frequency of extremely dry years by late century in the broader Los Angeles region ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)). Statewide analyses likewise project less frequent but more intense storms and more precipitation falling as rain instead of snow ([California Legislative Analyst’s Office](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/4575)).

Drought should therefore be understood as more than lack of rainfall. A useful conceptual water balance is

\[
\Delta S = P + I - E - R - W,
\]

where \(\Delta S\) is the change in stored water, \(P\) precipitation, \(I\) imported or incoming water, \(E\) evaporation and evapotranspiration, \(R\) runoff leaving the system, and \(W\) withdrawals. This is a bookkeeping identity, not a Los Angeles forecast. It shows why warmer conditions can reduce storage or worsen vegetation stress even without a large decline in \(P\): \(E\) can increase, snowmelt timing can alter \(I\), and intense storms can raise rapid runoff \(R\) before water is captured.

Flood risk can rise at the same time. Los Angeles contains paved watersheds, channelized rivers, steep tributaries, dense development, and recently burned slopes. Short, intense rain can overwhelm drainage, create flash flooding, and trigger debris flows. Because a few storms already dominate annual rainfall, changes in storm intensity may matter more for infrastructure than a modest change in the annual total.

## Wildfire: climate influence within a larger causal system

Wildfire is a process, not a single climate variable. A destructive urban or wildland-interface fire requires ignition, burnable fuel, weather favorable to spread, and people or assets in its path. Climate can affect fuel growth, fuel dryness, the duration of the dry season, and fire weather; it does not directly determine every ignition, windstorm, suppression outcome, building failure, or evacuation.

The state’s broader regional summary projects a **60–75% increase in annual burned area by mid-century**, but the underlying assessment also emphasizes uncertainty in wildfire modeling ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)). Burned area depends on vegetation, ignitions, suppression, development, fuel management, and the fact that prior fires remove fuel for a time. A percentage projection is therefore a scenario result, not a calendar prediction.

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires offer a useful case study in compound risk. NOAA’s synthesis identified consecutive wet winters that encouraged vegetation growth, followed by extreme heat, a record-dry autumn, and a powerful Santa Ana event ([NOAA Climate.gov](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)). A rapid World Weather Attribution analysis estimated that, in a world about 1.3°C warmer than preindustrial conditions, the extreme Fire Weather Index conditions were about **35% more likely** and approximately **6% more intense**, with an estimated present-day recurrence of roughly once every 17 years ([WWA scientific report](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/wp-content/uploads/WWA-scientific-report-LA-wildfires.pdf)).

Those estimates should be treated as conditional attribution results, not proof that climate change “caused” the fires. Fire Weather Index combines temperature, humidity, wind, and antecedent precipitation; it does not model ignition, building vulnerability, suppression, or every local terrain effect. The WWA assessment was produced rapidly, and eight of eleven models showed an increase in extreme January fire weather—supportive, but not unanimous, model evidence ([WWA scientific report](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/wp-content/uploads/WWA-scientific-report-LA-wildfires.pdf)). The established conclusion is that warming increased important background risks; the precise fraction of total destruction attributable to climate change remains much harder to quantify.

## Coast, health, infrastructure, and unequal exposure

Rising relative sea level raises the baseline on which tides, waves, and storm surge operate. California’s 2022 indicator report warns that the combined effects of higher seas, storms, beach erosion, heavy precipitation, and shallower coastal groundwater can amplify flooding and drainage problems ([OEHHA sea-level indicator](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/epic/downloads/03sealevelrise.pdf)). The state’s Los Angeles summary gives a likely planning range of roughly **1.3–2.8 feet by 2100**, with a low-probability high-end possibility around **6.3 feet** ([California Climate Adaptation Strategy](https://climateresilience.ca.gov/regions/los-angeles.html)). The range reflects emissions, ocean response, ice-sheet uncertainty, and local conditions; it should not be collapsed into a single certain number.

Exposure is concentrated in low-lying beaches, ports, roads, utilities, wetlands, and drainage systems. Impacts may occur well before permanent inundation because occasional high-water events reach farther inland from a higher baseline. Beaches and wetlands can sometimes migrate landward, but development, seawalls, and steep topography may block that adjustment.

Heat is likely to remain the most widespread direct health hazard. California’s legislative health review links extreme heat to heat stroke and increased risks of cardiovascular illness, respiratory illness, kidney failure, and preterm birth. It identifies older adults, medically vulnerable people, outdoor workers, and lower-income households as disproportionately exposed or less able to adapt ([California Legislative Analyst’s Office](https://www.lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/4580)). Wildfire smoke adds acute and chronic respiratory hazards, while power outages can remove cooling or medical support during the same event.

Risk can be summarized as an interaction:

\[
\text{Risk} \approx \text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure} \times \text{Vulnerability}.
\]

This is a conceptual relationship, not a calibrated equation. It clarifies why identical temperatures need not produce identical outcomes. Tree canopy, housing quality, air conditioning, energy cost, occupational exposure, health status, language access, mobility, and neighborhood infrastructure all influence harm. Adaptation that lowers one term—such as cool roofs reducing indoor exposure—can reduce risk even while the climatic hazard increases.

## What a defensible outlook supports

A reasonable Los Angeles adaptation portfolio addresses multiple hazards and timescales:

- Heat plans can combine warnings, accessible cooling spaces, shade, reflective roofs and pavements, worker protections, building retrofits, and reliable electricity. Tree planting can help, but species choice, water demand, maintenance, fire conditions, and unequal canopy distribution must be considered.
- Water planning can integrate conservation, reuse, groundwater management, stormwater capture, imported-supply diversification, and flood capacity. Strategies should be tested against both prolonged dryness and intense storms.
- Fire resilience can include ignition prevention, defensible space, building hardening, vegetation management appropriate to local ecosystems, evacuation planning, backup communications, and land-use decisions that do not continually add exposure.
- Coastal planning can use adaptive pathways rather than one fixed forecast: protect some assets, accommodate temporary flooding, restore natural buffers where feasible, and consider relocation when protection would be technically, financially, or ecologically unsustainable.
- Public-health adaptation should prioritize people with the least capacity to avoid heat, smoke, displacement, or rising utility and insurance costs.

These measures involve trade-offs. Dense infill can reduce transportation emissions and pressure to expand into fire-prone land, but poorly designed density can intensify heat exposure. Coastal armoring may defend an asset while accelerating beach loss. More air conditioning protects health but increases peak electricity demand unless paired with efficient buildings, demand management, clean generation, and storage. Vegetation can cool neighborhoods yet become hazardous if inappropriate plants are poorly maintained near structures.

## Limits of present knowledge

This outlook is not a prediction of Los Angeles weather in a particular future year. Climate models estimate distributions under assumed forcing pathways. Uncertainty comes from future emissions, internal variability, model physics, downscaling, land-use change, demographic development, and adaptation. Uncertainty is especially large for precipitation, wildfire area, rare coastal extremes, and neighborhood-scale outcomes.

The principal quantitative regional source remains California’s 2018 Fourth Climate Change Assessment. As of September 30, 2026, California’s Fifth Assessment was in the process of releasing new CMIP6-based scenarios, research, and regional synthesis products; the state described public webinars beginning in September 2026 rather than presenting the full regional synthesis as an already settled replacement ([Governor’s Office of Land Use and Climate Innovation](https://lci.ca.gov/climate/icarp/climate-assessment/)). Consequently, the older RCP-based figures reported here should be read as established assessment results, not necessarily the newest available generation of every projection.

The evidence nevertheless supports a firm core conclusion. Los Angeles has already warmed; relative sea level has risen; and further warming, more frequent extreme heat, greater evaporative stress, and higher coastal water levels are expected. Annual rainfall totals and individual storms will remain strongly variable, but damaging precipitation extremes and rapid wet-to-dry transitions warrant planning. The future magnitude of change is not predetermined: global emissions influence long-term hazards, while local decisions determine how much those hazards become human disasters.
