# San Antonio’s Climate Past and Next Fifty Years: Heat, Water, Drought, and Flood

## The practical question

What climate should San Antonio prepare for: its twentieth-century climate, its recent run of exceptional heat and drought, or something hotter still?

The evidence points to a clear but uneven answer. San Antonio’s future climate is likely to be substantially hotter, with many more triple-digit days and warm nights. Annual rainfall is much less certain: models disagree on whether the region will become modestly wetter or drier. Yet several lines of evidence indicate that warming will intensify water stress even without a large decline in rainfall, while the heaviest downpours may become more intense. The useful planning picture is therefore not simply “hotter and drier.” It is **hotter, more evaporative, vulnerable to longer and more damaging drought, yet still exposed to exceptional rain and flash flooding**.

That combination matters because San Antonio sits near the Balcones Escarpment, where terrain and atmospheric moisture can help focus intense rainfall, and because part of its water supply comes from the rainfall-sensitive Edwards Aquifer. Climate hazards are not independent: heat raises water demand and evaporation during drought, while rapid urban growth increases both the number of people exposed to heat and the amount of infrastructure that must safely carry stormwater.

## Prerequisites: weather, climate, normals, and projections

Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular time. Climate describes the distribution of weather over a much longer period: not only averages, but also variability, seasonal patterns, and the probability of extremes.

A **climate normal** is conventionally a 30-year average. NOAA’s current official U.S. normals use 1991–2020 observations from weather stations; they are a recent reference baseline, not a promise that the next 30 years will behave the same way ([NOAA, “Understanding Climate Normals”](https://www.noaa.gov/explainers/understanding-climate-normals)). A normal can tell us what has recently been typical, but it cannot capture the full range of rare events. Nor is it necessarily a stable guide in a warming climate.

A **projection** is also different from a weather forecast. A forecast attempts to predict a particular sequence of weather. A climate projection asks what statistical conditions could emerge under specified assumptions about greenhouse-gas emissions and the climate system. San Antonio’s city-scale assessment used two CMIP5 pathways: RCP4.5, a lower-emissions pathway, and RCP8.5, a much higher pathway. It averaged output from 21 models over 30-year windows—2011–2040, 2041–2070, and 2071–2100—to reduce the influence of individual wet, dry, hot, or cool years ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)).

Those scenarios should not be read as exact predictions of policy, population, or emissions. They are conditional experiments: *if the world follows a pathway with roughly these radiative effects, what does the model ensemble imply?* The disagreement among models is part of the result, particularly for precipitation.

## San Antonio’s present climate profile

At San Antonio International Airport, the 1991–2020 normal annual mean temperature is 69.6°F, constructed from a normal daily maximum of 80.3°F and minimum of 58.9°F. Normal annual precipitation is 32.38 inches ([NOAA/NCEI station normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00012921)).

That annual rainfall number conceals a strong seasonal and event-scale structure. May is normally the wettest month, at 4.40 inches, followed by September at 3.88 inches and October at 3.75 inches. Winter is comparatively dry: January, February, and December average 1.96, 1.74, and 2.00 inches, respectively. The station normally records measurable precipitation—at least 0.01 inch—on about 81 days per year, while precipitation of at least one inch occurs on only about nine days ([NOAA/NCEI station normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00012921)).

This is the first key to San Antonio’s climate: its water does not arrive smoothly. A relatively small number of storms can supply a substantial portion of a month’s or year’s total. Long rainless intervals can therefore coexist with destructive downpours.

The second key is geographic transition. San Antonio lies between more humid eastern Texas and the progressively drier country to the west. Across the Southern Great Plains, average annual precipitation ranges from roughly 10–15 inches in the west to more than 50 inches near the eastern boundary, and precipitation varies greatly from season to season and year to year throughout the region ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southern Great Plains](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/26/)). San Antonio’s climate reflects this tension between Gulf moisture, dry continental air, episodic tropical moisture, frontal systems, and the lifting and storm organization associated with the Balcones Escarpment.

## What past extremes reveal

### Heat: exceptional days are becoming a central planning concern

San Antonio has always been capable of severe heat. On September 5, 2000, the city reached 111°F, its official all-time record in the National Weather Service’s daily record series ([NWS San Antonio September records](https://www.weather.gov/media/ewx/climate/SATSeptember.pdf)). Earlier, a 108°F reading on August 19, 1986, had set the record before temperatures of 109°F and 111°F surpassed it in September 2000 ([NWS historical weather highlights](https://www.weather.gov/media/ewx/wxevents/ewx-18992000.pdf)).

Individual records do not, by themselves, establish a climate trend. They do show what combinations of dry soils, atmospheric circulation, sunlight, and regional air masses can produce. Their planning importance grows when a warmer background climate makes thresholds such as 100°F easier to cross and heat episodes harder to interrupt at night.

Warm nights deserve particular attention. Daytime heat loads the body, buildings, pavement, and the electricity system; a hot night limits physiological and structural cooling before the next day. The Fifth National Climate Assessment concludes with high confidence that extreme heat and high humidity harm health and deepen existing inequities in the Southern Great Plains ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southern Great Plains](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/26/)). Exposure is not uniform: outdoor work, limited tree shade, inadequate housing insulation, lack of reliable cooling, chronic illness, and inability to afford electricity can turn the same meteorological event into very different human risks.

Cold extremes have not vanished. San Antonio’s historical record includes a low of 6°F on February 2, 1951, and February 2021 produced daily record cold conditions, including 13°F on February 14 ([NWS San Antonio February records](https://www.weather.gov/media/ewx/climate/SATFebruary.pdf)). A warming average reduces the expected frequency of freezing nights, but it does not eliminate the need to protect pipes, power systems, transportation, or vulnerable residents from occasional winter outbreaks. “Fewer cold extremes” is not equivalent to “no severe freezes.”

### Rainfall: the 1998 flood as a mechanism lesson

The October 17–18, 1998 flood demonstrates why San Antonio’s annual rainfall average is an incomplete measure of risk. Moisture associated with two eastern Pacific hurricanes interacted with a nearly stationary front. A mesoscale convective system then produced almost continuous rain for as long as 36 hours along the Balcones Escarpment ([NWS account of the October 1998 flood](https://www.weather.gov/ewx/wxevent-1998flood?lv=true)).

San Antonio International Airport measured 11.26 inches on October 17—the city’s greatest calendar-day rainfall—and 13.35 inches over 24 hours spanning October 17–18. October’s total reached 18.07 inches, making it the wettest month then recorded at the station ([NWS account of the October 1998 flood](https://www.weather.gov/ewx/wxevent-1998flood?lv=true)). The one-day total was about 35% of the airport’s present 32.38-inch annual normal. That comparison is not a return-period calculation; it simply illustrates how concentrated San Antonio rainfall can be.

The mechanism also explains the region’s flash-flood sensitivity. When storms repeatedly form or move over the same corridor—often called “training”—rain can accumulate faster than soils, channels, drainage systems, and floodplains can carry it away. Urban pavement and roofs reduce infiltration and accelerate runoff. A storm’s damage therefore depends not only on its total rainfall but also on intensity, duration, antecedent soil moisture, drainage design, and the placement of people and property.

A “100-year storm” is frequently misunderstood. It means a rainfall magnitude with a 1% chance of being equaled or exceeded in any given year under the statistical conditions used to estimate it. It does not mean such a storm arrives only once per century, and two can occur close together. Moreover, if rainfall statistics change over time, a return period calculated from the historical record may no longer represent the same future probability. San Antonio’s climate assessment explicitly notes that design-storm estimates are important for drainage capacity and flood mapping and may change as the climate changes ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)).

### Drought: the episode that shaped water institutions

The 1950s drought is the foundational water-planning event for modern San Antonio. On August 17, 1956, the J-17 index well reached a record low elevation of 612.5 feet above mean sea level. The crisis helped prompt creation of the Edwards Underground Water District in 1959, although that district initially lacked regulatory powers ([Edwards Aquifer Authority historical timeline](https://www.edwardsaquifer.org/about/history/timeline/)). During the six-year 1951–1956 drought, Comal Springs stopped flowing for 144 days—the only known cessation in the documented aquifer record beginning in 1934 ([Edwards Aquifer Recovery Implementation Program report](https://edwardsaquifer.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Edwards-Aquifer-Recovery-Implementation-Program-Nov-2021.pdf)).

The hydrologic reason is straightforward. The Edwards is a karst aquifer: its faulted and fractured limestone transmits water efficiently. Rain falling across contributing watersheds enters streams, and much of the recharge occurs where those streams cross exposed, permeable limestone in the recharge zone. Historical recharge has consequently varied enormously. EAA estimates for 1934–2008 ranged from 43,700 acre-feet during the 1956 drought to 2.486 million acre-feet in 1992 ([EAA 2008 hydrologic data report](https://www.edwardsaquifer.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2009_2008HydrologicData.pdf)).

Fast recharge is an advantage when rain falls in the right places and amounts, but it also means aquifer levels and springflow respond to meteorological variability. Pumping during a prolonged rainfall deficit accelerates water-level decline. Low springflow is simultaneously a water-supply, river-flow, and ecological problem because Comal and San Marcos springs support federally protected species.

These drought experiences produced institutions, not merely memories. The Edwards Aquifer Authority began operating in 1996, introduced metering, and in 2002 implemented critical-period management that reduces authorized withdrawals as index-well levels or springflows fall ([EAA historical timeline](https://www.edwardsaquifer.org/about/history/timeline/)). For the San Antonio Pool, Stage 1 is triggered when the ten-day average at J-17 falls below 660 feet above mean sea level, or when Comal or San Marcos springflow crosses specified thresholds ([EAA critical-period management](https://www.edwardsaquifer.org/groundwater-users/critical-period-drought-management/)).

San Antonio has also diversified its supply. In 1995, the Edwards Aquifer provided all of the city utility’s water; SAWS now operates projects drawing on seven sources and describes diversification as protection against drought and source-specific climate impacts ([SAWS water-supply portfolio](https://www.saws.org/your-water/management-sources/)). In 2025, approximately 154,000 acre-feet—47% of the water used by SAWS customers—came from the Edwards. Under a worst-case drought, regulatory and habitat-conservation constraints lead SAWS to assume a firm Edwards yield of about 105,000 acre-feet per year ([SAWS Edwards Aquifer overview](https://www.saws.org/your-water/management-sources/edwards-aquifer/)).

## The next five decades: what is projected through roughly the 2070s?

### Heat: the most robust signal

The city-scale model ensemble projects a dramatic change in the frequency of familiar temperature thresholds. Relative to a 1971–2000 baseline of seven days per year above 100°F, the 2041–2070 average is 46.6 days under RCP4.5 and 61.4 days under RCP8.5. Average summer daytime maximum temperature rises from 94.7°F in the baseline to 98.9°F and 100.1°F, respectively. The study’s “summer maximum” indicator rises from 101.1°F to 107.2°F under the lower pathway and 108.5°F under the higher pathway ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)).

Warm nights increase even more conspicuously. Nights with minimum temperatures above 80°F rise from a modeled historical average of 0.03 per year to 6.5 under RCP4.5 or 15.9 under RCP8.5 in 2041–2070. Freezing nights fall from 21.8 per year to 12.6 or 10.0, respectively ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)).

A newer, peer-reviewed downscaling study for the broader Edwards Aquifer Region, using selected CMIP5 and CMIP6 models, reinforces the direction while showing a wide range of magnitudes. For 2036–2065, it projects average maximum temperatures about 1.68°C–2.18°C higher under intermediate emissions and 2.08°C–2.66°C higher under high emissions. The modeled increase in days at or above 100°F ranges from about 19–43 days under intermediate emissions and 30–51 days under high emissions ([Wootten et al., 2024, *Earth’s Future*](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024EF004716)).

These are regional changes rather than an airport-specific count, and the ranges reflect differences between model generations and subsets. Nevertheless, the agreement in direction is strong: San Antonio should expect a large expansion of its extreme-heat season. The precise count of future 100°F days is uncertain; the emergence of substantially more dangerous heat is not.

### A worked heat example

Consider the city assessment’s mid-century RCP4.5 result:

\[
46.6\ \text{future hot days} - 7.0\ \text{baseline hot days}
= 39.6\ \text{additional days above }100^\circ\text{F per year}.
\]

That is roughly 5.7 additional weeks of triple-digit days each year. Under RCP8.5:

\[
61.4 - 7.0 = 54.4\ \text{additional days},
\]

or about 7.8 weeks. These are 30-year ensemble averages, not a claim that every year will have exactly 47 or 61 hot days. Some years would have fewer, others more. The practical implication is that heat preparedness becomes a seasonal operating condition rather than a response reserved for a few exceptional afternoons.

### Water availability: uncertain rainfall, clearer evaporative stress

The city assessment projects annual precipitation declining from a 1971–2000 baseline of 32.5 inches to approximately 29.6 inches under RCP4.5 and 29.5 inches under RCP8.5 in 2041–2070 ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)). Numerically, that is a reduction of about 9%.

This estimate should not be treated as settled. The newer Edwards Aquifer Region study found that its CMIP5 subset generally projected more annual precipitation while its CMIP6 subset projected less. At mid-century, ensemble-mean changes ranged from an increase of 27.23 millimeters to a decrease of 40.85 millimeters under intermediate emissions, and from a decrease of 14.55 millimeters to an increase of 16.78 millimeters under high emissions ([Wootten et al., 2024](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024EF004716)). That disagreement is evidence of substantial precipitation uncertainty, not evidence that rainfall will remain harmlessly unchanged.

Water stress can intensify even if annual rainfall changes little. Higher temperatures increase potential evapotranspiration—the atmospheric demand for water from soils, vegetation, and open water. Dry soils then amplify heat because less incoming energy is spent evaporating water and more heats the land and air. Greater outdoor water demand can coincide with reduced recharge and drought-related pumping restrictions. The Fifth National Climate Assessment therefore projects increasing drought intensity and more extremely hot days across the Southern Great Plains, with southern and southwestern portions generally becoming drier, while emphasizing that regional precipitation outcomes vary geographically ([Fifth National Climate Assessment, Southern Great Plains](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/26/)).

For the Edwards system, total annual rainfall is only one control. Recharge depends on where, when, and how rain falls; how dry the soil and channel system were beforehand; how much runoff reaches losing streams; and how much groundwater is withdrawn. A heavy storm can generate flood damage without proportionately solving a long drought, especially if its water rapidly runs downstream or falls outside the most effective contributing and recharge areas.

### Extreme rain and the drought–flood paradox

The city assessment projects only modest mid-century changes in its average heavy-rain indicators. Days exceeding two inches increase from 0.53–0.54 per year in 2011–2040 to 0.61 under RCP4.5 or 0.66 under RCP8.5 in 2041–2070. The wettest five-day total rises from about 4.5 inches to 4.57 or 4.86 inches ([City of San Antonio/UTSA climate-projection report](https://www.sanantonio.gov/Portals/0/Files/Sustainability/SAClimateReady/San-Antonio-Climate-Projections.pdf)).

The newer regional study similarly finds a small-to-moderate increase in annual maximum one-day precipitation despite disagreement about annual totals. Its mid-century ensemble changes in the one-day maximum span approximately 8.26–12.01 millimeters under intermediate emissions and 6.06–12.06 millimeters under high emissions ([Wootten et al., 2024](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024EF004716)).

There is no contradiction in projecting both drought stress and heavier downpours. Drought concerns the balance among precipitation, evaporation, soil moisture, recharge, storage, and demand over weeks to years. Flash flooding can arise from a few hours of exceptionally intense rain. A warmer atmosphere can increase moisture availability during favorable storm conditions, while longer dry intervals and stronger evaporation can still leave soils and water supplies stressed between storms.

## What the evidence supports—and what it does not

Several findings are comparatively well established:

1. **Heat will increase strongly.** Both the older city-specific ensemble and newer Edwards-region downscaling agree on higher average maximum temperatures and many more days above 100°F.

2. **Warm nights will become more common.** This increases concern for cumulative heat exposure, cooling demand, and people without dependable air conditioning.

3. **Cold nights will become less frequent, but severe freezes remain possible.** Infrastructure must therefore handle a broader operational challenge: much more heat without assuming winter hazards have disappeared.

4. **Water stress is likely to intensify.** Warming increases evaporative demand and can compound rainfall deficits, while Edwards recharge and permitted supply remain sensitive to drought.

5. **Extreme rainfall remains a major hazard and may intensify.** Neither a drier annual average nor a drought projection implies protection from floods.

Important uncertainties remain. Models disagree about the sign and magnitude of annual precipitation change. A single airport station cannot represent every neighborhood or the entire Edwards contributing and recharge zones. Urban expansion, irrigation, tree cover, pavement, drainage investment, groundwater regulation, conservation, and future supply projects will shape actual impacts. Older CMIP5 projections also should be interpreted alongside newer CMIP6 work rather than treated as the final word.

Finally, projections do not determine damage. The same physical climate can produce different outcomes depending on building quality, shade, public-health outreach, electricity reliability, floodplain development, drainage capacity, water conservation, and access to transportation and cooling.

## Conclusion: the climate profile San Antonio should plan around

San Antonio’s past has already supplied the essential lessons. The 1950s drought showed that prolonged rainfall deficits and pumping can drive the Edwards Aquifer and its springs to critical levels. The 1998 flood showed that a large fraction of normal annual rainfall can arrive in a day. The 111°F record in 2000 showed the upper reach of historical heat, while subsequent regional assessments indicate that extreme heat is moving from exceptional toward recurrent.

Over the next five decades, the clearest change is a much hotter climate: roughly five to eight additional weeks of 100°F days per year by mid-century in the city’s older scenario analysis, accompanied by more very warm nights. Water availability is harder to quantify because rainfall projections disagree, but warming alone raises evaporative stress and worsens the consequences of drought. Heavy-rain projections are less dramatic than the heat projections but generally point upward, preserving or increasing the possibility of severe flash floods.

The useful conclusion is therefore not a single forecast number. San Antonio needs plans that work across a range: conservation and diversified supply for multiyear drought; aquifer and springflow protection; heat-ready housing, workplaces, neighborhoods, and power systems; and drainage and land-use standards that anticipate nonstationary rainfall extremes. The city’s climate history is not merely a record of what happened. It identifies the interacting mechanisms—heat, evaporation, uneven rainfall, rapid runoff, recharge, pumping, and exposure—that will determine whether future extremes become manageable disruptions or cascading crises.
