# Boston: Climate History and Outlook

## Why Boston’s climate is unusually place-specific

Boston’s climate reflects an intersection of continental weather, the Atlantic Ocean, a heavily built urban landscape, and a low-lying harbor shoreline. Massachusetts lies on the eastern edge of North America, where moist maritime air frequently meets hot or cold continental air; the nearby jet stream contributes to rapid weather changes and precipitation in every season. Coastal locations are generally milder in winter than inland Massachusetts, although they remain exposed to nor’easters, tropical systems, and coastal flooding ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Three distinctions are essential before interpreting Boston’s climate history:

1. **Weather** is the atmospheric state over hours or days. A blizzard, heat wave, or rainstorm is weather.
2. **Climate** is the statistical distribution of weather over decades: averages, seasonal cycles, variability, and extremes.
3. A **climate normal** is a standardized multidecade reference, not a prediction of what every year should resemble. The current U.S. standard is generally 1991–2020.

Likewise, an observation and a projection answer different questions. Observations measure what occurred. Projections estimate what could occur under stated assumptions about greenhouse-gas emissions, climate sensitivity, ice-sheet behavior, and local conditions. A projection is neither a weather forecast nor a prophecy.

Boston-specific measurements also should not be conflated with statewide averages. The Logan International Airport weather station represents an exposed coastal location at approximately 42.36°N, 71.01°W and an elevation of only 12 feet. Neighborhoods farther inland, at different elevations, or surrounded by more pavement and less vegetation can be hotter or otherwise climatologically different. Statewide records are valuable for long-term context but combine coastal, inland, urban, rural, lowland, and upland environments.

## The modern baseline: what “normal” Boston weather looks like

NOAA’s 1991–2020 normals for Boston Logan Airport give an annual mean temperature of **51.9°F**, based on an average daily maximum of 59.3°F and minimum of 44.5°F. January, the coldest month by mean temperature, averages 29.9°F; July, the warmest, averages 74.1°F. Average July daily highs and lows are 82.1°F and 66.0°F, while January highs and lows average 36.8°F and 23.1°F ([NOAA Boston Logan 1991–2020 normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00014739)).

These averages conceal considerable variability. Logan normally experiences about **14.1 days per year with a maximum of at least 90°F**, approximately 23.2 days whose maximum remains at or below freezing, and 91.6 nights whose minimum reaches 32°F or lower. A temperature of at least 100°F is rare in the normal record—about one-tenth of a day per year on average—but rarity does not mean impossibility ([NOAA Boston Logan 1991–2020 normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00014739)).

Annual normal precipitation is **43.59 inches**. It is distributed relatively evenly across the calendar rather than being confined to a sharply defined wet season: monthly normals range from 3.21 inches in February to 4.30 inches in December. On average, measurable precipitation of at least 0.01 inch occurs on about 128 days annually, precipitation of at least 0.50 inch on 29 days, and precipitation of at least 1 inch on roughly 10 days ([NOAA Boston Logan 1991–2020 normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00014739)).

The airport’s normal annual snowfall is approximately **49.2 inches**, but snowfall is especially variable. A warmer climate can reduce average snow or shorten the snow season without eliminating major snowstorms. Boston’s 2014–2015 season illustrates the difference between a long-term trend and an individual extreme: the city recorded 110 inches of seasonal snowfall even though Massachusetts’ winter temperature ranked only the 30th coldest in the statewide record ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)). A single snowy winter therefore does not disprove warming, just as one heat wave does not by itself establish a climatic trend.

## What the historical record shows

### Warming is established, although its local expression varies

The clearest regional signal is warming. NOAA concludes that Massachusetts’ average temperature increased by almost **3.5°F from the beginning of the twentieth century through 2020**. The statewide number of days reaching at least 90°F remained mostly near or below its 1950–2020 average through 2009 but stayed above average after 2010; the 2015–2020 multiyear average, 11.5 hot days per year, was the highest in that record ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Warm nights are changing as well. NOAA defines a warm night in this analysis as one with a minimum temperature of at least 70°F. Massachusetts averaged 5.3 such nights per year over 1950–2020, but their frequency rose steadily from the mid-1990s, reaching its highest multiyear value during 2015–2020. Very cold nights, by contrast, have generally been below average since the early 1990s ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Nighttime warming matters because the human body and buildings lose less accumulated heat when nights remain warm. It also reveals the influence of the built environment. Pavement, roofs, and masonry absorb solar energy by day and release it after sunset, while limited vegetation reduces evaporative cooling. Boston’s heat assessment therefore describes the entire city as an urban heat island, though not a spatially uniform one ([City of Boston Heat Resilience Solutions](https://content.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience/heat-resilience-solutions-boston)).

The correct conclusion is not that every recent year is warmer than the preceding one. Atmospheric circulation, ocean conditions, snow cover, and random variability still produce cold months and severe winters. The established finding is that the distribution has shifted: average conditions are warmer, hot conditions occur more often, warm nights have increased, and extreme cold has generally become less frequent.

### Boston and Massachusetts have become wetter overall, but not uniformly

Statewide annual precipitation is highly variable. From 1895 through 2020, individual Massachusetts annual totals ranged from roughly 29 to 61 inches. Nevertheless, precipitation since 1970 averaged about **4.7 inches more per year** than during 1895–1969. The wettest consecutive ten-year interval was 2005–2014, averaging approximately 51 inches annually, compared with the 1895–2020 mean of 45.4 inches ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

That does not mean every season became steadily wetter. NOAA finds no long-term trend in total summer precipitation across 1895–2020, and Massachusetts experienced extreme drought in 2016–2017 and again in 2020. A warmer, wetter climate can still have damaging dry intervals because rainfall timing, evaporation, soil moisture, and water demand matter alongside annual totals ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Heavy rainfall provides a stronger warning signal than a simple statewide annual average. Massachusetts’ long-term average was 2.1 days per year with at least 2 inches of precipitation, and 2005–2014 had about 30% more such events than the long-term mean. Yet the record is short and variable enough that NOAA does not identify a simple monotonic statewide trend in that particular threshold metric. In March 2010, three intense storms produced more than 19 inches near Jamaica Plain and several other eastern Massachusetts locations, contributing to regional flood damage estimated above $2 billion ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

This combination—higher annual totals, more evidence of very heavy rainfall, and continuing drought—is physically coherent. Warming increases evaporation from land and water, which can worsen drying between storms. At the same time, warmer air can contain more water vapor, increasing the moisture available when a storm does develop. Storm tracks, convection, and local circulation determine whether that thermodynamic potential becomes rainfall at a particular place, so confidence is higher in the direction of change for heavy precipitation than in the exact magnitude at a particular Boston drainage basin.

### Relative sea level is already rising

For a coastal city, the relevant quantity is **relative sea level**: the height of the ocean relative to the adjacent land. It includes both changes in ocean height and vertical land motion. A tide gauge therefore measures the combined local result rather than global mean sea level alone.

NOAA’s Boston Harbor gauge recorded a linear relative mean sea-level trend of **2.97 ± 0.15 millimeters per year from 1921 through 2024**, equivalent to **0.97 ± 0.05 feet per century** if that historical linear rate were simply extended ([NOAA U.S. tide-gauge trends](https://prod.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/sltrends/data/USStationsLinearSeaLevelTrends.pdf)). Multiplying the central rate by 104 years gives about 309 millimeters, or 12.2 inches, of fitted change across the period. That calculation describes the historical trend; it is not a twenty-first-century forecast, because future sea-level rise is expected to be nonlinear.

Rising mean water levels elevate the starting point for tides and storm surge. Consequently, a storm need not become proportionally stronger for a given flood elevation to be exceeded more often. NOAA’s Massachusetts assessment reports that sea-level rise has already produced a gradual increase in nuisance-level tidal flooding at Boston ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

## Mechanisms connecting global warming to Boston

The primary mechanism behind long-term warming is the increased atmospheric concentration of greenhouse gases, which reduces the rate at which Earth loses infrared energy to space. Boston’s outcome is then shaped by regional circulation, the Atlantic Ocean, land cover, and urban form.

Extreme rainfall has a related thermodynamic mechanism. The Clausius–Clapeyron relationship implies that saturation vapor pressure increases approximately exponentially with temperature:

\[
e_s(T) \approx e_0
\exp\left[\frac{L_v}{R_v}
\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac{1}{T}\right)\right],
\]

where \(e_s\) is saturation vapor pressure, \(L_v\) is latent heat of vaporization, \(R_v\) is the gas constant for water vapor, and temperature \(T\) is expressed in kelvins. Near ordinary surface temperatures, this corresponds to roughly a 7% increase in atmospheric moisture capacity per 1°C of warming. It is not a rule that every storm’s rainfall must increase by exactly 7%; circulation, instability, storm duration, and moisture supply also matter. Boston’s 2016 research assessment found agreement among regional model studies on increasing extreme precipitation but much less agreement on the magnitude, partly because global models do not explicitly resolve individual thunderstorms or hurricanes well ([Boston Research Advisory Group report](https://www.boston.gov/sites/default/files/file/document_files/2016/12/brag_report_-_final.pdf)).

Sea level rises through several processes: seawater expands as it warms; mountain glaciers and ice sheets lose land-based ice to the ocean; land-water storage changes; regional ocean circulation redistributes water; and land itself moves. The geographic source of melting matters because removing a large ice mass changes gravity, Earth’s rotation, and crustal deformation. Boston’s 2016 assessment estimated that the city receives a smaller-than-global sea-level signal from Greenland melt but an amplified signal from West Antarctic ice loss. It also incorporated estimated local subsidence of 0.8 ± 0.3 millimeters per year ([Boston Research Advisory Group report](https://www.boston.gov/sites/default/files/file/document_files/2016/12/brag_report_-_final.pdf)).

## Outlook through the twenty-first century

### Heat: high confidence in more warming, scenario-dependent magnitude

The robust result is that Boston will become hotter. NOAA’s statewide CMIP5 assessment projects that, under a high-emissions pathway, parts of Massachusetts could gain as many as **40 additional days above 90°F by mid-century**. Even under a lower-emissions pathway, annual temperatures are projected most likely to exceed the state’s historical record range by mid-century, although individual model estimates span a wide range ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Boston’s 2022 heat plan translates the regional science into city-scale planning figures. It reports a historical baseline near 10 days above 90°F per summer and estimates that by the 2070s Boston could experience approximately **60 such days per year if emissions continue at a high rate**, versus roughly **40 days under aggressive emissions reductions**. It also estimates up to about 130 days above 80°F under continued high emissions ([City of Boston Heat Resilience Solutions](https://content.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience/heat-resilience-solutions-boston)).

These are conditional projections, not promised annual counts. Differences between the historical values in various sources—about 10 days in an older planning baseline versus 14.1 days in the 1991–2020 Logan normal—reflect different stations, periods, and analytical purposes. Updating the baseline to include recent warm decades raises the “normal” against which future change is described.

Heat exposure is also finer-grained than the airport record. Boston’s modeled July 2019 heat-wave analysis found higher daytime temperatures where solar exposure was high and vegetation or ventilation was limited; nighttime heat was greatest in dense areas with restricted sky view and substantial thermal storage. In the model, parts of Chinatown remained above the study’s heat thresholds for 37 hours, with modeled afternoon air temperatures of 104–107°F and nighttime temperatures above 90°F across much of the neighborhood ([City of Boston Heat Resilience Solutions](https://content.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience/heat-resilience-solutions-boston)). These were modeled neighborhood conditions for a selected intense event, not a long-term observational average.

### Rain, snow, and inland flooding

NOAA projects increasing winter and spring precipitation in Massachusetts. Under its higher-emissions mid-century scenario, spring precipitation increases by 5–10% in eastern and southeastern Massachusetts. The frequency of extreme precipitation events is projected to more than double by late century, while warmer winters could cause 12–30% more cold-season precipitation to fall as rain rather than snow ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

Rainfall projections require careful interpretation. A “100-year storm” does not occur on a schedule. If annual exceedances are independent and the annual probability is \(p\), the conventional return period is

\[
R=\frac{1}{p}.
\]

Thus \(p=0.01\) corresponds to a 100-year return period. The probability of at least one such event in \(n\) years is

\[
P(\text{one or more})=1-(1-p)^n.
\]

With \(p=0.01\), the probability over a 30-year mortgage is about 26%, not 1%. Climate change further complicates this terminology because \(p\) is no longer stationary: a rainfall depth historically associated with a 1% annual chance may become more frequent.

Boston’s research advisory group reviewed city and regional analyses projecting increases in five-day precipitation amounts of **0–30% by 2030** and **10–40% by 2070**, relative to the studies’ baselines. The report cautioned that estimates depended on older model ensembles, statistical downscaling, assumed linear relationships with global temperature, and limited validation of rare local events ([Boston Research Advisory Group report](https://www.boston.gov/sites/default/files/file/document_files/2016/12/brag_report_-_final.pdf)). Those ranges are useful for stress-testing infrastructure, but they should not be treated as precise rainfall forecasts for every neighborhood.

Snowfall is expected to decline on average as winter temperatures rise and more precipitation falls as rain. Nevertheless, heavy snowstorms remain plausible because cold-enough storms can still draw on abundant moisture. Boston therefore faces a transitional climate in which average snow cover diminishes but disruptive snowfall does not disappear.

### Coastal flooding and sea-level uncertainty

NOAA gives a broad likely global sea-level range of **1–4 feet by 2100**, but Boston’s local relative sea level can differ because of land motion, ocean dynamics, and ice-sheet fingerprints ([NOAA Massachusetts State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ma/)).

The 2016 Boston assessment used 10,000 probabilistic samples and a 2000 baseline. It projected a likely 2050 relative rise of approximately **19–45 centimeters** across low- to high-emissions pathways, indicating little scenario separation in the first half of the century. By 2100 the estimates diverged: **56–117 centimeters under RCP2.6**, **75–156 centimeters under RCP4.5**, and **97–226 centimeters under RCP8.5** ([Boston Research Advisory Group report](https://www.boston.gov/sites/default/files/file/document_files/2016/12/brag_report_-_final.pdf)).

These ranges should be treated as an important historical planning assessment, not the final scientific word. The report itself explicitly called for periodic revision because Antarctic ice-sheet physics and other constraints were evolving. Its RCP scenarios and CMIP5-era models also predate newer generations of climate assessment. What remains well established is the direction of change, the near-term need to plan for higher water, and the widening late-century uncertainty—especially from emissions and ice-sheet behavior.

## Risk is climate hazard multiplied by exposure and vulnerability

A climate hazard becomes a disaster through contact with people, buildings, infrastructure, and ecosystems. The same air temperature is more dangerous for an older adult living alone without affordable air conditioning than for a healthy person with access to a cooled building. Similarly, the same flood elevation has different consequences for a park, an electrical substation, a hospital entrance, and a basement apartment.

Boston’s heat plan separates risk into **exposure**, **sensitivity**, and **adaptive capacity**. Exposure concerns the intensity and duration of heat; sensitivity includes age and chronic health conditions; adaptive capacity includes access to air conditioning, affordable electricity, transportation, social support, and information in an accessible language ([City of Boston Heat Resilience Solutions](https://content.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience/heat-resilience-solutions-boston)).

Present vulnerability also reflects historical policy. During the city’s modeled heat wave, formerly redlined areas were calculated to be 7.5°F hotter by day and 3.6°F hotter at night than Boston overall. Those areas had 20% less parkland and 40% less tree canopy than areas historically graded “A: Best” by mortgage-security maps ([City of Boston Heat Resilience Solutions](https://content.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience/heat-resilience-solutions-boston)). This does not prove that redlining alone caused every present-day temperature difference, but it is strong evidence that climate risk is spatially structured by past land-use, investment, and housing decisions.

## What adaptation can and cannot accomplish

Boston has completed coastal-resilience planning for its 47-mile shoreline and a citywide heat plan, while current programs address coastal flooding, stormwater, green infrastructure, buildings, emergency response, and tree canopy ([City of Boston climate-resilience program](https://www.boston.gov/departments/climate-resilience)). Useful measures include shaded streets, healthy tree canopy, cool or reflective roofs, weatherized buildings, affordable indoor cooling, accessible cooling centers, heat-warning systems, worker protections, floodable open space, restored wetlands, raised or protected critical infrastructure, and drainage designed for future rather than historical rainfall.

Adaptation should be evaluated as a portfolio. Trees can reduce local heat, intercept some rainfall, improve air quality, and provide public space, but they require soil, water, maintenance, and time to mature. Seawalls can protect particular assets, but poorly designed barriers may shift water elsewhere, obstruct access, damage habitat, or be overtopped as sea level rises. Air conditioning protects health but can raise electricity demand and emissions unless paired with efficient buildings, clean power, and grid resilience.

Mitigation and adaptation are complementary. Adaptation reduces harm from warming already experienced or committed. Emissions reductions limit how far the climate shifts, with their largest effect on late-century heat and sea-level outcomes. Boston cannot determine the global emissions pathway alone, but local decisions can reduce both emissions and vulnerability.

## Limits of the evidence

Several uncertainties should remain visible:

- Logan Airport is not a complete proxy for all Boston neighborhoods.
- Statewide trends do not automatically specify city-scale trends.
- Rare extremes are difficult to estimate from short observational records.
- Model resolution remains limited for thunderstorms, tropical cyclones, and fine-scale urban conditions.
- Different studies use different baselines, thresholds, models, and emissions scenarios.
- Sea-level projections depend strongly on uncertain ice-sheet dynamics after mid-century.
- Adaptation and demographic change will alter future impacts even if the physical hazard follows a given projection.

These limitations do not erase the central findings. Boston and Massachusetts have warmed substantially; hot days and warm nights have become more common; annual precipitation has increased, with evidence of intensifying heavy rainfall; and relative sea level in Boston Harbor has risen for more than a century. Continued warming, heavier precipitation extremes, reduced average snow, and additional sea-level rise are strongly supported. The exact late-century magnitude—especially for extreme rainfall and coastal water levels—remains conditional on global emissions, ice-sheet response, regional circulation, and local adaptation.
