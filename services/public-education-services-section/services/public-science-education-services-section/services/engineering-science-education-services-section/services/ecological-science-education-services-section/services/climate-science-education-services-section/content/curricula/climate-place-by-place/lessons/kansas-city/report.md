# Kansas City: Climate History and Outlook

## Why place and scale matter

Kansas City’s climate cannot be described by a single number or jurisdiction. The metropolitan area straddles Missouri and Kansas, while the official long-running weather station used for Kansas City climate reporting has been Kansas City International Airport in Missouri since 1972. That station—NOAA identifier USW00003947—is northwest of the urban core at an elevation of 1,005 feet and coordinates 39.2972°N, 94.7306°W. Consequently, its measurements provide a consistent regional benchmark but do not capture every neighborhood’s temperature, rainfall, or flood exposure. [NOAA’s station normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947) and the [National Weather Service’s Kansas City records page](https://www.weather.gov/eax/kcrecnorm) document the station and observational framework.

That distinction is essential. “Kansas City climate” may refer to measurements at the airport, conditions within Kansas City, Missouri, conditions in Kansas City, Kansas, or the wider metropolitan area. Statewide records are broader still. This report therefore uses:

1. airport observations for the present-day seasonal baseline;
2. Missouri and Kansas records for long historical context;
3. regional and city-scale studies for projected change and vulnerability.

These sources answer related but not identical questions. A statewide trend should not be interpreted as a precise trend for a particular neighborhood, and a model projection for Kansas City, Missouri, should not be mistaken for a weather forecast or an exact prediction for every part of the metro.

## Weather, climate, normals, and projections

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere over hours or days. **Climate** describes the statistical distribution of weather—averages, variability, seasonal cycles, and extremes—over much longer periods. A cold week can occur in a warming climate, just as a single destructive downpour does not by itself establish a long-term precipitation trend.

A **climate normal** is a standardized average for a defined period, ordinarily 30 years. NOAA recommends its 1991–2020 normals for current comparisons and explains that these normals are updated by decade; they are reference values rather than immutable descriptions of what the future will be like. [NOAA’s U.S. Climate Normals documentation](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals) describes the calculation periods, source observations, and intended uses.

A **climate projection** is different from both a normal and a forecast. It is a conditional estimate produced by running climate models under specified assumptions about future greenhouse-gas emissions and other influences. “Under a higher-emissions pathway” means that the result depends on that pathway; it is not a claim that society is certain to follow it. Projections also contain uncertainty from natural variability, differences among climate models, local downscaling, and future human choices.

## The physical setting

Kansas City lies in the continental interior, far from an ocean that might moderate rapid temperature changes. Missouri’s climate summary describes the state as open to cold northern air and warm, moist Gulf air because no major mountain barrier blocks north–south movement. That circulation helps produce large seasonal contrasts, humid summer heat, winter cold, thunderstorms, and sharp temperature changes. [NOAA’s Missouri State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) identifies these air-mass influences and documents the state’s pronounced north–south climatic gradient.

Moisture from the Gulf of Mexico supplies many warm-season rain events, while frontal systems and convection organize that moisture into thunderstorms. The resulting rainfall is uneven in both space and time: one neighborhood can receive a downpour while another gets little rain. The city’s position near major rivers adds riverine flood exposure, but short, intense rainfall can also cause flash flooding away from mapped river floodplains. The Mid-America Regional Council, or MARC, identifies river flooding, flash flooding, and drainage failures in older or disrupted stormwater systems as distinct parts of the region’s flood problem. [MARC’s regional Climate Risk and Vulnerability Assessment](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) notes that flash floods are especially dangerous because water rises rapidly and warning time can be short.

## The 1991–2020 baseline

At Kansas City International Airport, the 1991–2020 normal annual temperature is **54.7°F**. The normal daily maximum averaged over the year is **65.0°F**, and the normal daily minimum is **44.5°F**. January is normally the coldest month, with a mean of **29.0°F**, an average daily high of **38.4°F**, and an average low of **19.5°F**. July is normally the warmest, averaging **78.2°F**, with a daily high of **88.3°F** and low of **68.1°F**. [NOAA’s Kansas City airport monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947) provide these values.

The same normals show that an average year has about **36.3 days with a maximum of at least 90°F**, **2.6 days reaching at least 100°F**, **25.8 days whose maximum remains at or below freezing**, **106.8 nights at or below 32°F**, and **3.8 nights at or below 0°F**. These are averages over 30 years, not quotas: individual years can be far above or below them. [NOAA’s station table](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947) reports the threshold frequencies.

Normal annual precipitation at the airport is **39.30 inches**, spread across about **103.7 days with at least 0.01 inch**. Precipitation is strongly weighted toward the warm season. May normally receives **5.32 inches**, June **5.25 inches**, and July **4.58 inches**, whereas January averages only **1.16 inches**. On average, Kansas City records about **10.5 days per year with at least one inch of precipitation**. [NOAA’s precipitation normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947) quantify this seasonal cycle.

Normal annual snowfall is **18.2 inches** at the airport. February has the largest monthly normal, **5.9 inches**, followed by January at **4.9 inches** and December at **4.0 inches**. Yet snowfall is intrinsically variable: NOAA’s modeled probability levels for annual snowfall span **3.4 inches at the 25th percentile, 11.2 inches at the median, and 25.0 inches at the 75th percentile**. The difference between the 18.2-inch arithmetic mean and 11.2-inch median illustrates a skewed distribution in which snowy years pull the mean upward. [NOAA’s snowfall normals and probability table](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947) support these figures.

The station also averages **1,363 cooling degree days** and **5,071 heating degree days** using a 65°F base. A daily degree-day calculation can be represented as

\[
CDD_{65}=\max(T_{\text{mean}}-65^\circ\mathrm{F},0)
\]

\[
HDD_{65}=\max(65^\circ\mathrm{F}-T_{\text{mean}},0).
\]

Annual totals sum these daily values. They are simplified indicators of weather-related cooling and heating demand, not direct measurements of electricity or fuel consumption. Building efficiency, behavior, humidity, equipment, and energy prices all affect actual demand. The station totals and base temperature are reported in [NOAA’s normals table](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00003947).

## What the historical record establishes

The clearest regional finding is long-term warming, but the seasonal history is not a smooth upward line. Missouri warmed by almost **1°F from the beginning of the twentieth century through 2020**, with much of the warming concentrated in winter and spring. The 2000s were warmer than other historical periods except the early-1930s Dust Bowl era. Missouri’s recent summer warmth has been expressed especially through higher nighttime minima, while average daytime summer maxima remained nearer the long-term average. [NOAA’s Missouri assessment](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) reports these distinctions.

The history on the Kansas side is similar in its complexity. Kansas experienced some of its warmest multiyear spring conditions after 2000, while summer averages were near to above the long-term mean. Nevertheless, the state’s hottest summers remained **1934 and 1936**, during the Dust Bowl. [NOAA’s Kansas State Climate Summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ks/) documents the seasonal time series.

This apparent tension—overall warming alongside extraordinary 1930s daytime heat—is not a contradiction. Dust Bowl drought and severe land degradation suppressed evaporative cooling and intensified daytime temperatures. Modern warming, by contrast, is evident across longer averages and increasingly warm nights. Missouri’s summer minimum temperatures since the mid-1990s have remained near or above the long-term average, and the 2010–2020 period reached the highest multiyear nighttime levels in the state series. [NOAA’s Missouri summary](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) compares maximum, minimum, and mean summer temperatures.

Cold extremes have declined. In both Missouri and Kansas, multiyear counts of nights at or below **0°F** were below their respective long-term averages after 1990. This does not imply that dangerous cold has disappeared; it means that such nights occurred less frequently in the recent multiyear record than in the full twentieth-century reference. [The Missouri](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) and [Kansas](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ks/) state summaries show the observed cold-night series.

Precipitation has also changed, although totals remain highly variable. Missouri’s annual precipitation was generally above average after 1990, but summer precipitation had no consistent long-term trend through 2020. Kansas precipitation varied sharply from year to year; annual totals were generally above average after 1985, and two-inch precipitation events showed a general increase, especially in eastern Kansas. [NOAA’s Missouri](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) and [Kansas](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ks/) assessments document these observations.

The broader Midwest evidence strengthens the heavy-rainfall finding. The amount of precipitation falling in the region’s most intense **1% of events increased by 45% between 1958 and 2021**. That statistic describes a regional change in the heaviest events, not a 45% increase in every Kansas City storm or in annual rainfall. [The Fifth National Climate Assessment’s Midwest chapter](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/24/) reports the regional calculation.

## Mechanisms behind the emerging pattern

A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapor, providing storms with a larger moisture supply when circulation, instability, and lifting are favorable. That thermodynamic mechanism helps explain why extreme precipitation can intensify even where changes in seasonal or annual rainfall are smaller or uncertain. Local outcomes still depend on storm tracks, soil moisture, land cover, and atmospheric dynamics; warming alone does not determine where or when a storm forms.

Heat and drought also interact. Evaporation and plant water loss generally increase as temperature and atmospheric moisture demand rise. During a rain-free interval, warmer conditions can therefore remove soil moisture more rapidly. A place can consequently experience increasing annual precipitation and worsening drought risk: more water may arrive in intense episodes, while longer dry intervals and greater evaporative demand deplete water between storms. The [Fifth National Climate Assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/24/) concludes that evapotranspiration has become an increasingly important drought driver in much of the Midwest, while precipitation deficits remain especially important in the southwestern part of the region.

Urban form adds another layer. Pavement and buildings store heat, reduced vegetation limits shade and evaporative cooling, and waste heat from vehicles and buildings raises local temperatures. MARC reports that heat exposure is greatest in neighborhoods with more heat-absorbing impervious cover and less tree canopy. It also emphasizes that age, health, outdoor work, transportation access, and access to air-conditioning affect whether exposure becomes illness. [MARC’s vulnerability assessment](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) connects these physical and social factors.

## The outlook: robust conclusions and conditional numbers

Several conclusions are well supported across scales:

- Kansas City will continue to warm under all assessed emissions pathways, with more warming under higher emissions.
- Hot extremes and warm nights will become more frequent or intense.
- Cold extremes will generally become less frequent, though damaging winter weather will remain possible.
- Heavy precipitation is expected to intensify.
- Annual and seasonal precipitation changes are less certain than temperature changes, particularly in summer.
- Drought can intensify despite stable or rising annual rainfall because higher temperatures accelerate moisture loss and rainfall may become more episodic.

The quantitative range matters. For Missouri, models assessed by NOAA project end-of-century warming relative to the **1901–1960 average** of roughly **2.2°F to 9.0°F under a lower-emissions pathway** and **6.7°F to 14.9°F under a higher pathway**, with substantial model spread. These are statewide ranges and use an older historical baseline, so they should not be added directly to the Kansas City airport’s 1991–2020 normal. [NOAA’s Missouri projection figure and explanation](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) specify the baseline, pathways, and ranges.

Seasonal precipitation projections differ across the state line. Under a higher-emissions pathway, most of Missouri was projected to receive approximately **10%–15% more spring precipitation by midcentury** relative to the late twentieth century, with statistically significant increases across much of northern Missouri. Kansas, meanwhile, was projected to receive approximately **5%–10% less summer precipitation**, although the projected decrease was statistically significant mainly in central Kansas. [NOAA’s Missouri](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/mo/) and [Kansas](https://statesummaries.ncics.org/chapter/ks/) summaries show these CMIP5-based maps. Kansas City lies near the geographic and climatic transition between those patterns, which is one reason a simple “wetter” or “drier” label is inadequate.

MARC’s local assessment provides more specific—but more conditional—figures derived from an earlier Kansas City study. Under its high-growth emissions scenario, average annual temperature rises from a modeled historical value of **56.5°F to 60.3°F by midcentury and 64.4°F by 2100**. Days above **105°F** increase from **0.7 per year to 6.3 by midcentury and 21.9 by century’s end**. The modeled heat-wave daytime temperature reaches **111.4°F**, while the corresponding nighttime temperature reaches **90.2°F** by 2100. Cooling degree days nearly double, while heating demand declines by an estimated **27%**. [MARC’s regional assessment](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) reports these scenario results.

The same local study projects annual precipitation increasing from **38.8 to 44.6 inches** by 2100. The maximum one-day total rises from **3.4 to 4.0 inches**, maximum five-day precipitation from **5.5 to 7.0 inches**, and maximum 15-day precipitation from **7.5 to 10.4 inches**. Days exceeding **1.5 inches** increase from **5.0 to 5.6 by midcentury and 9.3 by 2100**. Yet the maximum annual run of consecutive dry days also increases, from **30.9 to 39.5 days**. [MARC’s projection summary](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) illustrates how heavier rainfall and longer dry spells can coexist.

These local numbers should be treated as scenario outputs, not precise predictions. The MARC report itself explains that they originate in a 2016 analysis of Kansas City, Missouri, under two emissions scenarios and assumes that the results are relevant across the wider region. Newer model generations, alternative downscaling methods, different baselines, and actual emissions could produce different values. [MARC describes the projection source and geographic assumption](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf). The most defensible lesson is therefore about direction and risk: warming and heavier extremes are more certain than the exact temperature of a future heat wave or the precise number of future downpours.

## Consequences for people and systems

More intense rainfall does not translate mechanically into a particular flood depth. Flooding also depends on antecedent soil saturation, drainage capacity, watershed shape, river level, pavement, channel modification, and where a storm occurs. Nevertheless, increasing short-duration rainfall raises the loading on storm sewers, roads, bridges, streams, and wastewater systems. MARC identifies housing displacement, mold, sewer backup, stream erosion, flooded transportation routes, and interrupted emergency access among the region’s principal flood concerns. [The regional vulnerability assessment](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) assigns high potential impacts to housing, health, transportation, water systems, agriculture, and land-use planning.

Heat risk combines exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Higher nighttime temperatures are particularly important because buildings and bodies receive less relief after daytime heat. MARC’s review found **27 reported heat-related deaths and 32 injuries in its planning area from 2000 through 2019**, while caution is warranted because event databases may not capture every heat-related illness or death. [MARC cites NOAA Storm Events data in its heat profile](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf).

The distribution of risk is unequal. MARC constructed a socioeconomic-stress index from census-tract measures of nonwhite population, population below 200% of the poverty level, residents under five and over 65, and renter-occupied housing. It estimated that **13% of the planning-area population** lived in tracts classified as high or highest stress, concentrated principally in the urban cores of Kansas City, Kansas, and Kansas City, Missouri. The index is a screening tool, not a complete measure of individual vulnerability; MARC explicitly notes that local assessments may need additional indicators. [MARC’s mapping methodology and results](https://www.marc.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/Climate-Risk-and-Vulnerability-Assessment.pdf) explain the variables, z-score approach, and limitation.

For agriculture and ecosystems around the metropolitan area, alternating wet and dry extremes may matter more than a modest change in annual rainfall. Wet springs can delay fieldwork and increase erosion or nutrient loss; summer heat and soil-moisture deficits can then stress crops. Across the Midwest, the Fifth National Climate Assessment judges changes in crop production likely but assigns only medium confidence because effects vary among crops, soils, management systems, and locations. [The Midwest assessment](https://nca2023.globalchange.gov/chapter/24/) explicitly describes both the evidence and uncertainty.

## Reading Kansas City’s climate future responsibly

Three comparisons should not be conflated.

First, **normal versus record**: a 30-year normal describes typical conditions, while a record identifies an exceptional observation over a much longer and sometimes station-changing history.

Second, **observation versus projection**: observed trends describe what instruments recorded; projections describe what models simulate under assumptions.

Third, **regional versus local**: statewide and Midwest findings establish context, but neighborhood outcomes depend on topography, land cover, infrastructure, housing, and access to protective resources.

The evidence supports a clear overall interpretation. Kansas City retains a highly variable continental climate, including severe cold, snow, thunderstorms, floods, drought, and humid heat. Against that variable background, the long-term distribution is shifting toward higher average temperatures, warmer nights, fewer extreme-cold nights, and heavier precipitation extremes. Annual precipitation may increase, yet summers can still bring longer dry intervals and greater soil-moisture stress. The magnitude of late-century change remains strongly contingent on emissions and uncertain model details, but the direction of warming and the growing importance of heat and heavy-rainfall management are established findings.
