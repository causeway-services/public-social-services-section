# San Francisco’s Changing Climate: From Fog and Winter Rain to Heat, Flooding, and Water-Supply Risk

## The central question

San Francisco is famous for a climate that seems almost engineered to resist extremes: cool summers, mild winters, afternoon winds, and fog spilling through the Golden Gate. Yet that familiar profile is not fixed. Temperatures are rising, relative sea level is climbing, damaging heat is becoming more likely, and the Sierra Nevada snowpack that supports the city’s imported water supply is changing. Rainfall remains highly variable, while the future of coastal fog is more complicated—and less certain—than the simple claim that it is disappearing.

What, then, might San Francisco experience over the next 50 years?

The most defensible answer is not that the city will abruptly acquire the climate of inland California. The Pacific Ocean, coastal upwelling, marine air, hills, and the Golden Gate will continue to moderate conditions. Instead, warming is shifting the probabilities around the city’s unusual baseline: hot days that were once rare become less rare; higher seas allow ordinary tides and storms to reach farther inland; warmer winters store less precipitation as Sierra snow; and rainfall may alternate between longer dry periods and more intense storms without a clear long-term increase or decrease in annual totals.

Understanding those changes requires separating four things:

1. **Weather**, meaning individual events such as a heat wave or atmospheric river.
2. **Climate normals**, meaning averages over a standard period, usually 30 years.
3. **Observed trends**, measured in historical records.
4. **Projections**, which are conditional model results rather than exact predictions.

It also requires respecting scale. A record from San Francisco International Airport is not a perfect description of the city’s western neighborhoods, while a regional fog index extending hundreds of kilometers along the coast cannot prove what happened on every block in the Sunset District.

## Prerequisites: why San Francisco’s climate is so localized

San Francisco has a Mediterranean precipitation regime: most rain falls during the cool season, while summers are usually dry. NOAA’s downtown climate description reports roughly 20 inches of average annual precipitation, with about 80% falling from November through March and little or none from June through September. The same source emphasizes that the city’s 49-square-mile area contains unusually large differences in temperature, sunshine, and cloudiness because of its terrain and exposure to both the Pacific and the bay ([NOAA Annual Local Climatological Data for San Francisco Downtown](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/access/cebrequests/2023lcdannual/01202313SFOC.pdf)).

Those differences are not incidental. Hills approaching 1,000 feet partially block marine clouds, while gaps allow fog to penetrate inland. NOAA estimates that possible summer sunshine ranges from only about 25–35% near the ocean to 70–80% in the city’s sunniest lee-side areas. Typical summer maximum temperatures are consequently in the lower 60s °F near the ocean but in the upper 60s °F in sunnier eastern districts ([NOAA Annual Local Climatological Data](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/access/cebrequests/2023lcdannual/01202313SFOC.pdf)).

The basic fog mechanism begins offshore. Persistent northwesterly winds encourage coastal upwelling, bringing cold subsurface water toward the surface. Moist marine air cools over that water, producing low cloud or fog beneath a warmer, drier layer aloft. At the same time, strong summer heating in the Central Valley contributes to an inland pressure gradient that draws cool marine air through the Golden Gate. NOAA describes the resulting daily pattern as fog moving inland during the late afternoon or evening, persisting overnight, and often evaporating during the following morning ([NOAA Annual Local Climatological Data](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/access/cebrequests/2023lcdannual/01202313SFOC.pdf)).

This mechanism explains two apparent paradoxes. First, San Francisco’s warmest season is not necessarily midsummer: average warmth often peaks in September or October, when fog diminishes and continental air can move westward. Second, regional warming does not translate evenly into local warming. Changes in ocean temperature, upwelling winds, inland heat, cloud height, and the depth of the marine layer can reinforce or oppose one another.

## What has already changed?

### Temperature: fluctuations around a rising background

The modern normal remains mild. At San Francisco International Airport, NOAA’s 1991–2020 normals give an annual mean temperature of 58.7°F, an average daily maximum of 66.9°F, and an average daily minimum of 50.6°F. September has the highest monthly mean in that airport record—65.3°F—rather than July, whose mean is 64.0°F ([NOAA 1991–2020 Monthly Normals for SFO](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00023234)).

A climate normal is a benchmark, not a statement that every year resembles it. San Francisco’s temperatures fluctuate with ocean conditions, fog, winds, and large-scale circulation. A cool, cloudy summer can therefore occur within a long-term warming trend, just as a wet winter does not by itself reverse a drought.

The strongest conclusion from the available evidence is that the probability distribution is shifting toward warmth. The San Francisco Department of Public Health defines a local extreme-heat day as one exceeding 85°F, corresponding to the upper 2% of temperatures in the 1961–1990 reference period. Its assessment reports that such events are becoming more frequent and are projected to increase further ([San Francisco Heat and Air Quality Resilience report](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/HAQR-2305221_0.pdf)).

Heat is especially consequential in a city adapted to cool weather. Many buildings were not designed around routine air-conditioning, and indoor temperatures can remain elevated after outdoor temperatures fall. The public-health report accordingly treats building conditions, social isolation, age, health, and access to cooling as components of risk rather than assuming that the same outdoor temperature produces the same harm everywhere ([San Francisco Heat and Air Quality Resilience report](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/HAQR-2305221_0.pdf)).

### Precipitation: large variability, weak evidence for a simple trend

San Francisco possesses a very long rainfall record, beginning in 1849, while its temperature observations began in 1875 ([NOAA’s *Climate of San Francisco* catalog record](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/7276)). The rainfall history shows the defining features of California hydroclimate: strong seasonality, large year-to-year variability, and sequences of wet and dry winters.

What it does **not** justify is a confident claim that San Francisco is simply becoming steadily wetter or steadily drier. A peer-reviewed analysis of 20 climate projections across California’s hydrologic regions found statistically significant annual-precipitation trends in only a small minority of simulations; most projections also did not produce significant trends in wet-season precipitation through 2099 ([Huang and Ullrich, “Projected Changes in Precipitation, Temperature, and Drought across California’s Hydrologic Regions”](https://www.mdpi.com/2225-1154/6/2/31)).

That uncertainty in annual totals does not mean precipitation risk is unchanged. A warmer atmosphere can alter storm intensity, rain-versus-snow partitioning, evaporation, and the spacing of storms even if the annual average moves little. Thus “no robust trend in total rainfall” and “greater risk from drought or intense precipitation” are not contradictory statements. Annual totals discard information about timing and intensity.

### Fog: evidence of decline, but not a settled citywide forecast

One influential observational study reconstructed summer fog frequency along coastal northern California from 1951 through 2008. Fog frequency averaged about 44% from 1951 to 1975, declined through the 1980s and 1990s to a minimum of 27% in 1997, and partially recovered to an average of 42% in the study’s final decade. Across the full record, the estimated trend was −0.9 percentage points per decade, but it narrowly missed conventional statistical significance at \(p=0.06\) ([Johnstone and Dawson, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822705/)).

That result supports a cautious conclusion: northern California experienced a substantial multidecadal change in summer fog, but the record also contains pronounced year-to-year and decadal variability. The \(p\)-value does not prove that there was no change; it indicates that the estimated linear trend did not cross the study’s chosen significance threshold. Nor does the study measure only San Francisco. Its index represents a larger coastal region, so applying its exact percentage to the city would be unjustified.

Fog responds to the temperature contrast between the coast and interior, the height and strength of the marine inversion, ocean conditions, pressure patterns, winds, and cloud microphysics. Those interacting influences are one reason projections remain uncertain. California’s Bay Area climate assessment cites a modeling study that produced a 12–20% reduction in coastal fog over 1900–2070, but that is a model result spanning a broad coast, not a direct prediction that San Francisco will have precisely 12–20% fewer foggy days by 2070 ([California Fourth Climate Change Assessment: San Francisco Bay Area](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2019-11/Reg_Report-SUM-CCCA4-2018-005_SanFranciscoBayArea_ADA.pdf)).

The practical interpretation is that fog should not be treated as a guaranteed climatic thermostat. A thinner, higher, less frequent, or differently timed marine layer could increase solar heating in some neighborhoods even if low coastal cloud remains common offshore. Conversely, individual foggy years will continue to occur.

### Sea level: a clear historical rise

Sea-level evidence is less ambiguous. NOAA’s long San Francisco tide-gauge record measured a relative mean-sea-level trend of \(2.01 \pm 0.21\) millimeters per year over 1897–2006, equivalent to approximately 0.66 feet per century ([NOAA Technical Report on geospatial data and sea-level trends](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/publications/tech_rpt_57.pdf)).

“Relative” sea level is important: a tide gauge measures the ocean surface relative to the adjacent land. Its trend therefore combines changes in ocean volume and circulation with local vertical land movement. For infrastructure, that combined quantity is often the relevant one because it describes how high the water stands against wharves, seawalls, pipes, and buildings.

Historical linear extrapolation is nevertheless inadequate for long-lived planning. Future rise is expected to accelerate as oceans warm and land ice is lost, while the rate of ice-sheet loss remains a major uncertainty. The city’s vulnerability assessment explicitly notes that uncertainty about Antarctic ice loss becomes especially important after mid-century ([San Francisco Sea Level Rise Vulnerability and Consequences Assessment](https://default.sfplanning.org/plans-and-programs/planning-for-the-city/sea-level-rise/SLRVCA_Report_02.pdf)).

## The next 50 years: conditional projections, not a single forecast

### A worked sea-level and flooding example

San Francisco’s assessment reports that projections under different emissions pathways remain similar through about 2050 but separate more after mid-century. Relative to a 2000 baseline, its table gives a “likely” rise of 13 inches in 2050 under both RCP4.5 and RCP8.5. For 2070, the likely values are 20 inches under RCP4.5 and 24 inches under RCP8.5; low-probability, high-consequence values are 39 and 45 inches, respectively ([San Francisco Sea Level Rise Vulnerability and Consequences Assessment](https://default.sfplanning.org/plans-and-programs/planning-for-the-city/sea-level-rise/SLRVCA_Report_02.pdf)).

These numbers offer a useful approximation for the requested 50-year horizon, which extends to about 2076. They do not imply that the sea will stop at a particular height in that year. They show a range conditioned on emissions, climate response, ice-sheet behavior, and the probabilistic framework used in the assessment.

Consider an illustrative waterfront site where a present-day 100-year extreme tide reaches 41 inches above mean higher high water. The city’s matrix adds sea-level rise to that extreme-water level. With 24 inches of rise, the comparable combined level becomes approximately

\[
41\ \text{inches} + 24\ \text{inches}
= 65\ \text{inches above present mean higher high water}.
\]

The assessment’s published matrix gives that same 65-inch result for 24 inches of sea-level rise combined with its 100-year extreme tide ([San Francisco Sea Level Rise Vulnerability and Consequences Assessment](https://default.sfplanning.org/plans-and-programs/planning-for-the-city/sea-level-rise/SLRVCA_Report_02.pdf)).

The arithmetic is simple; the interpretation is not. A “100-year” event means a 1% annual-chance event under the statistical conditions used to estimate it, not an event occurring regularly once per century. Furthermore, adding static water levels does not fully represent waves, rainfall, sewer capacity, groundwater emergence, shoreline defenses, or future changes in storm behavior.

Still, the example reveals the central mechanism: sea-level rise elevates the baseline beneath every tide and surge. Flooding that currently requires an unusual combination of storm surge and high tide can occur more frequently as that baseline rises. Coastal flooding may also become compound flooding when high bay or ocean levels impede drainage while intense rain generates runoff. Low-lying bay-fill areas, including portions of the northeastern and southeastern waterfront, face additional concerns related to groundwater, subsidence, and liquefaction.

### Heat extremes: rare events become recurring events

Under the high-emissions RCP8.5 scenario used in San Francisco’s heat assessment, days above 85°F increase from a modeled baseline average of 3 per year in 1961–1990 to 7 per year in 2035–2064 and 15 per year in 2070–2099. The maximum annual counts across the modeled periods rise from 10 to 24 and then 51. Days above 95°F, essentially absent in the baseline average, rise to an average of 1 per year at mid-century and 2 per year late in the century ([San Francisco Heat and Air Quality Resilience report](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/HAQR-2305221_0.pdf)).

Those are model-period averages and maxima, not promises for a particular calendar year. The 2035–2064 and 2070–2099 windows also bracket rather than exactly match 2076. Nevertheless, they imply that during the next 50 years San Francisco is likely to move from a climate where 85°F is exceptional toward one where several such days occur in an ordinary year and substantially more occur in unusually hot years.

The risk will remain spatially unequal. Ocean-facing districts may retain greater marine cooling, while sunnier eastern neighborhoods can heat more. Dense buildings, limited tree canopy, upper-floor apartments, and inadequate nighttime ventilation may amplify exposure. Because local residents and buildings are acclimatized to cool conditions, health impacts can arise at temperatures that would not be considered extraordinary in inland California. Temperature thresholds must therefore be interpreted relative to local physiology, housing, and behavior, not as universal definitions of dangerous heat.

### Water availability: warming changes timing before it necessarily changes totals

San Francisco’s water problem is geographically displaced. Much of the regional system depends on Tuolumne River runoff and Hetch Hetchy Reservoir in the Sierra Nevada rather than rain falling within the city. The system serves approximately 2.7 million people in San Francisco and neighboring counties, so its vulnerability is regional rather than municipal ([SFPUC Long-Term Vulnerability Assessment](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/reports/long-term-vulnerability-assessment)).

Warming changes this supply through several connected mechanisms:

\[
\text{higher temperature}
\rightarrow \text{more winter rain and less snow}
\rightarrow \text{earlier snowmelt}
\rightarrow \text{more winter/early-spring runoff}
\rightarrow \text{less natural snow storage for summer}.
\]

Snowpack functions as a seasonal reservoir. When precipitation falls as snow, part of the winter supply is released gradually during spring and early summer. When more falls as rain, runoff arrives earlier, potentially when reservoirs must also preserve flood-control capacity. Higher temperatures additionally increase evaporation and vegetation water use.

SFPUC’s climate analysis indicates that, when projected warming is combined with decreases in precipitation, median annual Hetch Hetchy runoff could fall 7.6–8.6% from present-day conditions by 2040 and 24.7–29.4% by 2100. In critically dry years, the modeled reduction reaches as much as 46.5% by 2100. The analysis also projects less snow accumulation, earlier melt, more winter and early-spring runoff, and less late-spring and summer runoff ([SFPUC water-supply assessment](https://files.ceqanet.lci.ca.gov/288889-2/attachment/y4yRq7T82kF2LEf0NwaPPgkyIqUFsfYwHOnUbu-1fa2IxVYVyLpi5c9rjvRi7d7bJK2uNZYcxBmVYY_L0)).

Those end points should not be interpolated as though runoff must decline smoothly every year. Actual availability depends on precipitation sequences, reservoir operations, demand, environmental-flow requirements, water rights, local watershed supplies, infrastructure, and adaptation. SFPUC therefore used a vulnerability-based approach exploring many plausible combinations rather than selecting one deterministic future. The agency’s 2021 assessment also received a December 2024 erratum correcting a modeling error related to proposed instream-flow requirements, a reminder that policy assumptions can be as consequential as physical climate assumptions in system-performance results ([SFPUC Long-Term Vulnerability Assessment and erratum page](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/reports/long-term-vulnerability-assessment)).

## What is established, and what remains uncertain?

Several conclusions are strongly supported:

- San Francisco’s relative sea level has risen over the historical tide-gauge record, and continued rise will increase the frequency and reach of coastal flooding.
- Average temperatures and heat extremes are expected to increase, although ocean influence will continue to moderate the city compared with inland locations.
- Sierra warming will reduce snow storage and shift runoff earlier, complicating management of the Hetch Hetchy system.
- San Francisco’s precipitation will remain strongly seasonal and highly variable.

Other conclusions require more caution:

- The direction of change in annual precipitation is not robust across models. Water scarcity can worsen through warming and altered timing even without a large decline in annual precipitation.
- Historical northern California fog observations suggest decline and strong multidecadal variability, but they do not establish a precise San Francisco trend or a certain future rate.
- Sea-level estimates after mid-century span a widening range, substantially influenced by emissions and ice-sheet response.
- Neighborhood outcomes depend on terrain, buildings, drainage, shoreline protection, and social vulnerability; citywide averages can conceal those differences.

## Conclusion: a moderated climate is not an invulnerable climate

San Francisco’s climate profile is being altered through changes in frequency, timing, and baseline conditions rather than through the disappearance of its maritime character. Fog, wind, and the Pacific will continue to distinguish the city from inland California. But they will not prevent more frequent heat extremes. A highly variable rainy season will persist, yet warming can intensify drought stress and disrupt snow-fed water supplies. Sea-level rise will turn today’s exceptional waterfront levels into increasingly common hazards.

For the next 50 years, the most useful planning question is therefore not “What single climate will San Francisco have in 2076?” It is “What range of heat, flood, fog, rainfall, and runoff conditions must the city be able to handle?” The evidence favors measures that perform across scenarios: heat-resilient buildings and public-health systems, shoreline and drainage plans that can be adjusted as seas rise, water operations prepared for earlier runoff and longer droughts, and neighborhood-scale monitoring capable of detecting changes that regional averages miss.
