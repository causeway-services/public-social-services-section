## What this lesson covers

A specific application of [Starting a Side Hustle](./starting-a-side-hustle.md) for food producers: how to legally make and sell food from a home kitchen under [cottage food laws](../terms/cottage-food-laws.md). This is the lowest-capital entry point into the food business — no commercial lease, no health department inspection of a commercial kitchen, no build-out. It's where many successful food businesses begin.

## Prerequisites

[Starting a Side Hustle](./starting-a-side-hustle.md) covers the general framework. This lesson applies it specifically to food production.

## Before you start: know your state's rules

Cottage food laws vary by state. Before producing anything, research:

1. **What products are allowed?** Most states permit non-potentially hazardous foods: baked goods (cookies, bread, muffins, brownies, cakes without cream or custard filling), jams and jellies (high-sugar, high-acid), honey, candy, granola, dried herbs, roasted coffee, dry mixes. Most prohibit: anything requiring refrigeration, meat, dairy, canned low-acid vegetables, alcohol.

2. **Where can you sell?** Common categories:
   - Direct to consumer only (farmers' markets, farm stands, your front yard) — most restrictive
   - Direct to consumer plus online orders with local pickup or delivery
   - Direct to consumer plus wholesale to retail stores — most permissive

3. **Is there a revenue cap?** Many states cap annual cottage food revenue at $25,000–$75,000. If you exceed the cap, you must transition to a licensed commercial kitchen.

4. **What permits or registrations are required?** Some states require registration with the department of agriculture. Some require a food handler's certificate. Some require nothing beyond compliance with the labeling rules.

5. **What labeling is required?** Nearly all states require:
   - Product name
   - Ingredient list (in descending order by weight)
   - Allergen declaration (wheat, eggs, milk, nuts, soy, etc.)
   - Producer name and home address
   - Net weight or volume
   - "Made in a home kitchen" disclaimer (exact wording varies by state)

**Where to find this**: Search "[your state] cottage food law" or "[your state] home food production." Your state's department of agriculture website is the authoritative source.

## Choosing products

### What sells

At farmers' markets and local venues, these cottage food products consistently sell well:

| Product | Why it sells | Margin notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cookies** | Universal appeal, impulse purchase, easy to package | High margin if portioned well; ingredients are cheap |
| **Bread** | Perceived as difficult to make at home; artisan bread commands premium pricing | Moderate margin; flour is cheap but time investment is high |
| **Jams and preserves** | Gift-friendly, shelf-stable, seasonal variety keeps it interesting | Good margin on fruit purchased in bulk during peak season |
| **Granola and trail mix** | Health-conscious appeal, repeatable purchase | High margin; oats and nuts in bulk are inexpensive |
| **Baked goods with a niche** | Gluten-free, vegan, or allergen-free items serve an underserved market | Premium pricing; specialty ingredients cost more but customers pay more |

### What to consider

**Shelf life**: Cottage food products can't be refrigerated (in most states). How long does your product stay fresh and safe? Cookies: 1–2 weeks. Bread: 3–5 days. Jams: months to a year. Longer shelf life means less waste and more flexibility.

**Batch scalability**: Can you make 50 units as easily as 10? Cookies scale well (bigger batch of dough, same oven). Custom decorated cakes don't scale — each one requires individual attention.

**Ingredient cost stability**: Butter and vanilla fluctuate in price. Flour, sugar, and oats are stable and cheap. Products built on stable, cheap ingredients have more predictable margins.

**Differentiation**: A basic chocolate chip cookie competes with every grocery store. A brown butter chocolate chip cookie with sea salt and local honey competes with nobody at the farmers' market. The product doesn't need to be exotic — it needs to be noticeably better or different from what's easily available.

## Recipe development for sale

### Standardize recipes

A recipe that works at home ("a handful of walnuts, bake until golden") doesn't work for a business. Convert every recipe to exact weights and measures:

- Use a kitchen scale. Measure flour, sugar, and butter by weight (grams or ounces), not volume. A "cup of flour" varies by 20–30% depending on how it's scooped; 120 grams of flour is always 120 grams.
- Write down every step, including timing, temperatures, and visual cues.
- Test the recipe at production scale (3× or 5× the home recipe) — scaling isn't always linear. A triple batch of cookie dough may need slightly more leavening or different mixing time.

### Cost each recipe

Follow the recipe costing method from [Food Costing and Waste Reduction](./food-costing-and-waste-reduction.md), scaled for cottage production:

**Example: Sourdough loaf**

| Ingredient | Cost/unit | Qty per loaf | Cost per loaf |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | $0.55/lb | 1 lb | $0.55 |
| Starter (flour + water, maintained) | ~$0.05/feeding | 1 feeding | $0.05 |
| Salt | $0.02/tsp | 2 tsp | $0.04 |
| Water | negligible | — | $0.00 |
| **Ingredient cost** | | | **$0.64** |
| Packaging (paper bag + label) | | | $0.15 |
| **Total product cost** | | | **$0.79** |

At a sale price of $7.00, ingredient cost is 11% — excellent margin. But this doesn't include the baker's time (mixing, shaping, proofing, baking = ~30 minutes of active work per loaf in a batch of 4), energy cost (oven running for 45 minutes), or selling cost (market booth + time at the market). Include everything when setting prices.

## Pricing cottage food

### The formula

**Price = Product cost + Selling cost + Labor + Profit**

| Component | Example (sourdough loaf, batch of 4) |
|---|---|
| Product cost | $0.79 × 4 = $3.16 |
| Selling cost (booth fee ÷ items sold) | $35 booth ÷ ~40 items = $0.88 × 4 = $3.50 |
| Labor (2 hours baking + 4 hours at market = 6 hrs ÷ 10 products per batch cycle) | $20/hr × 6 hrs ÷ 10 items × 4 = $48.00 |
| **Cost basis for 4 loaves** | **$54.66** |
| **Cost per loaf** | **$13.67** |

At $7.00 per loaf, this producer is paying themselves less than minimum wage after costs. At $9.00 per loaf, it's better but still tight. At $12.00 per loaf — which artisan sourdough sells for at many markets — the math works.

### Market reality check

Walk the market before you start selling. What do comparable products sell for? If everyone sells sourdough at $8–$10, pricing at $12 requires either a noticeably superior product or a compelling story (heritage grains, 48-hour ferment, local flour). If you can't justify the premium, the batch economics may not support this product at this venue.

### Pricing mistakes

- **Pricing based on ingredients only**: Forgetting labor, booth fees, gas, and packaging. The $0.79 sourdough loaf looks profitable at $7 until you count the work.
- **Matching grocery store prices**: You're not competing with grocery stores. Your product is handmade, local, fresh, and made by a person the customer can talk to. Price accordingly.
- **Dropping prices when sales are slow**: This trains customers to wait for deals and undermines the price for future weeks. Instead, bring less product, or try different products.

## Selling

### Farmers' markets

The primary sales channel for most cottage food producers.

**Getting in**: Contact the market manager. Most markets have an application process and may limit the number of vendors per category (only two bakers, for example). Apply early — popular markets fill up months ahead.

**Booth setup**: Table, tablecloth, product display, price signs, payment method (Square reader on your phone), bags for customers, cash for making change. Keep it clean, simple, and inviting. The product should be the focus, not the booth decor.

**What to bring**: Start conservative. It's better to sell out early (creates scarcity and urgency for next week) than to bring home half your inventory (wasted product and demoralizing). Track sales each week and adjust quantity gradually.

**Building repeat customers**: Learn names. Remember what they bought last week. Offer a sample of the new flavor. Consistency — same booth, same quality, same friendly interaction — builds habit. The customer who comes every Saturday for your granola is worth $600/year. Treat them accordingly.

### Other channels

- **Farm stands and roadside sales**: If zoning allows, a table at the end of the driveway with a sign. Very low cost, very low effort, limited by foot/vehicle traffic.
- **Word of mouth and social media**: Post photos of what you're making. Take orders for pickup. Build an email list from market customers ("sign up and I'll text you when I have fresh bread this week").
- **Local shops (if your state allows wholesale)**: Approach a local coffee shop, gift store, or specialty grocer. Wholesale pricing is typically 50% of retail — make sure your margins support it.

## Growing beyond cottage food

When demand exceeds what your home kitchen can produce — or when revenue approaches the state cap — the next step is a commercial kitchen.

### Shared commercial kitchens

Many cities have commissary kitchens or shared commercial kitchens available for hourly rental ($15–$40/hour). These are licensed, inspected commercial spaces where multiple small producers share time. This lets you:
- Produce at higher volume (commercial ovens, larger mixers)
- Sell products not allowed under cottage food (items requiring refrigeration, canned goods)
- Access wholesale channels
- Without signing a lease or building out your own kitchen

### The transition decision

Moving from cottage food to a licensed commercial operation is a significant step: kitchen rental costs, health department licensing, potentially a business license and insurance. Before making the jump:

1. Are you consistently selling out at your current capacity?
2. Have you tracked demand — do you have customers asking for more than you can produce?
3. Does the revenue at the larger scale (minus the new costs) still produce acceptable income?
4. Is the additional time commitment compatible with your life?

If the answers are all yes, and you've been running the cottage operation profitably for 6–12 months, the transition is lower-risk — you've already validated the product, built a customer base, and learned the rhythm of production and sales.

## Guidance

- Research your state's cottage food law. List: allowed products, sales channels, revenue cap, labeling requirements, required permits. Can you legally sell what you want to sell?
- Choose one product and cost it completely: ingredients, packaging, booth fee share, and your time at a fair hourly rate. What price makes the math work? Is that price realistic for your market?
- Apply to one farmers' market. Plan your first booth: how many units will you bring, what's your booth setup, and what payment methods will you accept?
