## What this lesson covers

For many small producers — bakers, makers, crafters, growers, and artisans — markets and fairs are the primary or only sales channel. This lesson covers the practical, operational side: how to choose the right events, set up a booth that sells, manage inventory for an unpredictable channel, interact with customers face-to-face, and use each market day as a data-gathering exercise that improves the business.

## Prerequisites

[Starting a Side Hustle](./starting-a-side-hustle.md) for the general framework of small-scale business.

## Choosing events

### Types of events

| Event type | Frequency | Typical cost | Audience | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Farmers' market** | Weekly (seasonal or year-round) | $20–$75/week | Regular local shoppers, food-focused | Food products, plants, farm goods |
| **Craft fair** | 1–2 days, several per year | $50–$300/event | Gift shoppers, craft enthusiasts | Handmade goods, art, specialty products |
| **Holiday market** | Seasonal (Nov–Dec) | $75–$500/event | Gift buyers with budget | Higher-priced items, gift-ready packaging |
| **Pop-up event** | One-time, often in a retail or restaurant space | $0–$200 | Curious locals, social media followers | Testing a new market or product |
| **Flea market / swap meet** | Weekly or monthly | $15–$50 | Bargain hunters, browsers | Lower-priced items, vintage, secondhand |

### Evaluating a market

Before committing, visit the market as a customer:

- **Foot traffic**: How many people walk through during the hours you'd sell? A quiet market with 50 visitors won't support 20 vendors.
- **Customer type**: Do the shoppers match your product? A market full of bargain hunters won't support $12 artisan sourdough. A market in a high-income neighborhood might.
- **Competition**: How many vendors sell something similar to your product? One or two creates category validation; five means you're splitting a limited pie.
- **Vendor quality**: Are the existing vendors professional and well-presented? A market with high-quality vendors attracts higher-quality customers.
- **Market management**: Is the market well-organized, well-promoted, and well-maintained? Good management brings foot traffic; poor management lets it decay.

### Applying

Most established markets have an application process:
- Apply early — popular markets fill months ahead
- Include photos of your product and booth setup
- Many markets limit vendor categories (two bakers, three craft vendors, etc.)
- Some require proof of insurance (a general liability policy, often $300–$500/year)
- Some require relevant permits or licenses (food handler's certificate, [cottage food](../terms/cottage-food-laws.md) registration)

## Booth setup

Your booth is your store. Customers decide within 3 seconds whether to approach or walk past.

### The essentials

| Item | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Table** | Display surface | 6-foot folding table is standard. Cover with a tablecloth — never bare folding table. |
| **Canopy/tent** | Weather protection and visibility | 10×10 pop-up canopy. Required at most outdoor markets. Use weights (not stakes — many markets are on pavement). |
| **Signage** | Your business name and what you sell | Visible from 15+ feet. Large text, readable font. Include prices — customers who can't find prices often walk away. |
| **Display risers** | Vertical product presentation | Crates, boxes, or shelves that create height variation. Flat tables with everything at the same level are visually boring. |
| **Tablecloth** | Clean, professional appearance | Solid color that doesn't compete with the product. |
| **Price signs** | Every product priced clearly | Handwritten on kraft card stock works fine. Unpriced products create friction. |
| **Payment processing** | Accept cards | Square, SumUp, or similar. 75%+ of market customers prefer card. Cash-only booths lose sales. |
| **Cash box** | Make change | Start with $50–$75 in small bills and coins. |
| **Bags** | Carry-out for customers | Paper bags, branded if you want, plain if budget is tight. |
| **Business cards or handout** | Contact info for reorders | Include your name, product, and how to reach you (email, social media, phone). |

### Display principles

**Height variation**: Use crates, risers, and stacked boxes to create a display that has high, medium, and low elements. A flat table is a wall of sameness — the eye slides past it.

**Abundance, not clutter**: The booth should look full (abundance signals popularity) but not chaotic (clutter signals disorganization). Group similar items together. Leave breathing room between product categories.

**Focal point**: Place your best-selling or most visually appealing item at eye level, front and center. This is what draws people in.

**Samples** (for food): If allowed by your market and your state's rules, offer small samples. A taste of the jam or a bite of the cookie converts browsers to buyers more effectively than any sign.

## Inventory planning

### How much to bring

The hardest question for a new market vendor. The answer evolves with data.

**First market**: Bring what feels like "enough" — and it will probably be too much or too little. Either outcome is informative.

**After 3–4 markets**: You have data. Track:

| Week | Product | Brought | Sold | Leftover | Sold out? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sourdough loaves | 15 | 12 | 3 | No |
| 1 | Chocolate chip cookies (bag of 6) | 20 | 20 | 0 | Yes (by 11 AM) |
| 1 | Granola (12 oz bag) | 10 | 4 | 6 | No |
| 2 | Sourdough loaves | 14 | 13 | 1 | No |
| 2 | Chocolate chip cookies | 30 | 26 | 4 | No |
| 2 | Granola | 8 | 5 | 3 | No |

**Patterns after week 4**: Cookies are the volume seller — bring 25–30 and accept occasional sellout (selling out creates urgency; regular sellout by 10 AM means bring more). Sourdough is consistent at 12–14 loaves. Granola moves slowly — reduce to 6 bags or consider dropping it.

### Production scheduling

Work backward from market day:

| Day | Activity (for a Saturday market) |
|---|---|
| **Wednesday** | Order/buy ingredients if not on hand |
| **Thursday** | Mix and shape sourdough (overnight proof) |
| **Friday morning** | Bake sourdough. Mix and bake cookies. Package granola. |
| **Friday evening** | Package everything. Label. Load the car. |
| **Saturday 6 AM** | Set up at market |
| **Saturday 8 AM–1 PM** | Sell |
| **Saturday 2 PM** | Break down, go home, rest |

### Managing perishable leftovers

Leftover baked goods lose value rapidly. Options:
- Discount at the end of the market day ("last hour, everything half off" — this is acceptable at markets and creates urgency)
- Donate to a local food bank or shelter (some states provide liability protection for good-faith food donations)
- Freeze for personal use
- Don't make it in the first place — the best waste reduction is accurate production planning

## In-person selling

### The greeting

As a customer approaches:
- **Make eye contact and smile.** This is the invitation.
- **Say hello.** "Good morning!" or "Hi, welcome!" — warm, not aggressive.
- **Let them browse for 5 seconds before engaging.** A customer who picks something up is interested. A customer who's scanning the table needs a moment.
- **Then offer help.** "Have you tried the sourdough before?" or "The chocolate chip cookies are fresh from this morning."

### What not to do

- **Don't sit behind the table on your phone.** You're invisible. Stand, engage, be present.
- **Don't hover.** Give space. Some customers want to chat; others want to browse quietly and decide on their own.
- **Don't apologize for your prices.** "It's a little expensive but..." undermines your product. State the price confidently. If the price is right (you've done the math), own it.
- **Don't hard sell.** Market customers are browsing, not being pitched. Enthusiasm is welcome; pressure is not.

### Samples and storytelling

If you can offer samples (check market and state rules), they're the most effective selling tool. A customer who tastes the jam buys the jam. A customer who reads the label doesn't.

Storytelling works alongside samples: "I make this with strawberries from [local farm], about 20 minutes from here. The berries are so sweet I use less sugar than most jams." This is marketing — but it's also true, and customers at markets are buying the story as much as the product. They want to know the person and the process.

### Building a customer list

At the booth, have a signup sheet or a tablet:
- "Want me to text you when I have fresh bread this week?"
- "I'm starting to take pre-orders — can I add you to the list?"

A simple list of 50 names and phone numbers, messaged weekly, can generate pre-orders that reduce waste and guarantee revenue before market day.

## Using market data

Every market day is a business experiment. After each market:

### The post-market log

| Metric | This week |
|---|---|
| **Total revenue** | $187.00 |
| **Total product cost** | $52.00 |
| **Booth fee** | $35.00 |
| **Gas / mileage** | $8.00 |
| **Net revenue** | $92.00 |
| **Hours (production + market + travel)** | 9 |
| **Effective hourly rate** | $10.22 |
| **Best seller** | Chocolate chip cookies (26 sold) |
| **Slow mover** | Granola (5 sold) |
| **Weather** | Sunny, 72°F |
| **Customer comments** | "Do you have a gluten-free option?" (asked 3×) |

### What the data tells you

After 8 weeks of data:
- You know your average revenue, best and worst days, and revenue range
- You know which products sell and which don't (drop the granola, add gluten-free cookies)
- You know how weather affects sales (rainy days: 40% less traffic; hot days: cold drinks would sell)
- You know your effective hourly rate — and whether it's worth your time
- Customer questions reveal unmet demand (three people asked for gluten-free = test a gluten-free product next month)

This is [market analysis](../terms/market-analysis.md) at the smallest, most direct scale possible. You don't need surveys or focus groups. You have real sales data and face-to-face feedback every week.

## Scaling beyond one market

When the first market is consistent and profitable:

### Add a second market day

Different market, different day. This doubles production volume (economies of scale on ingredients), doubles revenue potential, and tests a new customer base. But it also doubles the time commitment — make sure it's sustainable.

### Add online ordering

Use your market customer list to take pre-orders for pickup at the market or delivery. This reduces waste (you make exactly what's ordered) and generates revenue before market day.

### Add wholesale

Approach a local coffee shop or specialty store: "I sell sourdough at the Saturday market. Would you be interested in carrying a few loaves? I can deliver fresh on Thursday." Wholesale pricing is typically 50% of retail — the margin is thinner, but the volume is consistent and requires no booth time.

### When to formalize

If market revenue consistently exceeds $500/week, or if you're adding wholesale accounts, it's time to:
- Register as a business (if not already done)
- Consider forming an LLC (see [Choosing a Corporate Structure](./choosing-a-corporate-structure.md))
- Get [business insurance](../terms/business-insurance.md) (general liability, product liability)
- Set up accounting software (graduate from the spreadsheet — see [Accounting Software Setup](./accounting-software-setup.md))
- Investigate a shared commercial kitchen to increase production capacity

## Guidance

- Visit two markets as a customer this weekend. For each: estimate foot traffic, note the vendor quality, and identify whether your product would fill a gap or duplicate what's already there.
- Set up a post-market log spreadsheet using the template above. After your first market day, fill it in. What's your effective hourly rate? Is it acceptable?
- Track every customer question for four weeks. What are people asking for that you don't offer? Is there a viable product in those requests?
