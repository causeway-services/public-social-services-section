## What this lesson covers

Most of the curricula in this series assume a business that is someone's primary enterprise — a restaurant with a lease, employees, and $200,000 in startup capital. This lesson covers the other end: a person with a job who wants to earn supplemental income by selling a product or service on the side. The stakes are lower, the capital is smaller, and the failure mode is wasted weekends rather than bankruptcy. But the fundamentals — understanding costs, finding customers, and managing money — are the same.

---

## Choosing what to sell

### Start from what you already do

The best side hustles emerge from existing skills, not from business ideas found on the internet:
- You bake well → sell baked goods at a farmers' market
- You're good with computers → offer tech support to small businesses
- You garden → sell seedlings, herbs, or produce at a farm stand
- You sew → sell alterations, custom items, or repairs
- You write clearly → freelance copywriting or editing
- You're organized → offer bookkeeping or administrative services
- You build things → sell furniture, cutting boards, or home goods

The skill already exists. The side hustle monetizes it.

### Test before you invest

Don't spend money on a logo, a website, or inventory before you know someone will pay. The [minimum viable product](../terms/minimum-viable-product.md) approach:

1. **Make a small batch.** 12 jars of jam, 3 cutting boards, 5 sample projects.
2. **Sell to real people for real money.** Friends and family count for the first batch — but only if they pay. Free is not validation.
3. **Listen to what they say.** What did they love? What would they change? Would they buy again? Would they recommend it?
4. **Adjust and repeat.** Make batch two based on what you learned from batch one.

If you can't sell 12 jars of jam to people you know, you probably can't sell 120 jars to strangers. Better to learn that before investing in labels, a market booth, and a commercial kitchen.

## Starting with minimal capital

Most side hustles can start for under $500. Many can start for under $100.

### What you actually need

| Need | Low-cost approach |
|---|---|
| **Raw materials** | Buy ingredients/materials for the first small batch only |
| **Packaging** | Simple, clean, functional. Mason jars, kraft paper bags, plain boxes. Fancy packaging comes later — if ever. |
| **Payment processing** | Square, Venmo, or Cash App for in-person sales. Free to start; transaction fees of 2.6–2.9%. |
| **Communication** | A dedicated email address (free) and a simple social media presence (free) |
| **Legal** | Check if your city requires a business license for your activity. Many don't for very small operations. |

### What you don't need yet

- A website (sell through markets, word of mouth, and social media first)
- A logo (your name and a clean label are sufficient)
- An LLC (operate as a sole proprietor until revenue justifies the cost — see [Choosing a Corporate Structure](./choosing-a-corporate-structure.md))
- Business cards (give people your phone number or social media handle)
- Accounting software (a spreadsheet tracks income and expenses until volume warrants software)

### The $100 test

Whatever your idea, ask: can I test this for $100 or less? If the answer is yes, test it this weekend. If the answer is no, find a smaller version of the idea that you can test for $100.

## Managing time

The hardest part of a side hustle isn't the work — it's fitting the work around a job, family, and rest.

### Be honest about available hours

Write down your week: job hours, commute, sleep, meals, family time, existing commitments. What's left? For most people, it's 5–15 hours per week. That's the side hustle budget. Don't plan a business that requires 25 hours a week from someone who has 10.

### Batch your work

Scattered 30-minute sessions are inefficient. Batch similar tasks:
- **Production day**: Saturday morning — bake all the cookies, cut all the boards, write all the content
- **Admin day**: Sunday evening — update the spreadsheet, reply to messages, plan next week's production
- **Market day**: Saturday afternoon — sell at the farmers' market (this is fixed by the market schedule)

### Protect your rest

A side hustle that consumes every free hour leads to burnout — and burnout kills side hustles more reliably than bad products do. Schedule at least one full day per week with no hustle work. The business will survive; you might not without the break.

### When the job conflicts

Some employers have policies about outside employment. Check your employment agreement or handbook. Common restrictions:
- Non-compete clauses may prohibit side work in the same industry as your employer
- Moonlighting policies may require disclosure of outside employment
- Use of company resources (computer, email, time) for side work is almost always prohibited

## Money management

### Separate the money

Open a free business checking account (many banks offer them). All side hustle income goes in; all side hustle expenses come out. This separation:
- Makes tax reporting simple (one account to review, not mixed personal transactions)
- Shows you the actual profit (revenue minus expenses, clearly visible)
- Protects against spending hustle money on personal expenses before setting aside taxes

### Track everything in a spreadsheet

Until revenue exceeds $1,000–$2,000/month, a simple spreadsheet is sufficient:

| Date | Description | Income | Expense | Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3/1 | Flour, sugar, butter (batch ingredients) | | $42.00 | Materials |
| 3/1 | Market booth fee | | $35.00 | Selling costs |
| 3/1 | Farmers' market sales (24 cookies @ $3, 6 loaves @ $7) | $114.00 | | Sales |
| 3/8 | Ingredients for next batch | | $38.00 | Materials |
| 3/8 | Market sales | $98.00 | | Sales |

At the end of the month: total income minus total expenses = profit. At the end of the year: hand this spreadsheet to a tax preparer (or use it to file Schedule C yourself).

### Pricing for a side hustle

The temptation is to underprice — "it's just a side thing, I don't need to charge much." But underpricing:
- Devalues the product (customers associate price with quality)
- Makes the work unsustainable (earning $4/hour after materials isn't a hustle — it's a hobby with extra steps)
- Undercuts other small producers who are trying to earn a living

Calculate your actual cost: materials + packaging + booth fee + gas to the market. Add your time at a rate you'd accept for skilled work ($15–$25/hour minimum). That's your floor price. See [Pricing Strategy and Menu Engineering](./pricing-strategy-and-menu-engineering.md) for the full framework, scaled down.

## Tax obligations

Side hustle income is taxable. The IRS does not have a "it's just a little extra money" exemption.

### What you owe

- **Self-employment tax**: 15.3% on net earnings over $400/year (Social Security + Medicare). This is the biggest surprise for new side hustlers.
- **Federal income tax**: At your marginal rate, on top of self-employment tax.
- **State income tax**: If your state has one.
- **Quarterly estimated taxes**: If you expect to owe more than $1,000 in taxes for the year, you should make quarterly payments to avoid a penalty. See [Small Business Tax Strategy](./small-business-tax-strategy.md).

### What you can deduct

Every legitimate business expense reduces taxable income:
- Materials and ingredients
- Market booth fees
- Mileage to and from markets or client sites (track with an app — the IRS requires a log)
- Packaging and shipping supplies
- Business portion of phone and internet (if used for the business — be reasonable and documentable)
- Home office, if a space is used exclusively for the business

### Example

You earned $4,800 from farmers' market sales this year. Expenses: $1,900 in materials, $700 in booth fees, $300 in mileage, $100 in packaging. Net profit: $1,800.

Self-employment tax on $1,800: ~$275. Federal income tax (assuming 22% bracket): ~$396. Total tax: ~$671.

Set aside 30–35% of each month's profit for taxes. Put it in a separate savings account. Don't spend it.

## The growth decision

At some point, a successful side hustle presents a question: do I grow this, or do I keep it where it is?

### Reasons to stay small

- The primary job provides income, benefits, and stability you don't want to lose
- The side hustle is enjoyable at its current scale but would become stressful at a larger scale
- Growth requires capital investment (commercial kitchen, employees, equipment) that changes the risk profile
- The market for the product is local and limited — 50 customers per week is the natural ceiling
- You value your free time more than additional income

**Staying small intentionally is a legitimate business strategy.** Not every business needs to grow. A side hustle that earns $500/month reliably, with work the owner enjoys, on a schedule the owner controls, is successful by any reasonable definition.

### Reasons to grow

- Demand consistently exceeds supply (you sell out every week and turn away customers)
- The income potential justifies leaving the primary job (after accounting for lost benefits, stability, and the increased risk)
- The work is more fulfilling than the primary job
- You've validated the market for 6–12 months and have confidence in the numbers
- You can grow incrementally without a massive capital investment

### The incremental path

Growth doesn't have to mean "quit your job and open a store." It can mean:
- Adding a second market day
- Starting an online ordering system for local delivery
- Renting a shared commercial kitchen for 4 hours/week (common in many cities, $15–$30/hour) to increase production capacity and sell products not allowed under [cottage food laws](../terms/cottage-food-laws.md)
- Hiring one part-time helper for production days
- Wholesaling to one local shop

Each step tests a larger scale before committing to it.

## Guidance

- Identify your side hustle idea using the "what do you already do" approach. What skill or product could you sell this weekend? What's the $100 test?
- Calculate your first product's true cost: materials, time, and selling expenses. Set a price that covers the cost and pays you fairly. Compare it to what similar products sell for — is it in range?
- Open a separate checking account for the side hustle before your first sale. Commit to running all income and expenses through it.
