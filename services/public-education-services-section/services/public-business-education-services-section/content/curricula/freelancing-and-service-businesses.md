## What this lesson covers

Service businesses sell time and expertise rather than physical products: freelance writing, graphic design, bookkeeping, tutoring, photography, web development, lawn care, house cleaning, handyman work, pet sitting, personal training. These businesses have near-zero startup cost (you already have the skills and, for many services, the tools) but face unique challenges: revenue depends on finding clients, scope creep threatens profitability, and the owner's time is the only inventory.

## Finding clients

### The first three clients

The first clients almost always come from personal connections — not from a website, not from advertising, and not from a marketplace.

1. **Tell everyone you know what you're offering.** "I'm taking on freelance bookkeeping clients" — said to 20 people — will produce 1–3 leads. People can't hire you for something they don't know you do.
2. **Ask for introductions, not work.** "Do you know anyone who needs help with their books?" is easier for someone to act on than "Will you hire me?" Referrals are the primary growth engine for service businesses at every stage.
3. **Offer to do a small project cheaply (or free) for the first client.** One project, one time, to build a portfolio piece and a testimonial. After that, charge full rate. Prolonged free work devalues the service and attracts clients who won't pay.

### Ongoing client acquisition

| Channel | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Referrals** | Existing clients recommend you to others | Every service business. The #1 source of clients for established freelancers. |
| **Local networking** | Chamber of commerce, business groups, community events | Local services (bookkeeping, design, photography, handyman) |
| **Online presence** | Simple website or portfolio showing past work | Design, writing, photography, web development |
| **Marketplaces** | Upwork, Fiverr, Thumbtack, TaskRabbit | Getting started and filling gaps between referral clients. Marketplace fees (10–20%) and price competition make these less profitable long-term. |
| **Social media** | Showing your work, sharing expertise, engaging with potential clients | Creative fields (design, photography, crafts) |
| **Cold outreach** | Emailing or calling potential clients directly | B2B services (bookkeeping, consulting, marketing) |

### The referral system

Satisfied clients generate more clients — but only if you make it easy:
- After completing a project: "I'm glad you're happy with the work. If you know anyone who could use similar help, I'd appreciate the introduction."
- Send a thank-you note or email after the project closes. People refer to people they remember fondly.
- Consider a referral incentive: "If you refer someone who becomes a client, I'll give you a $50 credit toward your next project."

## Setting rates

### Hourly vs. project-based

| Approach | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Hourly** | Simple, transparent, covers scope changes naturally | Penalizes efficiency (faster = less pay); client anxiety about open-ended costs |
| **Project-based** | Rewards efficiency; client knows the total cost upfront | Scope creep risk; underestimating time = working for less than intended |
| **Retainer** | Predictable recurring income; client has priority access | Requires established relationship; can feel like an obligation during slow periods |

Most freelancers start hourly and move toward project-based pricing as they gain experience and can estimate time accurately.

### How to calculate your rate

**Step 1: What do you need to earn?**

If the side hustle is supplemental income, the threshold is lower. If it's replacing a salary, calculate: desired annual income + self-employment tax (15.3%) + health insurance + retirement savings + business expenses = total revenue needed.

**Step 2: How many billable hours can you work?**

Not all hours are billable. Marketing, admin, invoicing, travel, and learning consume time. A freelancer working 20 hours/week on the side hustle may have 12–15 billable hours. A full-time freelancer working 40 hours/week may have 25–30 billable hours.

**Step 3: Divide.**

If you need $60,000/year and can bill 1,300 hours/year (25/week × 52): minimum rate = $46/hour.

If you need $20,000/year from a side hustle and can bill 600 hours/year (12/week × 50): minimum rate = $33/hour.

### Common rate mistakes

- **Pricing based on what you'd accept as an employee.** Employment includes benefits (health insurance, retirement match, paid time off, employer payroll tax) worth 30–40% of salary. A $25/hour job is equivalent to $35–$40/hour freelance.
- **Pricing based on what feels comfortable.** Discomfort with quoting a rate is not evidence that the rate is too high. It's evidence that you haven't done the math.
- **Racing to the bottom on marketplaces.** Competing on price against global freelancers willing to work for $5/hour is unwinnable. Compete on quality, reliability, and local presence instead.

## Managing client work

### The scope conversation

Before starting any project, agree on:
- **What's included**: Specific deliverables, described clearly. "A 5-page website with responsive design, content for each page provided by the client" — not "a website."
- **What's not included**: State exclusions explicitly. "This does not include ongoing maintenance, content writing, or SEO optimization."
- **Timeline**: When will work begin? When will drafts be delivered? When is the final deliverable due?
- **Revisions**: How many rounds of revisions are included? What happens after that? ("Two rounds of revisions are included. Additional revisions are billed at $X/hour.")
- **Price**: The total project fee, or the hourly rate and estimated hours.

Put this in writing — an email summary is sufficient for small projects. For projects over $500, use a simple contract or statement of work.

### Scope creep

Scope creep is when the client asks for additional work beyond the original agreement — a few extra pages, a different approach, "just one more thing." Each request is small; cumulatively they can double the project.

**How to handle it**: "I can definitely do that. It's outside the original scope, so it would be an additional $X. Want me to go ahead?" This isn't adversarial — it's professional. Clients respect clarity about what's included and what costs extra.

### Getting paid

- **Invoice immediately** upon completion (or upon reaching a milestone).
- **Require a deposit** for projects over $500. 50% upfront is standard for freelance work.
- **Set payment terms**: Net 15 is reasonable for freelance invoices. Net 30 is acceptable for larger clients. Anything longer should be negotiated.
- **Follow up on late payment** promptly. Day 1 past due: email reminder. Day 7: phone call. Day 14: firm email noting the overdue status. Don't be embarrassed about collecting money you earned.

## The feast-or-famine cycle

Service businesses have an inherent rhythm: when you're busy with client work, you have no time to market for new clients. When client work ends, you market — but there's a lag before new work arrives. This creates alternating periods of too much work and too little.

### Smoothing the cycle

- **Never stop marketing.** Even during busy periods, spend 1–2 hours/week on relationship maintenance, social media, or outreach. The pipeline needs constant feeding.
- **Build a waitlist.** When you're full, tell new prospects: "I can start in 3 weeks. Can I put you on my schedule?" This creates a buffer between projects.
- **Seek retainer clients.** A client who pays $500/month for 10 hours of bookkeeping is predictable income that smooths the cycle.
- **Diversify client types.** If all your clients are in the same industry, an industry downturn kills all your revenue simultaneously. Three clients in different industries is more resilient than five in the same one.
- **Save during feasts.** Set aside 20–30% of revenue during busy months to cover lean months. This is the freelancer's version of a cash reserve.

## Scaling (or not)

### Staying solo

Many freelancers choose to stay solo permanently. The advantages:
- No employees, no [payroll](../terms/payroll.md), no management overhead
- Full control over schedule, clients, and work quality
- Income scales with rate increases and efficiency — not headcount
- Simplest possible business structure

The ceiling: one person can only bill so many hours. Income is capped by time, not by demand.

### Adding capacity

If demand consistently exceeds capacity:
- **Subcontract**: Hire other freelancers for specific projects. You manage the client relationship; they do some of the work. You charge the client $75/hour and pay the subcontractor $50/hour, keeping $25/hour for project management and client acquisition.
- **Hire part-time help**: An assistant handles admin, scheduling, invoicing — freeing your time for billable work.
- **Specialize and raise rates**: Instead of adding hours, increase the value per hour. A generalist web designer at $50/hour becomes a Shopify e-commerce specialist at $100/hour — same hours, double the revenue.

## Guidance

- Identify three people in your network who might need the service you offer (or who know people who do). Reach out this week — not to sell, but to let them know what you're doing and ask if they know anyone who could use the help.
- Calculate your minimum hourly rate using the three-step formula. Is it higher or lower than you expected? How does it compare to marketplace rates for your skill?
- Write a scope description for a typical project you'd take on. Include: deliverables, exclusions, timeline, revision policy, and price. Would you feel comfortable sending this to a potential client?
