# Accounting, Unit Economics, Cash, and Capital Allocation

## Introduction: four views of one operating system

A firm converts resources—cash, labor, equipment, technology, supplier capacity, and managerial attention—into products or services for customers. Accounting, unit economics, cash analysis, and capital allocation examine different parts of that conversion:

- **Accounting** records economic resources, obligations, revenues, expenses, and changes in owners’ claims under defined recognition and measurement rules.
- **Unit economics** asks whether a useful unit—an order, customer, contract, location, or production batch—creates incremental economic value.
- **Cash analysis** examines when money actually enters or leaves the firm and whether the firm can meet obligations while continuing to operate.
- **Capital allocation** decides where scarce funds should go: operating capacity, product development, acquisitions, debt repayment, reserves, dividends, or share repurchases.

These views are complementary, not interchangeable. Accrual profit can rise while cash falls. A positive contribution margin can coexist with company-wide losses. A cash-rich firm can destroy value by investing at inadequate returns, while a cash-consuming project can create value if its eventual risk-adjusted inflows exceed its investment.

The central lesson is therefore not “maximize” a single financial measure. It is to build a traceable chain from customer demand and operating processes to accounting results, cash consequences, financing risk, and investment decisions.

## 1. Prerequisites: connect the business model to the process model

Financial analysis begins upstream of the financial statements.

First, identify the **value proposition and business model**:

1. Who is the customer, user, and payer?
2. What outcome does the offering promise?
3. What triggers demand?
4. What is sold: a product, subscription, transaction, license, project, capacity reservation, or bundle?
5. Who bears inventory, credit, warranty, regulatory, and demand risk?
6. When is the firm entitled to consideration, and when does it collect cash?

Second, map the **operating process**:

\[
\text{Demand} \rightarrow \text{Order} \rightarrow \text{Production or service} \rightarrow
\text{Delivery} \rightarrow \text{Billing} \rightarrow \text{Collection}
\]

For each stage, identify throughput, processing time, waiting time, yield, rework, capacity, and the inventory or receivables created. A bottleneck can restrict sales even when average capacity appears ample. A quality failure can appear financially as scrap, refunds, warranty expense, customer-support labor, delayed collection, or lost retention. Accounting numbers are compressed representations of these physical and contractual events.

This operational grounding matters because classifications are not self-explanatory. A delivery cost might vary with orders in one model but behave like committed network capacity in another. Software development may support current operations, future products, or both. Customer support may be a cost to serve, a retention investment, or an inseparable part of the promised service. The analyst must understand the mechanism before assigning a cost to a unit or interpreting a margin.

## 2. What financial accounting establishes

### 2.1 The reporting boundary and basic elements

General-purpose financial reporting is designed primarily to provide useful information to existing and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors. It does not directly measure every feature of a firm’s strategy, workforce, social impact, or competitive position. The IFRS Conceptual Framework identifies assets, liabilities, equity, income, and expenses as the elements of financial statements and treats relevance and faithful representation as fundamental qualities of useful information ([IFRS Foundation, *Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting*](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/conceptual-framework/)).

The framework defines:

- an **asset** as a present economic resource controlled by the entity because of past events;
- a **liability** as a present obligation to transfer an economic resource because of past events;
- **equity** as the residual interest after deducting liabilities from assets;
- **income** as increases in assets or decreases in liabilities that increase equity, excluding owner contributions; and
- **expenses** as decreases in assets or increases in liabilities that reduce equity, excluding distributions to owners.

These definitions produce the balance-sheet identity:

\[
\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}.
\]

This is an accounting identity, not a valuation assertion. Recorded equity is not necessarily the market value of the company. Internally developed capabilities, customer relationships, reputation, organizational knowledge, and growth options may be economically important without appearing as separately recognized assets. Conversely, a recorded asset may later prove less productive than expected and require impairment.

The Conceptual Framework also permits different measurement bases for different items, including historical-cost and current-value approaches. It explicitly acknowledges measurement uncertainty and states that reasonable estimates can remain useful when their nature and limitations are properly explained ([IFRS Foundation, 2026 issued Conceptual Framework](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/pdf-standards/english/2026/issued/part-a/conceptual-framework-for-financial-reporting.pdf?bypass=on)). Thus, audited accounting is disciplined measurement, but not measurement without judgment.

### 2.2 The three principal statements

The **statement of financial position**, or balance sheet, reports resources and claims at a date. It helps answer:

- How much cash and working capital are available?
- How much inventory, productive equipment, debt, and other obligations exist?
- How is the asset base financed?

The **income statement** reports recognized income and expenses over a period. A simplified structure is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Revenue}
- \text{Cost of revenue}
&= \text{Gross profit},\\
\text{Gross profit}
- \text{Operating expenses}
&= \text{Operating profit},\\
\text{Operating profit}
- \text{interest and taxes, with other items}
&= \text{Net income}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The **statement of cash flows** explains changes in cash and cash equivalents. IAS 7 classifies cash flows as operating, investing, or financing. Operating activities are the entity’s principal revenue-producing activities; investing includes acquiring and disposing of long-term assets and investments; financing changes contributed equity and borrowings ([IFRS Foundation, *IAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows*](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-7-statement-of-cash-flows/)).

IAS 7 permits operating cash flows to be presented directly, by showing major classes of cash receipts and payments, or indirectly, by reconciling profit to operating cash flow for non-cash items, accruals, and items assigned to investing or financing activities. Non-cash investing and financing transactions are excluded from the cash-flow statement but disclosed separately ([IFRS Foundation, IAS 7](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-7-statement-of-cash-flows/)).

### 2.3 Accrual accounting and timing

Accrual accounting recognizes economic activity according to applicable rules rather than simply when cash moves. Consider a credit sale of \$100 for inventory that cost \$60:

At delivery, if the revenue-recognition conditions are satisfied:

\[
\text{Accounts receivable} +100,\quad
\text{Revenue} +100,
\]

and:

\[
\text{Cost of sales} +60,\quad
\text{Inventory} -60.
\]

Profit rises by \$40, but no customer cash has yet arrived. When the customer pays, cash increases and receivables decrease; no new revenue is created.

This separation is essential. Receivables, inventory, payables, deferred revenue, depreciation, provisions, and capitalized expenditure can cause profit and cash flow to diverge. Neither measure is inherently superior:

- Profit attempts to measure performance across a period.
- Cash reveals liquidity, collection, payment, and investment consequences.
- The balance sheet carries timing differences and remaining resources or obligations.

The statements should therefore be reconciled as a system.

## 3. From accounting categories to unit economics

### 3.1 Define the unit before calculating the margin

A **unit economic model** estimates revenues, costs, cash flows, and sometimes capital requirements for a specified unit. The appropriate unit follows the decision:

- a product item for pricing or product-mix decisions;
- an order for fulfillment design;
- a production batch for setup and yield decisions;
- a customer or cohort for acquisition and retention;
- a contract for project acceptance;
- a store, route, machine, or territory for capacity expansion.

A model titled “profit per customer” is uninterpretable unless it states the customer definition, time horizon, included cash flows, allocation rules, and treatment of churn, refunds, credit losses, shared resources, and capital.

Suppose an order has a selling price \(P\), direct product cost \(c_p\), payment fee \(c_f\), variable fulfillment cost \(c_v\), expected refund and warranty cost \(c_r\), and incremental support cost \(c_s\). Its contribution is:

\[
CM_{\text{order}}=P-c_p-c_f-c_v-c_r-c_s.
\]

The contribution-margin rate is:

\[
CM\%=\frac{CM_{\text{order}}}{P}.
\]

This is not necessarily accounting gross profit. It is a managerial construct whose validity depends on its definition. Excluding a cost does not make that cost disappear; it means the model answers a narrower question.

### 3.2 Marginal, avoidable, and fully loaded costs

Three cost concepts should be separated:

1. **Marginal cost**: the additional cost caused by one more unit in the current operating range.
2. **Avoidable cost**: the cost that would cease if a product, customer, or activity were discontinued.
3. **Fully loaded cost**: direct cost plus allocated shares of common or overhead costs.

A short-run order-acceptance decision may depend on marginal cash contribution and available bottleneck capacity. A long-run product decision must cover capacity renewal, engineering, management, compliance, facilities, and other resources that are not variable order by order. Fully allocated cost can reveal the burden of maintaining a business, but arbitrary allocations can make a healthy product appear unattractive or disguise a product consuming disproportionate shared capacity.

When capacity is constrained, contribution per unit of the constraint can be more informative than contribution per item:

\[
\text{Contribution per bottleneck hour}
=
\frac{\text{Contribution per unit}}
{\text{bottleneck hours per unit}}.
\]

A product earning \$30 contribution while using three bottleneck hours yields \$10 per bottleneck hour. Another earning \$20 while using one hour yields \$20 per bottleneck hour. If demand exists and all other relevant effects are equal, the second product produces more contribution from the scarce resource even though its per-item margin is lower.

### 3.3 Customer acquisition and lifetime value

For a customer cohort:

\[
CAC=
\frac{\text{incremental acquisition expenditure attributable to the cohort}}
{\text{new customers acquired in the cohort}}.
\]

The denominator must distinguish paying customers from leads, registrations, trials, and reactivated customers. The numerator must specify whether it includes media, sales compensation, discounts, onboarding, agency fees, creative production, and shared marketing staff.

A defensible customer lifetime value calculation is a discounted cohort cash-flow model:

\[
LTV=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{S_t(m_t)-k_t}{(1+r)^t},
\]

where:

- \(S_t\) is expected revenue from the original cohort in period \(t\);
- \(m_t\) is the applicable contribution rate;
- \(k_t\) is cohort-level servicing or retention cost not already included;
- \(r\) is a discount rate reflecting time and risk;
- \(T\) is the explicit horizon.

Net customer value at acquisition is then:

\[
\text{Customer NPV}=LTV-CAC.
\]

This formulation exposes assumptions that a single “LTV/CAC ratio” conceals. Retention may change with customer age. Acquisition channels may attract different populations. Prices, usage, service costs, bad debts, and discount rates may change. A firm should report both forecast values and realized cohort behavior, label the observation window, and show sensitivity to the assumptions.

Unit economics can also be distorted by shared benefits and network effects. A low-margin product might improve retention, fill otherwise idle capacity, or attract demand for a profitable complement. Conversely, apparently positive customer contribution may depend on an underpriced shared platform whose replacement cost is omitted.

### 3.4 Why published unit metrics require skepticism

Unit economics, adjusted EBITDA, and free cash flow are often non-GAAP measures rather than standardized accounting subtotals. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission warns that similarly named non-GAAP measures may not be comparable across firms. It also says a measure may be misleading if it excludes normal recurring cash operating costs, changes its adjustments inconsistently between periods, excludes losses without treating comparable gains consistently, or substitutes individually tailored recognition rules for GAAP ([SEC, *Non-GAAP Financial Measures Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations*](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/corporation-finance-interpretations/non-gaap-financial-measures)).

The SEC specifically identifies as potentially misleading a “contribution margin” labeled “net revenue” and requires non-GAAP measures to be clearly described rather than given labels resembling standardized GAAP line items ([SEC guidance](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/corporation-finance-interpretations/non-gaap-financial-measures)). Disclosure and reconciliation help, but the SEC notes that a measure can be so misleading that extensive explanation does not cure it.

An operator should consequently maintain a metric dictionary specifying:

- exact formula and data sources;
- unit and cohort definitions;
- inclusions and exclusions;
- treatment of shared costs and capital expenditure;
- reconciliation to accounting records where possible;
- ownership and approval of definition changes;
- comparable historical series after any change.

## 4. Cash: the timing and survival layer

### 4.1 Working capital

Operating cash depends not only on profit but on the timing of inventory purchases, customer collection, and supplier payment. A common analytical approximation is:

\[
\text{Operating working capital}
=
\text{operating current assets}
-
\text{operating current liabilities}.
\]

The exact components depend on the business and analytical purpose. Cash and interest-bearing debt are often excluded when the aim is to isolate operating investment.

Three timing measures are:

\[
DSO=
\frac{\text{average accounts receivable}}
{\text{credit sales}}
\times \text{days},
\]

\[
DIO=
\frac{\text{average inventory}}
{\text{cost of sales}}
\times \text{days},
\]

\[
DPO=
\frac{\text{average accounts payable}}
{\text{purchases or an appropriate cost base}}
\times \text{days}.
\]

An approximate cash-conversion cycle is:

\[
CCC=DIO+DSO-DPO.
\]

These measures are diagnostic, not universal laws. Seasonal balances can make year-end snapshots misleading. DPO calculated with cost of sales rather than purchases is only an approximation. Subscription businesses, banks, insurers, marketplaces, and project contractors may require different measures.

Growth can consume cash when receivables and inventory rise before collections. It can release cash when customers prepay or suppliers finance the operating cycle. Faster collections are beneficial only if they do not rely on coercive practices that damage demand; longer supplier terms may improve reported cash while increasing supplier fragility or prices.

### 4.2 Profit, operating cash flow, and free cash flow

A simple reconciliation is:

\[
\text{Operating cash flow}
\approx
\text{Net income}
+\text{non-cash charges}
-\Delta\text{operating working capital}
+\text{other adjustments}.
\]

“Free cash flow” has no single universal definition. A common version is:

\[
FCF=\text{Operating cash flow}-\text{capital expenditures}.
\]

But this can conceal important distinctions: maintenance versus growth investment, cash purchases versus leases, acquisitions versus internally built assets, and stock-based compensation versus cash compensation. Every free-cash-flow presentation should therefore state its formula and reconcile it to the cash-flow statement.

Amazon’s 2025 Form 10-K illustrates why a system view is necessary. The company reported 2025 consolidated net sales of \$716.924 billion, including \$426.305 billion in North America, \$161.894 billion internationally, and \$128.725 billion from AWS ([Amazon 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000004/amzn-20251231.htm)). The same filing reported \$356.414 billion of cost of sales, \$109.074 billion of fulfillment expense, and \$108.521 billion of technology and infrastructure expense. Those categories combine businesses with different demand drivers and asset requirements, so consolidated sales or gross margin alone cannot reveal product- or customer-level economics.

Amazon also reported \$128.3 billion of cash capital expenditures in 2025, primarily for technology infrastructure—mostly supporting AWS growth—and fulfillment-network capacity ([Amazon 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000110465926041036/tm263815d4_ars.pdf)). This is historical expenditure, while management’s expectation that such spending would increase in 2026 is a forecast, not an established outcome. The example shows why declining free cash flow may reflect either deteriorating operations or deliberate investment; distinguishing them requires evidence about asset utilization, incremental demand, future margins, and eventual cash returns.

### 4.3 Liquidity and runway

For an early-stage or stressed firm, runway is often estimated as:

\[
\text{Runway}
=
\frac{\text{available unrestricted cash}}
{\text{expected monthly net cash burn}}.
\]

A single average is dangerous when receipts, debt maturities, taxes, inventory builds, or annual payments are lumpy. A rolling weekly cash forecast should show base, downside, and severe-but-plausible cases. It should distinguish committed from discretionary spending and include covenant thresholds, collateral requirements, restricted cash, and financing lead times.

Liquidity is therefore a design constraint on strategy. A positive-NPV project can still be infeasible if its interim cash needs exceed available financing or if its failure would threaten the firm’s survival.

## 5. Capital allocation: converting cash into future capability

### 5.1 The allocation menu

Cash generated or raised can be allocated to:

- necessary maintenance and regulatory compliance;
- working capital;
- organic capacity or product development;
- employee and organizational capability;
- acquisitions or minority investments;
- debt reduction;
- liquidity reserves;
- dividends; or
- share repurchases.

The relevant comparison is not merely project return versus zero. It is the project’s risk-adjusted incremental value versus the best feasible alternative, including preserving financial flexibility.

For an investment requiring initial outlay \(I_0\) and expected incremental cash flows \(CF_t\):

\[
NPV=-I_0+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}.
\]

A positive estimated NPV means the modeled discounted inflows exceed the outlay. It is not proof that value will be created: the result depends on forecasts, discount rate, terminal value, taxes, working capital, cannibalization, implementation risk, and opportunity cost.

The internal rate of return solves:

\[
0=-I_0+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t}.
\]

IRR can mis-rank mutually exclusive projects of different scale or timing and may have multiple solutions when cash-flow signs change repeatedly. NPV is generally the clearer decision criterion when the goal is absolute value creation, though capacity, solvency, strategic options, and stakeholder obligations also matter.

### 5.2 Use staged evidence, not one forecast

A sound capital proposal should include:

1. the customer or operational problem;
2. the causal mechanism by which spending produces benefits;
3. baseline performance;
4. incremental cash flows rather than allocated accounting totals;
5. capacity, quality, and implementation dependencies;
6. base, upside, and downside scenarios;
7. decision milestones and leading indicators;
8. a precommitted stop, redesign, or expansion rule;
9. post-investment review against the original forecast.

For reversible or uncertain projects, staged investment can purchase information before the full commitment. A pilot may test conversion, cycle time, defect reduction, or willingness to pay. Yet pilots can also mislead when they use unusually favorable customers, excess managerial attention, temporary discounts, or unconstrained capacity. Evidence should be tested under conditions representative of scale.

### 5.3 Repurchases, dividends, and reserves

Share repurchases create value for continuing owners only under particular conditions: the shares must be purchased below defensible intrinsic value, the firm must retain adequate liquidity, and the repurchase must beat available alternatives after risk. Repurchasing overvalued shares transfers value from continuing owners to sellers.

Berkshire Hathaway’s policy provides a concrete, company-specific example rather than a universal rule. Its 2024 annual report said repurchases were permitted below conservatively determined intrinsic value and would not be made if they reduced consolidated cash, cash equivalents, and U.S. Treasury bills below \$30 billion; Berkshire spent \$2.9 billion on repurchases in 2024 ([Berkshire Hathaway 2024 Form 10-K](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/202410-k.pdf)). At year-end, its insurance and other businesses held \$318.0 billion of cash, equivalents, and Treasury bills net of unsettled-purchase payables, while 2024 consolidated capital expenditure was \$19.0 billion. These facts describe Berkshire’s exceptional balance sheet and operating mix; they do not establish an appropriate reserve or repurchase policy for other firms.

Berkshire’s 2025 shareholder letter stated that it would repurchase shares when they traded below its conservatively estimated intrinsic value and retain earnings while each retained dollar was reasonably expected to create more than one dollar of shareholder market value ([Berkshire Hathaway 2025 shareholder letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)). Those are management’s allocation principles and expectations, not independently verified guarantees of future returns.

Dividends provide owners with cash and reduce managerial discretion over it. Reserves protect continuity and preserve options, but excessive idle cash can carry an opportunity cost. Debt repayment can reduce distress risk and interest expense, yet eliminating low-cost debt may be inferior to a sufficiently attractive investment. The decision depends on cash-flow volatility, covenants, refinancing conditions, taxes, asset durability, and the consequences of being wrong.

## 6. Governance and the evidence loop

Financial measures change behavior. If managers are rewarded for revenue, they may discount excessively or extend weak credit. If rewarded for gross margin, they may shift costs below the gross-profit line. If rewarded for EBITDA, they may favor capitalized expenditure or ignore capital intensity. If rewarded for free cash flow in one period, they may delay necessary maintenance or supplier payments.

Good governance therefore separates:

- metric production from operational ownership;
- proposal sponsorship from approval;
- forecast preparation from post-investment review;
- controllable performance from external effects;
- historical facts from forecasts and scenarios.

A practical monthly review can follow this loop:

\[
\text{Demand evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{operating drivers}
\rightarrow
\text{accrual results}
\rightarrow
\text{cash consequences}
\rightarrow
\text{capital decisions}
\rightarrow
\text{new evidence}.
\]

The review should reconcile operational data with the ledger, compare cohort forecasts with realized behavior, explain profit-to-cash differences, inspect bottleneck utilization and quality, and update—not silently overwrite—prior assumptions. Maintaining dated forecast vintages makes learning visible: managers can determine whether errors arose from demand, price, retention, cost, schedule, capacity, or financing assumptions.

## 7. Limitations and conclusions

No single metric captures the firm.

Accounting statements are standardized and reconcilable but necessarily aggregate transactions and rely on recognition, classification, and estimation judgments. Unit economics can reveal mechanisms hidden by company totals, but results are definition-sensitive and can omit shared costs, capacity renewal, risk, or adverse selection. Cash measures expose liquidity and timing, yet temporary working-capital movements can exaggerate or suppress underlying performance. Capital-allocation models impose discipline, but their outputs remain conditional forecasts.

The most defensible practice is triangulation:

1. Start with the customer promise and operating process.
2. Map physical and contractual events into accounting entries.
3. Define units and cost behavior for the decision at hand.
4. Reconcile profit to cash and model working-capital timing.
5. Evaluate investments with incremental, risk-adjusted cash flows.
6. Preserve liquidity under downside conditions.
7. Record assumptions, decision rights, and forecast vintages.
8. Compare realized outcomes with the original model and revise future policy.

The goal is not a perfectly precise number. It is an evidence-producing financial system in which definitions are explicit, totals reconcile, uncertainty is visible, incentives are considered, and decisions can be corrected as outcomes arrive.
