# Customer discovery, market intelligence, and demand evidence

## Practical question

How can a firm learn about demand without turning interviews, surveys, competitor observations, or early sales into stronger evidence than they are?

Customer discovery is an evidence-producing practice, not a ritual that certifies a founder's preferred story. A useful process keeps at least four objects distinct: the people sampled, what they say in a research setting, what they do under real constraints, and the commercial decision the firm must make. These objects interact, but none substitutes automatically for another.

## Voice of the customer as a translation process

Griffin and Hauser describe voice-of-customer work as a structured movement from customer language to a hierarchy of needs that can inform product development. Their account supplies techniques for eliciting and organizing needs; it does not warrant treating every utterance as an unbiased preference, every respondent as representative, or every ranked need as willingness to pay.

The translation itself is organizational work. Researchers choose participants, prompts, coding categories, aggregation rules, and which disagreements to retain. Those choices should be visible in the resulting claim.

## Market orientation as organizational information flow

Narver and Slater operationalize market orientation through customer orientation, competitor orientation, and interfunctional coordination, and relate that construct to profitability in a bounded sample. The useful lesson is relational: demand learning is not owned only by marketing. Sales, support, operations, finance, and product work each observe different parts of the exchange. Coordination can improve the firm's picture, while incentives and reporting conventions can also filter it.

Their cross-sectional evidence is not a universal causal law. Industry, organization form, measurement choices, and the period observed constrain what can be inferred.

## Stated preferences, choices, and reference points

Kahneman and Tversky show that observed choices can depend on framing, reference points, and asymmetric treatment of gains and losses. For customer research this is a warning against imagining a stable preference waiting to be extracted by a neutral question. Interview wording, current alternatives, switching costs, budgets, procurement rules, and the perceived status quo can change the choice problem.

This does not mean interviews are useless or that only completed purchases count. It means evidence should be typed. A report might distinguish a remembered problem, an observed workaround, an expressed preference, a signed commitment, a paid trial, retention, and expansion. Each answers a different question.

## A bounded evidence ladder

For a specific decision, a team can record:

1. the claim being tested and the costly action it would change;
2. the sampled population and important people excluded from it;
3. the observation method, prompt, incentive, and setting;
4. descriptive evidence before prescriptive interpretation;
5. counterevidence and disconfirming cases;
6. uncertainty about transfer across industries, scales, jurisdictions, and organization forms;
7. the next reversible test whose information value exceeds its cost.

No rung universally dominates another. A purchase is stronger evidence of one transaction than an interview, but a failed purchase does not by itself reveal whether the cause was price, trust, procurement, access, timing, or product fit. Behavioral evidence still requires interpretation.

## Market intelligence and competitor evidence

Competitor pricing pages, job postings, product changes, public filings, reviews, and distribution partnerships can constrain a firm's model of the market. They remain selected observations. Public signals omit private discounts, failed initiatives, internal costs, and customers who silently left. Copying a visible practice may import a solution without the conditions that made it viable.

Market intelligence is most useful when it changes a comparison: which customer, alternative, capability, or constraint matters for a pending decision. A large undirected archive can create the appearance of knowledge while raising coordination cost.

## Negative controls

- Interview enthusiasm is not a purchase commitment.
- One large customer's request is not automatically a market segment.
- A statistically neat survey does not repair a biased sampling frame.
- Competitor adoption does not prove a practice will work under another firm's capabilities or obligations.
- "Data driven" does not eliminate judgment; it relocates judgment into collection, measurement, and decision rules.
- Customer discovery is not permission to collect personal data without purpose limitation, consent where required, security, and retention limits.

## Situated use

This packet is framed for small and medium US-based commercial organizations using public or consented research and making reversible product or service decisions. Regulated procurement, labor relations, healthcare, finance, public administration, multinational privacy duties, and high-capital investments require additional domain evidence and legal review.
