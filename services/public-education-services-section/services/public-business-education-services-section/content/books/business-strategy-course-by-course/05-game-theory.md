# Game theory and business strategy

## What you will be able to do

- Identify the players in a competitive situation and analyze their
  motivations and capabilities.
- Map likely responses to your moves with payoff tables and game trees.
- Find dominant strategies and equilibria, and solve sequential games by
  looking ahead.
- See your business from other players' perspectives, including what makes
  a threat or promise believable.
- Change the game to your advantage.

## Why game theory

Your results depend not only on your moves but on everyone else's. A price
cut that would win customers if rivals held still may start a price war if
they respond. **Game theory** studies such interdependent decisions; Avinash Dixit and
Barry Nalebuff's *The Art of Strategy* is a readable guide to using it
[@dixit-nalebuff2008]. A
**game** has **players**, the **strategies** each can choose, the
**payoffs** each gets from every combination, and what each knows when it
chooses.

## Players, motivations and capabilities

Start by listing every player whose moves affect your payoff: rivals,
customers, suppliers, complementors, regulators. For each, ask:

- What do they want? (Profit, share, survival, reputation.)
- What can they do? (Their capabilities and constraints.)
- What do they believe about you?

The last question matters because players act on what they believe, and your
reputation is part of the game.

## Simultaneous games: the payoff table

When players choose at the same time, or without seeing each other's choice,
write the game as a **payoff table**: rows for one player's strategies,
columns for the other's, and each cell giving both payoffs.

A **dominant strategy** is best whatever the other player does. If both have
one, that pair is the likely outcome. More generally, a **Nash equilibrium**
is a set of strategies in which no player can do better by changing its own
choice alone, given the others' choices; John Nash proved that every finite
game has at least one, allowing mixed strategies [@nash1950].

The **prisoner's dilemma** is the famous case where each player's dominant
strategy leads to an outcome worse for both than cooperating would be: two
firms each do better by cutting price whatever the other does, and both end
up worse off than if both had held. In business it explains price wars and
advertising races.

## Repeated games

Most business games repeat: the same firms meet every month. Repetition
changes things, because today's move affects tomorrow's response. In Robert
Axelrod's tournaments of repeated prisoner's dilemmas, a simple strategy,
**tit for tat** (cooperate first, then do what the other did last time), did
consistently well: it's nice, retaliates, forgives and is easy to read
[@axelrod1984]. Repetition is why rivals can sustain restraint without any
agreement, and why reputation is valuable.

## Sequential games: looking ahead

When players move in turn, draw a **game tree** and solve it by **backward
induction**: start at the last move, work out what that player will choose,
and move back to the first. The result shows which threats and promises are
**credible**: a threat is credible only if carrying it out would be in the
threatener's interest when the time comes.

Thomas Schelling showed that a player can gain by limiting its own options: a
**commitment** that makes a threat credible, such as an investment that can't
be undone, changes what others expect and so what they do [@schelling1960].

## Changing the game

Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff argued that the biggest gains come not
from playing the game better but from changing it [@brandenburger-nalebuff1995;
@brandenburger-nalebuff1996]. Their elements, **PARTS**:

- **Players:** bring in new players (another supplier, a complementor) or
  change who's in the game.
- **Added values:** raise your own added value (what the game would lose
  without you) or lower others'.
- **Rules:** change the rules, as with contracts, most-favored-customer
  clauses or loyalty programs.
- **Tactics:** shape what others believe.
- **Scope:** link or separate games, widening or narrowing the field.

The authors called the mix of competition and cooperation **co-opetition**:
the same player can be a competitor in dividing value and a complementor in
creating it.

## Worked example: FieldTable and Continental

**Players and motivations.** Continental: a national distributor seeking
profit and share across hundreds of markets; FieldTable is small to it.
FieldTable: survival and growth in one region. Also in the game: the farms
(who can sell to either), the restaurants, and the courier co-op.

**A simultaneous game.** FieldTable is deciding whether to cut its fee;
Continental whether to match local prices on produce. Monthly profit, in
thousands of dollars (FieldTable, Continental in this region):

| | Continental holds | Continental matches |
|---|---|---|
| **FieldTable holds** | 40, 400 | 20, 370 |
| **FieldTable cuts** | 55, 380 | 30, 360 |

FieldTable: if Continental holds, cutting pays 55 against 40; if it matches,
30 against 20. Cutting dominates. Continental: if FieldTable holds, holding
pays 400 against 370; if FieldTable cuts, 380 against 360. Holding
dominates. Equilibrium: FieldTable cuts, Continental holds: (55, 380).
Matching local prices costs Continental more than the customers it would win
back.

**A sequential game.** Continental is considering its own local-sourcing
program. If it enters, FieldTable can fight (deep discounts) or accommodate.
Payoffs (Continental, FieldTable):

- Continental stays out: (0, 100).
- Continental enters, FieldTable accommodates: (30, 50).
- Continental enters, FieldTable fights: (−20, 10).

Backward induction: if Continental enters, FieldTable prefers accommodating
(50 against 10), so its threat to fight isn't credible. Continental expects
30 from entering against 0 from staying out, so it enters. Outcome: (30, 50).

**Changing the game with a commitment.** FieldTable signs two-year exclusive
supply agreements with the region's 40 best farms, costing it 10 in
premiums. Now an entering Continental can't get the supply that makes local
sourcing worth doing: entering yields it −5 whatever FieldTable does, while
staying out yields 0. Continental stays out, and FieldTable earns 100 − 10 =
90 instead of 50. The commitment worked by changing the players available
to Continental (PARTS: players and rules), not by threatening a price war.

## Apply it to your organization

1. Name the players whose moves most affect your results, and for each, what
   they want and what they can do.
2. Pick a move you're considering. Build a two-by-two payoff table with your
   main rival's likely response. Does either side have a dominant strategy?
3. Is there a threat a rival has made, or you have made, that wouldn't be
   carried out when the time came? What commitment would make it credible?
4. Using PARTS, name one way you could change the game rather than play it
   better.

## Answers

1. A set of strategies, one per player, such that no player can do better by
   changing its own strategy while the others keep theirs.
2. For A: advertising pays 10 against 5 if B advertises, and 30 against 20 if
   B doesn't; advertising dominates. B is symmetric, so advertising dominates
   for B too. Equilibrium: both advertise, (10, 10).
3. Yes: each firm's dominant strategy (advertise) leads to (10, 10), worse
   for both than (20, 20) if neither advertised.
4. Because each firm's move today affects the other's response tomorrow, so
   cheating on restraint can be punished and cooperation rewarded; reputation
   carries forward.
5. Because once Continental has entered, fighting pays FieldTable 10 while
   accommodating pays 50, so it wouldn't actually carry the threat out.
6. They made entry unprofitable for Continental (−5 against 0 for staying
   out), so it stays out; FieldTable earns 90 instead of 50.
7. Players, added values, rules, tactics, scope.
8. For example: what it wants (growth in a new customer segment, using its
   existing drivers); what it can do (large marketing budget, a delivery
   fleet, but no farm relationships); what it believes about FieldTable (that
   it's small and easy to outspend).
