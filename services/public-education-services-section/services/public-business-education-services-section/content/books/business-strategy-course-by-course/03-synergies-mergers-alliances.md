# Synergies, mergers and alliances

*Companion to LSM313, Synergies, Mergers, and Alliances. Before you start:
Chapter 1's value created.*

## What you will be able to do

- Identify potential synergies that would raise value creation and profit.
- Choose how to realize a synergy: build it, ally, or acquire.
- Evaluate a potential partner's fit.
- Plan the synergy's realization, anticipate what goes wrong, and track
  results.

## What a synergy is

A **synergy** exists when two businesses together create more value than
apart. Using Chapter 1's terms, combining raises customers' willingness to
pay, lowers costs, or both, beyond what either could do alone:

- **Cost synergies:** shared facilities, purchasing or routes lower costs.
- **Revenue synergies:** combined offers, shared customers or channels raise
  sales or willingness to pay.
- **Capability synergies:** one side's skills or assets make the other's
  products better.

Synergies are easy to promise and hard to deliver. Mark Sirower studied
acquisitions and found that acquirers often paid premiums that their promised
synergies could never repay, because the premium is paid up front and the
synergies must be earned later, against competitors who respond
[@sirower1997]. Widely cited estimates put the share of acquisitions that
fail at 70 to 90 percent [@christensen2011]; the exact rate depends on how
failure is measured, but the direction is consistent: realizing synergies is
the hard part.

## Build, borrow or buy

There are three ways to get what you need [@capron-mitchell2012]:

- **Build (organic growth):** develop it yourself. Most control, slowest,
  fits when you have the capabilities and time.
- **Borrow (contracts and alliances):** license, contract or partner. Faster
  and cheaper to exit; less control.
- **Buy (merger or acquisition):** acquire the business that has it. Fastest
  route to full control; most costly and hardest to integrate.

Oliver Williamson's transaction-cost economics explains part of the choice:
when a relationship needs large investments specific to the partner, a
contract leaves each side exposed to the other's opportunism (the **hold-up
problem**), and owning the partner becomes more attractive [@williamson1985].

Jeffrey Dyer, Prashant Kale and Harbir Singh gave practical tests for
choosing between allying and acquiring [@dyer-kale-singh2004]:

- **The kind of synergy.** *Modular* synergies (each side does its part
  separately and pools results) suit non-equity alliances. *Sequential*
  synergies (one side's work feeds the other's) suit equity alliances.
  *Reciprocal* synergies (the two must work closely together throughout) suit
  acquisitions.
- **The resources.** Soft resources (people, know-how) are easily lost in an
  acquisition; redundant resources favor acquisition, where costs can be cut.
- **Market uncertainty.** High uncertainty favors alliances, which keep
  options open.
- **Competition for the partner.** If rivals are courting the same partner,
  moving to acquire may be necessary.

## Partner fit

A good partner brings what you lack and fits how you work. Evaluate:

- **Strategic fit:** do the goals point the same way, and does each gain?
- **Resource fit:** are the capabilities complementary rather than
  duplicated?
- **Organizational and cultural fit:** do decision-making, pace and values
  match well enough to work together?
- **Financial fit:** can each side sustain its part?
- **Track record:** how has the partner behaved in past deals?

## Realizing and tracking the synergy

Most synergies are lost after the deal. Plan the realization before signing:

1. State each synergy as a number, with an owner and a date.
2. Map what must change to realize it: systems, routes, people, prices.
3. Anticipate the usual failures: customers leaving during the transition,
   key people quitting, cultures clashing, rivals attacking while attention
   is inward.
4. Track realized against planned synergy regularly, and stop or change
   course when it isn't coming.

## Worked example: FieldTable's delivery question

FieldTable spends about $4 a case on delivery through the courier
cooperative, and wants cheaper, more reliable deliveries with cold storage.
Three options:

- **Build:** buy refrigerated vans and hire drivers. Full control; about two
  years to reach the co-op's route density; the capital would be spent on
  something FieldTable doesn't know how to run.
- **Borrow:** a deeper alliance with the courier co-op: shared route
  planning, a jointly leased cold room, and a volume commitment in exchange
  for a lower rate.
- **Buy:** acquire a small cold-storage and delivery firm for sale in the
  region.

**Applying the tests.** The synergy is sequential: FieldTable's orders feed
the courier's routes, and each side can do its part without working inside
the other. That points to an equity alliance, with a joint investment
such as the shared cold room, not an acquisition. The key resources are soft (the co-op's drivers and their
knowledge of kitchens' back doors), which an acquisition could lose. Market
uncertainty is high (Chapter 2's scenarios). No rival is courting the co-op.
All four tests favor the alliance.

**Fit.** Strategic: both want more local delivery volume. Resource:
complementary (orders and software; drivers and vans). Cultural: both
cooperative-minded and small. Track record: four years of reliable service.

**Realization plan.** Synergy targets: delivery cost from $4.00 to $3.20 a
case within six months (owner: FieldTable's operations lead); spoilage below
1 percent with the shared cold room (owner: the co-op). Risk: the co-op's
drivers see route changes as extra work; mitigation: plan routes with them.
Tracked monthly; review at six months.

## Apply it to your organization

1. Name one capability your organization lacks. List a way to build it, a
   way to borrow it and a way to buy it, with the rough cost and time of
   each.
2. Is the synergy modular, sequential or reciprocal? Which way of getting it
   do Dyer, Kale and Singh's tests point to?
3. For the most likely partner, score strategic, resource, cultural and
   financial fit, and note its track record.
4. Write each expected synergy as a number, with an owner and a date.

## Answers

1. Cost: two bakeries sharing one delivery route. Revenue: a bookstore and a
   café selling each other's products to shared customers.
2. That acquirers often paid premiums up front that the promised synergies
   could never repay.
3. When one side makes large investments specific to a partner, the partner
   can exploit that dependence to renegotiate; owning the partner removes the
   risk.
4. Reciprocal synergies, where the two must work closely and continuously
   together.
5. Because an alliance keeps options open and is cheaper to exit if the
   market goes another way.
6. Any four of: strategic, resource, organizational and cultural, financial
   fit; track record.
7. So that realization can be tracked and someone is accountable; most
   synergies are lost when no one owns them after the deal.
