# Value creation and profitability

*Companion to LSM311, Value Creation and Profitability. No prerequisites.*

## What you will be able to do

- Diagnose a market: who the target customers are and how a business can
  create value for them.
- Measure value created and value captured, and explain why a business needs
  both.
- Align a business's resources with how it creates value.
- Identify and assess the threats to a business's profits using the five
  forces.
- Choose strategies to mitigate those threats, including working with
  complementors.

## Value created and value captured

A transaction creates value when the buyer values the product more than it
costs to make. Adam Brandenburger and Harborne Stuart made this precise
[@brandenburger-stuart1996]:

- **Willingness to pay (WTP):** the most a customer would pay.
- **Supplier opportunity cost:** the least a supplier would accept, which is
  what it could earn elsewhere.
- **Value created** = WTP − supplier opportunity cost.

The value created is then divided. The **customer's surplus** is WTP minus
the price. The **supplier's surplus** is what it's paid minus its opportunity
cost. The **firm's share** is what's left between the price it charges and
what it pays. Strategy has two jobs: widen the gap (raise willingness to pay
or lower costs) and capture a share of it. A business that creates value but
captures none, because rivals compete its prices down, has no profit; one
that captures value without creating it loses customers.

## Diagnosing the market

Market diagnosis asks who the target customers are, what they need that
isn't met, and what raises their willingness to pay. The answer is specific:
not "restaurants," but "independent restaurants whose menus change with the
season and who advertise local sourcing." Then ask how the business can raise
those customers' WTP (by quality, reliability, convenience or meaning) or
lower the cost of serving them, beyond what rivals already offer.

## Resources and advantage

An advantage lasts only if rivals can't easily copy it. Jay Barney's
resource-based view says a resource gives sustained advantage when it's
**valuable** (it raises WTP or lowers cost), **rare**, **inimitable** (hard
to copy, because of history, complexity or relationships) and
**non-substitutable** [@barney1991]. Aligning resources means directing
investment to the few resources that meet those tests and that the chosen
way of creating value depends on, and not spending evenly on everything.

## Threats to profit: the five forces

Michael Porter described five forces that divide an industry's value
between its firms and everyone else [@porter1979; @porter2008]:

1. **Rivalry among existing competitors:** price wars, feature races.
2. **Threat of new entrants:** kept down by barriers such as scale, networks,
   brand or regulation.
3. **Bargaining power of buyers:** strong when buyers are large, concentrated
   or can switch easily.
4. **Bargaining power of suppliers:** strong when suppliers are few or their
   input is essential.
5. **Threat of substitutes:** other ways of meeting the same need.

The stronger the forces, the less of the value created the industry's firms
keep. Assess each force for your industry and ask which is squeezing profit
most, and which you could weaken.

## Complementors and the value net

Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff added a sixth kind of player that the
five forces leave out: the **complementor**, whose product makes yours more
valuable to customers, as software makes a computer more valuable
[@brandenburger-nalebuff1996]. Their **value net** places a business among
customers, suppliers, competitors and complementors. Complementors create
win-win opportunities: making the pie larger together, even while competing
over how to divide it.

## Mitigating threats

Responses match the force:

- **Rivalry:** differentiate so customers don't compare on price alone.
- **Entry:** build what's hard to copy: relationships, networks, reputation.
- **Buyer power:** serve many smaller buyers; make switching costly by adding
  value (integration, service), not by trapping them.
- **Supplier power:** multiply suppliers or make yourself their best outlet.
- **Substitutes:** raise the value of your offer relative to the substitute.
- **Complementors:** partner to grow the whole market.

## Worked example: FieldTable's value and its threats

**Value created, one case of heirloom tomatoes.** A chef will pay up to $48
for a case picked the day before (WTP). The farm could sell it at the
farmers' market for $20 after its costs and time (opportunity cost). Value
created: $48 − $20 = $28. FieldTable charges the restaurant $40 and pays the
farm $30. The chef keeps $8 of surplus, the farm $10, and FieldTable $10,
from which it pays about $4 for pickup and delivery. The $28 is split 8 + 10
+ 10.

**Diagnosis.** Target: independent restaurants with seasonal menus that
advertise local sourcing. What raises their WTP: freshness, traceability
(which farm), and the story on the menu. What Continental can't match: a
next-day, named-farm supply chain.

**Resources.** FieldTable's relationships with 120 farms took four years to
build (rare, hard to copy); its software is valuable but imitable. So it
invests in farm relationships and in data on what each farm will have next
week, not in software features a rival could copy.

**Five forces.** Rivalry: high (Continental's prices and range). Entry:
moderate (software is easy; a farm network isn't). Buyers: moderate, though
three restaurant groups buy 20 percent of volume. Suppliers: low individually,
but the best farms are courted by everyone. Substitutes: high (chefs can drive
to the farmers' market). The biggest squeeze: substitutes and rivalry.

**Mitigation.** Against substitutes: guaranteed next-day delivery that saves
a chef's morning. Against rivalry: differentiate on traceability rather than
price. With a complementor: a local restaurant association promotes
"FieldTable-sourced" menus, which raises diners' willingness to pay and so
restaurants'.

## Apply it to your organization

1. Pick one product and one target customer group. Estimate their
   willingness to pay and your suppliers' opportunity cost. How much value is
   created, and roughly how is it split between customers, suppliers and you?
2. Name the one thing that most raises this group's willingness to pay. Is it
   something rivals can copy? Test it against Barney's four questions.
3. Rate each of the five forces high, medium or low for your industry, with
   one fact for each rating. Which one squeezes your profit most?
4. Name one complementor and one way you could grow the market together.

## Check yourself

1. A customer would pay up to $100 for a service; the supplier's
   opportunity cost is $60. The firm charges $85 and pays the supplier $70.
   How much value is created, and how is it divided?
2. Why can a business create value but earn no profit?
3. Which of Barney's four tests does FieldTable's software fail?
4. A few giant hospital chains buy most of a medical device maker's output.
   Which force is strong, and why?
5. What's a complementor? Give an example for a coffee shop.
6. Name one way to weaken the threat of substitutes.
7. In the worked example, what share of the value created does FieldTable
   keep after delivery costs?
8. A bakery says its target market is "people who like bread." Restate it as
   a diagnosed target, and name one thing that would raise that group's
   willingness to pay.

## Answers

1. Value created: 100 − 60 = $40. Customer surplus 100 − 85 = $15; supplier
   surplus 70 − 60 = $10; firm 85 − 70 = $15.
2. Because rivalry or other forces can push prices down until the business
   captures none of the value it creates.
3. Inimitability: software can be copied by a determined rival.
4. Buyer power: buyers are large and concentrated, so they can demand lower
   prices.
5. A business whose product makes yours more valuable to customers; for a
   coffee shop, a nearby bookstore or a coworking space.
6. Raise the value of your offer relative to the substitute, for example by
   adding convenience or reliability the substitute lacks.
7. $10 − $4 = $6 of the $28, about 21 percent.
8. For example: "office workers within a block who buy lunch on weekdays and
   want something fresh and quick." Raising their willingness to pay: ready
   to collect at a set time, with no queue.
