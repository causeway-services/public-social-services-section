## Relationships as a source of advantage

A business is a web of relationships: with suppliers, customers, partners,
complementors and its own people, which coordinate the assets and skills it
needs. Jeffrey Dyer and Harbir Singh argued that some of a firm's advantage
comes not from what it owns but from its relationships: **relation-specific
assets** (investments tailored to a partner), **knowledge-sharing routines**,
**complementary resources** and **effective governance** can create value
neither party could create alone [@dyer-singh1998].

## When contracts aren't enough

Contracts can't specify everything, especially in long relationships where
circumstances change. Much of what makes a relationship work is a
**relational contract**: shared understandings about how each will behave,
sustained because both value the future of the relationship more than any
gain from cheating today. Robert Gibbons and Rebecca Henderson showed that
such understandings are hard to build and hard for rivals to copy, which is
why they can be a lasting advantage [@gibbons-henderson2012]. They need two
things: **credibility** (each believes the other will keep its word) and
**clarity** (each understands what the other expects).

## Assessing a relationship's health

Start by listing key relationships: those where losing the partner would
seriously hurt the business, or where a better relationship would add
substantial value. For each, score its health on:

- **Mutual benefit:** does each side gain enough to want to continue?
- **Trust and credibility:** do both keep promises?
- **Clarity of expectations:** do both understand what's expected, including
  what isn't written down?
- **Communication:** are problems raised early and handled together?
- **Dependence and balance:** is one side so dependent it can be exploited,
  or so powerful it can walk away?
- **Adaptability:** can the relationship adjust when conditions change?

## Keeping relationships healthy

Healthy relationships are maintained deliberately:

- **Clarify expectations** regularly, especially after changes.
- **Share information** that helps the partner plan.
- **Invest in the relationship** where it pays both sides: shared tools,
  joint planning, people who know each other.
- **Handle problems early and fairly**, including when the contract would
  let you take advantage.
- **Review** the relationship's health on a schedule, together.

## Common challenges

- **Hold-up:** one side exploits the other's relation-specific investment
  (Chapter 3). Guard with balanced investments or explicit protections.
- **Drift in expectations:** each side's understanding changes without the
  other knowing. Guard with regular reviews.
- **Asymmetric dependence:** one side has no alternatives. Guard by keeping
  options, and by not exploiting a partner's dependence, which destroys
  credibility.
- **People changing:** relationships held by one person leave with them.
  Guard by spreading contacts across both organizations.
- **Short-term temptation:** a one-time gain that costs the relationship's
  future. Guard by making the future's value visible.

## Worked example: FieldTable's key relationships

**Key relationships:** its top 20 farms (most of its best-selling produce);
the courier co-op (every delivery); the three restaurant groups (20 percent
of volume); the restaurant association (a complementor).

**Health scorecard (1 = poor, 5 = strong):**

| Dimension | Top farms | Courier co-op | Restaurant groups |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mutual benefit | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| Trust | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Clarity | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| Communication | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Balance | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| Adaptability | 4 | 3 | 3 |

**Reading it.** The restaurant groups score lowest on clarity and balance:
they expect first pick of scarce items, which FieldTable has never agreed to,
and they could switch to Continental easily. The farms score well on trust but
only moderately on clarity: some expect FieldTable to buy their surplus, which
it doesn't promise.

**Plan.** With the restaurant groups: a written allocation rule for scarce
items and a quarterly review meeting. With the farms: a seasonal planning
session each winter, where each farm says what it will grow and FieldTable
says what it expects to sell; surplus rules written down. With the co-op:
spread contacts beyond the one dispatcher who holds the relationship today.

**A challenge anticipated.** When Continental courts the top farms (Chapter
2's hardest scenario), the relational contract is FieldTable's defense:
paying farms on time every week for four years is credibility a newcomer
can't buy.

## Apply it to your organization

1. List your five most important business relationships.
2. Score each on benefit, trust, clarity, communication, balance and
   adaptability, from 1 to 5.
3. For the lowest score, write one action for the next month that would
   raise it, and who will take it.
4. Which of your relationships depends on a single person on either side?

## Answers

1. Relation-specific assets, knowledge-sharing routines, complementary
   resources and effective governance.
2. A shared understanding, much of it unwritten, of how each party will behave,
   sustained because both value the relationship's future. It needs
   credibility and clarity.
3. Because it's slow and hard to build, and rivals can't copy it by signing a
   contract.
4. People changing: build relationships with others at the supplier before
   the contact leaves.
5. Any four of: mutual benefit, trust, clarity of expectations,
   communication, dependence and balance, adaptability.
6. It destroys your credibility with that partner and others, and the
   partner may leave as soon as it can, taking future value with it.
7. It turns an unclear, disputed expectation into a clear one both can rely
   on.
8. No: losing it wouldn't seriously hurt the business, and a better
   relationship wouldn't add much value. Key relationships are those where one
   of those is true.
