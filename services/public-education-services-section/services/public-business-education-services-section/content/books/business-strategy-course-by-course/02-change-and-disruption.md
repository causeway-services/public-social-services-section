## Kinds of change

Industries change in different ways, and each calls for a different
response:

- **Gradual change:** costs, tastes and rules shift slowly; the forces of
  Chapter 1 move by degrees.
- **Shocks:** a sudden event (a pandemic, a new law, a supply failure)
  changes conditions at once.
- **Disruption:** a new approach, at first inferior on the measures the
  market values, gradually takes over.
- **Uncertainty:** the future can go more than one way and no one can know which.

Assessing change starts by asking which of these you face, and what it would
do to each of the five forces, to customers' willingness to pay, and to your
costs.

## Disruption and its conditions

Clayton Christensen distinguished **sustaining innovation**, improvement for
existing customers on the measures they already value, which incumbents are
good at, from **disruptive innovation**, which explains how
successful incumbents lose to entrants [@christensen1997;
@christensen2015]. An entrant starts either at the **low end**, with a
cheaper, simpler product for customers the incumbent overserves, or in a
**new market**, serving people who weren't customers at all. Its product is
worse on what the incumbent's best customers care about, so the incumbent,
rationally chasing its most profitable customers, ignores it. The entrant
improves until it's good enough for the mainstream, and takes it.

The theory has limits. Jill Lepore argued its case studies were chosen to fit
[@lepore2014]; Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh asked experts about the
theory's own examples and found only a minority met all its conditions
[@king-baatartogtokh2015]. Treat it as a mechanism with conditions, not a
prophecy. Before calling a rival disruptive, check:

1. Does it start with overlooked customers (low end or non-consumers)?
2. Is it worse on the incumbent's key measures, and better on others (price,
   convenience)?
3. Are incumbents rationally declining to respond?
4. Is it improving fast enough to reach mainstream customers?

## Planning under uncertainty

When the future can go more than one way, a single forecast misleads.
**Scenario planning** builds a few plausible futures and tests strategies
against them. Paul Schoemaker sets out a longer process; its core steps are
these [@schoemaker1995]:

1. Identify the forces that matter most to your business.
2. Separate what's predictable from what's uncertain.
3. Pick the two most important uncertainties, and cross them to make four
   scenarios.
4. For each scenario, ask what would happen to the business and what it
   would need.
5. Choose strategies that hold up across scenarios, and **signposts** that
   tell you which future is arriving.

## Responding to change

Responses range from defending the core, to adapting it, to building a
separate business for the new market, to exiting. Christensen's advice for
incumbents facing disruption was to address it with a separate unit whose
costs and customers suit the new market, because the core's processes and
priorities reject it. For entrants, the lesson is the reverse: find the
customers incumbents don't want.

## Resistance inside the organization

Strategies fail as often inside as outside. Kurt Lewin described change as
three movements: **unfreezing** old habits, **changing**, and **refreezing**
the new ones into routine [@lewin1947]. John Kotter listed eight steps from
his study of transformation efforts that failed: establish urgency; form a
guiding coalition; create a vision; communicate it; empower others to act;
create short-term wins; consolidate gains; and anchor changes in the culture
[@kotter1995]. A later review found support for many of the steps but little
direct testing of the sequence as a whole [@appelbaum2012], so use them as a
checklist of the places change efforts break.

Resistance usually has reasons: fear of losing status or skill, doubt that
the change will work, or being left out of the decision. Find the reasons,
involve the people affected, show early results, and change the routines and
incentives that hold the old way in place.

## Worked example: FieldTable in a changing market

**Change.** Gradual: restaurants' demand for local produce is growing.
Shock risk: a fuel price spike would raise delivery costs. Uncertain:
whether Continental will build its own local-sourcing program.

**Is FieldTable disruptive to Continental?** Check the conditions. It
started with customers Continental serves poorly (small restaurants wanting
named-farm produce): yes. It's worse on Continental's measures (range, price,
one-stop ordering) and better on others (freshness, traceability): yes.
Continental rationally ignores a small, low-margin segment: so far, yes. Is it
improving toward mainstream customers? Only slowly: its range is limited by
the region's seasons. Conclusion: a new-market foothold, not yet a disruption
of Continental's core.

**Scenarios.** Crossing fuel costs (low or high) with Continental's response
(ignores or enters local sourcing):

| | Continental ignores | Continental enters |
|---|---|---|
| **Low fuel** | Grow steadily | Compete on farm relationships |
| **High fuel** | Consolidate routes | Hardest case: defend the best farms |

A strategy that holds across all four: secure the best farms' loyalty and
make delivery routes denser. Signposts: Continental job ads for "local
sourcing" roles; fuel futures.

**Resistance.** FieldTable's operations team resists moving from daily to
twice-daily pickups. Reasons: longer days, doubt that restaurants will pay
for it. Response: a four-week trial with volunteers and a shift bonus
(unfreeze), weekly results shared (short-term wins), and a new schedule
written into the routine once it works (refreeze).

## Apply it to your organization

1. Name the biggest change your industry faces and classify it: gradual,
   shock, disruption or uncertainty.
2. If a newcomer worries you, run the four disruption tests on it and write
   one sentence of evidence for each.
3. Choose your two biggest uncertainties, cross them, and name the four
   scenarios. What one strategy would hold up in all four? What signpost
   would tell you which is arriving?
4. Think of a change your organization tried that stalled. Which of Kotter's
   steps was missing?

## Answers

1. It starts with overlooked customers; it's worse on the incumbent's key
   measures but better on others; incumbents rationally decline to respond;
   it improves fast enough to reach the mainstream.
2. That only a minority of the examples met all the theory's conditions.
3. Four. Because when the future is uncertain, a single forecast hides the
   range of outcomes; scenarios let you choose strategies that hold up in
   more than one.
4. An early, observable sign of which scenario is unfolding.
5. Unfreezing, changing, refreezing.
6. Any three of: establish urgency; form a guiding coalition; create a
   vision; communicate it; empower others to act; create short-term wins;
   consolidate gains; anchor changes in the culture.
7. For example: longer days (response: pay a shift bonus and ask for
   volunteers); doubt it will work (response: a short trial with results
   shared).
8. (a) A shock; (b) gradual change; (c) uncertainty.
9. Build a separate unit: the threat starts with non-customers and a
   different business model, which the core's processes and priorities would
   reject, as Christensen advised for incumbents facing disruption.
