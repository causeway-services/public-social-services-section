## Network effects

A **network effect** exists when a product becomes more valuable to each
user as more people use it. Michael Katz and Carl Shapiro analyzed how such
"network externalities" shape competition and compatibility [@katz-shapiro1985].
There are two kinds:

- **Same-side (direct):** more users on one side make it better for that
  side, as more people on a messaging app make it more useful to each.
- **Cross-side (indirect):** more users on one side make it better for the
  other side, as more buyers attract sellers and more sellers attract buyers.

## Two-sided platforms

A **platform** creates value by enabling interactions between groups, rather
than by making a product itself. A **two-sided market** has two distinct
groups linked by cross-side effects: buyers and sellers, riders and drivers,
diners and restaurants. Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole showed that in
such markets the *structure* of prices matters, not only their level: how the
total charge is split between the sides decides whether both come
[@rochet-tirole2003].

The practical rule, set out by Thomas Eisenmann, Geoffrey Parker and Marshall
Van Alstyne: often subsidize the side whose presence the other side values
most and which is most sensitive to price, and charge the side that values
access more and is less sensitive [@eisenmann2006]. The subsidized side may
pay nothing or even be paid.

## Evaluating a platform's potential

Ask:

- **Are there real cross-side effects?** Does each side value the other's
  presence?
- **Can it overcome the chicken-and-egg problem?** Neither side comes without
  the other.
- **Will users multi-home?** If users can easily use more than one platform at
  once, one platform is less likely to take the whole market.
- **Will the market tip?** Strong network effects, high costs of
  multi-homing and little demand for special features push a market toward
  one winner (a **winner-take-all** market); the opposite conditions allow more than one platform to survive
  [@eisenmann2006].
- **Can it capture value?** A platform that creates interactions but can't
  charge for them has no business.

## Starting, growing and defending a platform

Geoffrey Parker, Marshall Van Alstyne and Sangeet Paul Choudary describe
platform strategy as designing the core interaction, then attracting,
facilitating and matching participants [@parker2016].

- **Start:** solve the chicken-and-egg problem. Common tactics: subsidize one
  side; bring one side yourself (seed supply); offer single-side value (a
  tool one side wants even before the other side arrives); start in a narrow
  market where density is achievable.
- **Grow:** raise the value of each interaction (better matching, trust,
  reviews, payments); reduce friction for new participants; add related
  interactions.
- **Defend:** strengthen network effects and quality; make it worthwhile to
  stay (data and history that help users); watch for **envelopment**, a
  larger platform bundling your function into its own [@eisenmann2006];
  govern the platform so participants trust it.

## Selling through someone else's platform

Many businesses don't run a platform; they sell through one: an app store, a
marketplace, a delivery app. The benefits are reach and ready customers. The
risks:

- **Dependence:** the platform sets fees and rules and can change them.
- **Data:** the platform, not you, may own the customer relationship and its
  data.
- **Disintermediation or competition:** the platform may copy successful
  products or steer customers to its own.

A sound strategy uses the platform for reach while building direct
relationships where allowed; spreads risk across more than one channel; and
tracks what the platform costs against what it brings.

## Worked example: FieldTable as a platform

**Sides and effects.** Restaurants value FieldTable for the number and range
of farms; farms value it for the number of restaurants. Cross-side effects
are strong. Same-side effects are small: more restaurants compete for scarce
items, which can be negative.

**Which side to subsidize?** Farms are fragmented, cautious about new
channels and sensitive to fees; restaurants value the farm range and pay for
convenience. So FieldTable charges farms nothing to list and picks up their
produce free; restaurants pay a fee on each order.

**Chicken and egg.** At launch FieldTable signed 20 farms first, gave them a
free harvest-planning tool (single-side value), and started in one
neighborhood of dense restaurants, so each delivery route was full.

**Will the market tip?** Restaurants multi-home: they keep buying from
Continental. Differentiation (named farms, seasonal items) matters. So the
market won't tip to one winner, and FieldTable doesn't need to be the only
channel, only a needed one.

**Defense.** Chapter 5's exclusive agreements with the best farms; a
restaurant-facing record of every farm's products and schedule that helps
chefs plan menus; and watchfulness for envelopment by a large food-delivery
app adding wholesale.

**Selling through another platform.** Some farms ask to also list on a
national online marketplace. FieldTable doesn't stop them (that would harm
trust) and focuses on what the national platform can't offer: next-day
local delivery and planning with each farm.

## Apply it to your organization

1. Does your organization run a platform, sell through one, or both? Name
   them.
2. If you run one: which side do you subsidize and which do you charge? Do
   your prices follow Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne's rule?

   would you do if its fees doubled?
4. Will your market tip? Check network strength, multi-homing and demand for
   special features.

## Answers

1. Same-side: more users of a messaging app make it more useful to each
   user. Cross-side: more sellers on a marketplace make it more valuable to
   buyers.
2. That the split of charges between the two sides, not only the total
   level, determines whether both sides join.
3. The side whose presence the other side values most and which is most
   sensitive to price.
4. Any three of: subsidize one side; seed one side yourself; offer
   single-side value; start in a narrow, dense market.
5. Strong network effects, high costs of using more than one platform, and
   little demand for special features.
6. A larger platform bundling your platform's function into its own offer,
   often capturing your users.
7. Any two of: dependence on the store's fees and rules; not owning the
   customer relationship or data; the store promoting competitors. Reduce by
   selling on more than one channel or building a direct relationship with
   players, such as a mailing list.
