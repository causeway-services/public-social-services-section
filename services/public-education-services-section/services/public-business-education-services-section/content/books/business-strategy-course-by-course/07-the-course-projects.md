# The course projects: applying the tools to your organization

*A guide to the work each course asks you to produce. Before you start: the
chapter for the course you're taking.*

## What you will be able to do

- Say what each course's published takeaways ask you to be able to produce.
- Check your own course work against a list of what makes each kind of
  analysis strong.
- Avoid the mistakes that most often weaken a strategy analysis.

## What the courses ask for

Each of the certificate's courses is built around applying its frameworks to
a real organization, usually your own, and eCornell's course pages list what
you'll be able to do by the end [@ecornell-bs-2026]. The pages don't publish
the projects' instructions or grading, and this chapter doesn't describe them.
It gives, for each course, what a strong piece of work produces, keyed to the
course's published takeaways and to the chapter that teaches the tools. The
"Apply it to your organization" section at the end of each chapter is a
first draft of the same work.

## A checklist for each course

**LSM311, Value Creation and Profitability (Chapter 1).** A strong analysis:

- names a specific target customer group and what raises its willingness to
  pay, with evidence;
- estimates value created and how it's divided;
- names the resources the business's value depends on and tests them for
  being valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable;
- rates each of the five forces with a fact for each rating, and picks the
  one squeezing profit most;
- proposes responses to the main threats, including one with a complementor.

**LSM312, Change, Disruption, and Growth (Chapter 2).** A strong analysis:

- classifies the main changes (gradual, shock, disruption, uncertainty);
- tests any claimed disruption against the four conditions;
- builds a scenario grid from two uncertainties, with a strategy that holds
  across it and signposts to watch;
- names the likely internal resistance, its reasons, and a plan for each.

**LSM313, Synergies, Mergers, and Alliances (Chapter 3).** A strong analysis:

- states each potential synergy as a number;
- compares building, borrowing and buying, and applies the ally-or-acquire
  tests;
- scores a candidate partner's fit;
- sets owners, dates and tracking for realization, and names the likely
  failure points.

**LSM314, Business Relationships (Chapter 4).** A strong analysis:

- identifies the key relationships by the test of what losing or improving
  each would mean;
- scores each one's health on the six dimensions;
- sets actions for the weakest scores and a schedule for reviewing them
  together;
- anticipates the common challenges for each.

**LSM315, Game Theory and Business Strategy (Chapter 5).** A strong analysis:

- names the players with their motivations, capabilities and beliefs;
- maps at least one decision as a payoff table or game tree, and solves it;
- tests which threats and promises are credible;
- proposes a way to change the game using PARTS.

**LSM316, Digital Platform Strategy (Chapter 6).** A strong analysis:

- identifies the sides and the network effects between them;
- evaluates the platform's potential (cross-side effects, chicken and egg,
  multi-homing, tipping, value capture);
- plans how to start, grow and defend it, or how to sell through someone
  else's platform and limit the risks.

## Common mistakes

- **Frameworks without evidence.** A five-forces rating with no facts, or a
  SWOT of adjectives, shows you know the framework, not the business.
- **Analysis without a choice.** Each course's takeaways end in selecting,
  planning or devising; a strong piece of work recommends something.
- **Treating every force or relationship as equally important.** Rank them.
- **Forgetting the other players.** A plan that assumes rivals, partners and
  staff will stand still is incomplete (Chapter 5).

## Worked example: reviewing a draft

A learner's first draft for LSM311 rates rivalry "high" and substitutes
"medium" with no evidence, and ends with "we should differentiate." Against
the checklist: the ratings need facts ("three competitors cut prices 10
percent this year"); the target customers and their willingness to pay are
missing; and "differentiate" needs saying on what, for whom, with which
resources. The revision names the customer group, estimates value created per
order, ties the differentiation to the one resource rivals can't copy, and
names a complementor to partner with.

## Check yourself

1. Why does each checklist end with a choice, plan or proposal rather than an
   analysis alone?
2. What's missing from a five-forces rating of "high, high, medium, low,
   medium"?
3. Name two things a strong LSM313 analysis states about each synergy.
4. How do the "Apply it to your organization" sections relate to the course
   projects?
5. What's wrong with a plan that assumes a rival won't respond?

## Answers

1. Because each course's published takeaways end in selecting, planning or
   devising strategy; analysis is the means, a decision is the result.
2. The facts behind each rating, and which force squeezes profit most.
3. Any two of: its size as a number; an owner; a date; how it will be tracked;
   the likely failure points.
4. They're first drafts of the same work: the exercises apply each chapter's
   tools to your organization, which is what the courses ask.
5. Rivals respond to moves that affect them; ignoring their likely response
   (Chapter 5) can make a plan fail, as a price cut can start a price war.
