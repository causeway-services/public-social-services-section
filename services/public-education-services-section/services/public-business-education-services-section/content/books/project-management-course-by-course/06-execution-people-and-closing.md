# Execution II: leading the team, communicating and closing

## What you will be able to do

- Describe what makes a team effective, and recognize the stages a new team
  goes through.
- Build an inclusive team environment and handle the ethical questions a
  project manager meets.
- Influence people you don't manage, using the sources of power you have.
- Plan and run a meeting that ends in decisions and owned actions, and write
  a status report.
- Close a project: get acceptance, hand over, capture lessons and report the
  outcome.

## What makes a team effective

A team is effective when it reaches its goals and its members would work
together again. Research on teams keeps finding the same conditions: a clear,
shared goal; clear roles; members who can depend on one another; and the
willingness to speak up.

That last condition has a name. Amy Edmondson defined **psychological
safety** as a team's shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks
there: to ask a question, admit a mistake, or disagree. In her study of work
teams, psychological safety went with more learning behavior, such as asking
for feedback and discussing errors, and learning went with better
performance [@edmondson1999]. A project manager builds it by asking for
disagreement, responding to bad news with thanks rather than blame, and
admitting their own mistakes first.

## How teams develop

In 1965 Bruce Tuckman reviewed studies of small groups and proposed that
groups pass through four stages [@tuckman1965]:

1. **Forming:** members are polite and unsure; they look to the leader for
   direction.
2. **Storming:** disagreements surface over goals, roles and ways of working.
3. **Norming:** the group settles its rules and roles and starts to cohere.
4. **Performing:** the group works well together with little direction.

In 1977 he and Mary Ann Jensen added a fifth, **adjourning**, for the end of a
group's life [@tuckman-jensen1977]. The stages aren't a timetable, and teams
can slide back when members change. Their use is diagnostic: storming isn't a
sign the team is failing, and a project manager adjusts their style to the
stage, giving more direction while forming and more room while performing.

## Inclusion and ethics

An **inclusive** team is one where every member can contribute fully,
whatever their background, role or working style. In practice that means
meeting times that work across time zones and caregiving hours; documents and
meetings that are accessible, including captions and screen-reader-friendly
files; making sure quieter people get the floor; and noticing whose ideas get
credit.

Ethical questions come up in execution as well as procurement. The PMI code's
four values (responsibility, respect, fairness, honesty) are a usable test
[@pmi-code-of-ethics]. Report status honestly, even when it's bad. Don't
promise what the team can't deliver. Give credit accurately. Raise a
concern about safety or law with someone who can act on it.

## Influence without authority

Project managers usually lead people who don't report to them. So they lead
by **influence**. John French and Bertram Raven identified five bases of
social power in 1959 [@french-raven1959]:

- **Legitimate:** from a formal position ("I'm the manager").
- **Reward:** from the ability to give something people value.
- **Coercive:** from the ability to impose penalties.
- **Expert:** from knowledge or skill others need.
- **Referent:** from being liked, respected or identified with.

A project manager typically has little legitimate, reward or coercive power
over a borrowed team. They rely on expert power (knowing the plan and the
problems best) and referent power (being someone people want to help). Both
are earned: by being prepared, keeping promises, crediting others, and
understanding what each person needs from the project. Influence starts with
asking what the other person cares about, then showing how the request serves
it.

**Emotional intelligence**, the ability to recognize and manage your own
emotions and read and respond to others', which Daniel Goleman popularized
[@goleman1995], is the everyday skill underneath influence. Before a hard
conversation, notice your own state, and consider the other person's.

## Meetings and status reports

Meetings cost everyone's time at once, so each one needs a reason. Before
calling one, answer: what's it for (to decide, to plan, to share, to solve a
problem), and who has to be there for that? Then:

- Send an **agenda** ahead, with the purpose and topics.
- Invite only the people the purpose needs.
- Keep to time; park off-topic items for later.
- Record **decisions** and **action items**, each with one owner and a due
  date, and send the notes within a day.

Common project meetings include the **kickoff** (the team's first meeting,
to align on goals, roles and ways of working), regular **status** meetings,
short daily **stand-ups** (Chapter 8), **retrospectives** (Chapter 5) and
**stakeholder reviews**. Judge a meeting afterward by one question: did it
produce the decisions and actions it was called for?

A **status report** gives stakeholders the project's state in a form they can
read in two minutes: overall status (on track, at risk, off track) with a
one-line reason; progress since the last report; what's next; the top risks
and issues; and any decisions needed from the reader. Put the most
important thing first.

## Closing the project

A project is finished when its success criteria are met and the sponsor
formally **accepts** its deliverables, or when the sponsor decides to stop it.
Either way it must be closed deliberately. Closing matters because an
unclosed project keeps consuming people, money and attention, and because its
lessons are lost unless someone records them.

The closing steps:

1. Get **formal acceptance** of the deliverables, in writing.
2. **Hand over** to operations: the people who'll run the result get the
   documents, training and contacts they need.
3. **Close contracts:** confirm vendors' work is accepted and paid.
4. **Release the team** back to their regular work, with thanks and, where it
   helps them, feedback for their managers.
5. Hold a final **retrospective** and record **lessons learned**.
6. Write a **closeout report**: what was delivered, against the success
   criteria, the budget and the schedule, and what was learned. An **impact
   report** goes further and shows what the result achieved for the people it
   served.
7. **Archive** the project documents in one place.
8. **Celebrate.** Recognizing the team's work is part of closing, not an
   extra.

## Worked example: leading and closing Eastside

**Storming.** In week 2 the volunteer coordinator and the programs manager
disagree over who schedules volunteers. The programs manager recognizes
storming, not failure. They meet, agree that the coordinator owns shift
schedules and the programs manager owns task deadlines, and add it to the
RACI chart.

**Influence.** The school custodian doesn't work for the food bank and has
no reason to give up Saturday mornings. The programs manager asks what the
custodian's concerns are: floors damaged by carts, and being locked out of
their own supply room. The team buys rubber-wheeled carts and agrees to leave
the supply room alone. The custodian becomes the pantry's advocate with the
principal. That's referent power, earned by solving someone else's problem.

**A status report (week 5).**

> **Status: at risk.** Recruitment is one day behind on the critical path.
> **Done:** agreement signed; shelving installed; 18 volunteers trained.
> **Next:** final 4 volunteers; district inspection on day 21.
> **Risks:** inspection date not yet confirmed.
> **Decision needed from the director:** approve $300 from contingency for
> lobby queue signs.

**Closing.** The pantry serves 64, 71, 58 and 69 households on its first four
Saturdays. One Saturday misses the 60-household criterion, and the closeout
report says so, with the cause (a snowstorm). The director signs acceptance.
The weekend operations lead receives the volunteer roster, the cold-storage
log, the building-use agreement and the principal's contact. The shelving
contract is closed. The final retrospective records three lessons: sign the
site agreement first; recruit 25 percent more volunteers than slots; plan an
indoor waiting area. The impact report tells the board how many families
were served and what they said. The team eats pizza in the gym.

## Check yourself

1. Define psychological safety in one sentence.
2. Two team members argue about how to split the work, two weeks in. Which
   Tuckman stage is this, and what should the project manager do?
3. Which two bases of power does a project manager leading a borrowed team
   usually rely on, and why those?
4. What four things should come out of a meeting's notes?
5. List five items a status report should contain.
6. Name six of the eight closing steps.
7. What's the difference between a closeout report and an impact report?
8. In a meeting, a quiet volunteer's idea is credited to someone else. Which
   of the PMI code's values does this touch, and what should the project
   manager do?

## Answers

1. A team's shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks there,
   such as asking questions, admitting mistakes or disagreeing.
2. Storming. Treat it as normal: bring the disagreement into the open, help
   the team settle roles and working rules, and record them (moving toward
   norming).
3. Expert and referent power, because they usually lack the legitimate,
   reward and coercive power a line manager has over the same people.
4. Decisions, action items, an owner for each action, and a due date for
   each.
5. Overall status with a reason; progress since the last report; what's
   next; top risks and issues; decisions needed from the reader.
6. Any six of: formal acceptance; handover to operations; close contracts;
   release the team; final retrospective and lessons learned; closeout
   report; archive documents; celebrate.
7. A closeout report measures the project against its own criteria (scope,
   schedule, budget, success criteria) and records lessons. An impact report
   shows what the result achieved for the people it was meant to serve.
8. Fairness and honesty (giving credit accurately), and respect. Correct the
   record in the meeting or its notes, credit the volunteer, and make room
   for quieter people to speak first next time.
