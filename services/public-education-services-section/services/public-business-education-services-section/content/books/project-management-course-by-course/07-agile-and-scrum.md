## Where Agile came from

By the 1990s, many software teams had found that planning a whole product up
front didn't work when customers couldn't know what they wanted until they
saw something working. Teams tried lighter methods with names like Extreme
Programming, Scrum and Crystal. In February 2001, seventeen people who had
developed those methods met in Snowbird, Utah, and wrote the **Manifesto for
Agile Software Development** [@agile-manifesto-2001]. It states four values:

> Individuals and interactions over processes and tools\
> Working software over comprehensive documentation\
> Customer collaboration over contract negotiation\
> Responding to change over following a plan

The manifesto adds that "while there is value in the items on the right, we
value the items on the left more." That sentence matters: Agile doesn't throw
out plans, documents or contracts. It ranks them below the things they exist
to serve.

The manifesto also lists twelve principles. Among them: satisfy the customer
through early and continuous delivery; welcome changing requirements, even
late; deliver working software frequently, in weeks rather than months;
business people and developers work together daily; working software is the
primary measure of progress; and at regular intervals the team reflects on
how to become more effective, then adjusts. Read the full list on the
manifesto's site; it's short.

## Agile and Waterfall compared

| | Waterfall (predictive) | Agile (adaptive) |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Whole project up front | Detailed for the next cycle; rough beyond |
| Requirements | Fixed early; change is controlled | Expected to change; revisited each cycle |
| Delivery | Once, at the end | In small increments, often |
| Customer involvement | At the start and end | Throughout |
| Progress measured by | Tasks done against plan | Working results delivered |
| Fits | Stable, well-understood work; costly late change | Uncertain work; cheap change |

Neither is better in general. The Eastside pantry's shelving would be absurd
to deliver "iteratively"; its sign-up process would be foolish to fix before
any family has tried it. Blending, a hybrid, is common: a predictive plan for
the parts that must be built once, and Agile cycles for the parts that must be
learned. Organizations also combine Agile frameworks, such as Scrum's roles
and events with Kanban's board and work-in-progress limits, sometimes called
"Scrumban."

## Scrum's theory: three pillars

**Scrum** is the most widely used Agile framework. It was developed by Ken
Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, who maintain its definition, *The Scrum Guide*.
This book follows the November 2020 edition [@scrum-guide-2020].

Scrum rests on **empiricism**, the idea that knowledge comes from experience
and decisions should be based on what's observed, and on lean thinking,
which cuts waste and focuses on essentials. Empiricism in Scrum stands on
three pillars:

- **Transparency:** the work and the process are visible to those doing the
  work and those receiving it. You can't inspect what you can't see.
- **Inspection:** the team frequently checks its product and its progress
  toward agreed goals, to detect problems.
- **Adaptation:** when inspection shows something is off, the team adjusts
  as soon as possible.

Each pillar depends on the one before: without transparency, inspection
misleads; without inspection, adaptation is guesswork.

## Scrum's five values

The guide names five values that make the pillars work in practice:
**commitment** (to the team's goals and to supporting each other),
**focus** (on the sprint's work), **openness** (about the work and its
problems), **respect** (for one another as capable, independent people), and
**courage** (to do the right thing and work on hard problems). The link to the
pillars is direct. Transparency needs openness and courage: someone has to
say "this isn't working." Adaptation needs commitment and focus.

## The Scrum Team and its accountabilities

A **Scrum Team** is small, typically ten people or fewer, and
cross-functional: it has every skill needed to create a valuable result each
sprint. It's self-managing: it decides internally who does what, when and
how. The 2020 guide defines three **accountabilities** within it:

- **Product Owner.** Accountable for maximizing the value of the product. They
  manage the **Product Backlog**: stating the product goal, writing and
  ordering the backlog items, and making sure it's visible and understood. The
  Product Owner is one person, not a committee, though they may represent many
  stakeholders. An effective one knows the customers and the business,
  decides, and says no to low-value work.
- **Scrum Master.** Accountable for the team's effectiveness and for
  establishing Scrum as the guide defines it. They coach the team in
  self-management, help remove impediments, make sure events happen and stay
  productive, and help the organization around the team understand Scrum. An
  effective one serves and teaches rather than directs.
- **Developers.** The people who create each increment of the product. They
  plan the sprint, hold themselves to the **Definition of Done**, and adapt
  their plan each day. "Developers" includes anyone doing the work, not only
  programmers.

**A note on terms.** Earlier editions of *The Scrum Guide* called the
Developers the "Development Team," a team within the Scrum Team. The 2020
edition dropped that to stress one team with one goal. Course materials and
job postings use both terms; they mean the same people.

## Worked example: an Agile corner of the Eastside project

The pantry's family sign-up process is uncertain: nobody knows whether
families will prefer a paper form sent home with students, a phone line, or
signing up at the door. So the programs manager runs that piece of work as a
small Scrum team:

- **Product Owner:** the food bank's family services lead, who knows the
  families and can decide what matters most to them.
- **Scrum Master:** the programs manager, who coaches the team in the
  approach and clears obstacles, such as getting the school's permission to
  send forms home.
- **Developers:** two volunteers and a school family liaison, who design,
  try and improve the process.

Transparency: every piece of work is on a board in the shared folder, visible
to the director. Inspection: each Saturday, the team looks at how sign-up
went: how many families, how long the line, what families said. Adaptation:
the next week's version changes accordingly. When a volunteer admits that the
phone line went unanswered for an hour, that's openness and courage, and it
leads to a fix.

## Answers

1. Individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software
   over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract
   negotiation; responding to change over following a plan.
2. No. It says that while there's value in the items on the right, it values
   the items on the left more.
3. Transparency, inspection, adaptation. If the work and progress aren't
   visible and accurate, inspecting them produces wrong conclusions, and any
   adaptation based on them goes wrong too.
4. Commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage.

6. The Scrum Team is self-managing: the Developers decide who does what. The

7. Developers.
8. Predictive (Waterfall) for the renovation; Agile for the app. Together, a
   hybrid project.
