# Foundations: what a project is and what its manager does

*Companion to Course 1, Foundations of Project Management. No prerequisites.*

## What you will be able to do

- Tell a project apart from ongoing operations, and say why the difference
  matters for how the work is run.
- Describe what a project manager does, and how that role adds value without
  doing all of the work.
- Name the phases of a project's life cycle and what each one produces.
- Compare predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches, and choose one for a
  given project with a reason.
- Explain how an organization's structure and culture change a project
  manager's job, and why change management is part of it.

## Projects and operations

Start with two jobs at the same food bank. Every Tuesday, volunteers unload a
truck, sort donations and stock shelves. Nobody plans that from scratch each
week; it follows a routine and it doesn't end. Separately, the food bank
decides to open the Eastside Weekend Pantry. That work has a start, a finish
and a result that didn't exist before.

The first job is **operations**: ongoing, repeated work that keeps an
organization running. The second is a **project**: a temporary effort to
produce a unique result. Two words in that definition do the work.
*Temporary* means it has a defined beginning and end, even when the end
slips. *Unique* means the result is new in some way, so the team can't simply
repeat last week.

The difference matters because the tools differ. Operations are improved by
measuring a steady process and trimming waste from it. A project has no
steady process yet. It needs a goal, a plan, a team assembled for it, and a
point at which someone says it's done and hands the result over to
operations. When the Eastside pantry opens, running it every weekend becomes
operations.

Projects also nest into larger groupings. A **program** is a set of related
projects managed together because they share a goal, such as a food bank's
three new pantry sites. A **portfolio** is everything an organization chooses
to invest in, grouped to serve its strategy, which can hold programs,
projects and operations side by side.

## What a project manager does

A project manager is the person responsible for getting a project to its
goal. That doesn't mean doing all of the work. At Eastside, the volunteer
coordinator recruits volunteers and the school's custodian opens the gym. The
project manager makes sure the work is defined, assigned, sequenced,
resourced and finished, and that the people who need to know what's happening
do know.

In practice the job comes down to a handful of recurring tasks:

- **Planning and organizing:** turning a goal into tasks, estimates, a
  schedule and a budget.
- **Managing tasks:** tracking what's done and what's late, and acting when
  something slips.
- **Budgeting and controlling cost:** knowing what has been spent and what
  remains.
- **Communicating:** keeping the sponsor, the team and everyone else affected
  informed, in the form each needs.
- **Removing obstacles:** finding what's blocking the team and clearing it,
  often by asking someone with authority to decide.

The value a project manager adds is fewer surprises. Problems still happen,
but they're seen earlier, while they're cheaper to fix, and decisions get made
by the people who should make them. The role often carries responsibility
without much formal authority: the volunteers don't report to the project
manager. So the skills that matter most are interpersonal ones: communicating
well, negotiating, building trust, and influencing people whose managers
are someone else. Chapter 6 comes back to influence.

Many people come to project management from other work. A teacher plans a
semester; a restaurant manager coordinates suppliers, staff and a deadline
every night. Those are transferable skills, and the course asks you to name
yours.

## The project life cycle

Every project passes through the same broad phases, whatever the approach:

1. **Initiate.** Decide whether the project is worth doing, define its goal
   and scope, identify its stakeholders, and get approval. The main output is
   a **project charter**, a short document that authorizes the project.
2. **Plan.** Break the work into tasks, estimate them, schedule them, budget
   them, and plan for risk, quality and communication.
3. **Execute.** Do the work, while tracking progress against the plan and
   managing changes, problems and people.
4. **Close.** Confirm the result is accepted, hand it over, release the team,
   record what was learned, and formally end the project.

Monitoring isn't a separate phase; it runs through planning and execution.
Some sources, including PMI, name five process groups by splitting
"monitoring and controlling" out as its own.

Each phase exists for a reason, and skipping one costs you later. A project
that skips initiation often discovers halfway through that two sponsors
wanted different things. A project that skips closing leaves the result
half-handed-over and loses its lessons.

## Approaches: predictive, adaptive and hybrid

There are two broad ways to run the phases.

**Predictive** approaches, often called **Waterfall**, plan the whole project
up front and then execute the plan in sequence: requirements, then design,
then building, then testing, then delivery. The name comes from the
cascading diagram in Winston Royce's 1970 paper on large software projects.
Royce drew the sequential model and then warned that, done that way, it "is
risky and invites failure," recommending feedback loops and an early
prototype instead [@royce1970]. His paper is remembered as the origin of
Waterfall, but it argued against the pure form. Predictive approaches suit
work whose requirements are known and stable, and where changing course late
is expensive, such as construction.

**Adaptive** approaches, usually called **Agile**, deliver the result in
short cycles and plan in detail only a little ahead. Each cycle produces
something usable, gets feedback on it, and adjusts the next cycle.
**Scrum** and **Kanban** are the two most common Agile frameworks; Chapters 7
and 8 cover them. Adaptive approaches suit work whose requirements are
uncertain or likely to change, such as a new software product.

Two other families come up in the course. **Lean** thinking focuses on
removing waste and improving flow. **Six Sigma** focuses on reducing
variation and defects, using a five-step cycle called DMAIC: Define, Measure,
Analyze, Improve, Control. Organizations often combine them as Lean Six
Sigma for improving existing processes.

Real projects often mix approaches. That's a **hybrid**. Choosing among them
comes down to three questions:

- How well do we know what the result must be?
- How costly is it to change direction late?
- How often can the people who'll use the result give feedback?

## Structure, culture and change

A project lives inside an organization, and the organization's shape changes
the project manager's job.

In a **functional** (or classic) structure, people report up a single chain
by specialty: operations staff to an operations director, and so on. A
project manager borrows people from those departments and has little
authority over them. In a **matrix** structure, people report to a
functional manager and also to a project manager; the balance of power
between the two varies. In a **projectized** structure, teams are organized
around projects and the project manager has most of the authority. Many
organizations also have a **project management office (PMO)**, a group that
sets standards, templates and training for projects, and sometimes manages
them.

**Culture** is the unwritten set of values and habits that decides how things
really get done: how decisions are made, how bad news travels, how formal
meetings are. A project manager who reads the culture well knows whom to
consult before a meeting and which documents people will actually read.

Finally, most projects change how someone works. The Eastside pantry changes
how the school's custodian spends Saturdays and how families get food.
**Change management** is the work of helping the people affected understand,
accept and adopt the change. It starts early: involve the affected people in
planning, explain the reason for the change, and support them through it. A
project that delivers its result on time but that nobody adopts has failed.

## Worked example: framing the Eastside pantry

Take the pantry through the chapter's questions.

**Is it a project?** Yes. It has a start (the food bank's decision), an end
(the first open Saturday, handed to the weekend operations team) and a unique
result (a pantry that doesn't exist yet). Running it every weekend
afterward is operations.

**Who's the project manager, and what will they do?** The food bank's
programs manager. They won't stock shelves. They'll turn "open a pantry" into
tasks, get the principal's agreement in writing, schedule shelving delivery
around the volunteers' training, track the $12,000, and keep the food bank's
director, the school and the volunteers informed.

**Which approach?** Split the work by how certain it is:

| Part of the work | How certain? | Cost of late change | Approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site agreement, shelving, safety inspection | Known | High: shelving is ordered and installed once | Predictive |
| How families sign up and pick up food | Uncertain | Low: a form can change weekly | Adaptive |

So a hybrid: plan the physical setup up front, and run the family
experience in short cycles, trying a sign-up process on the first two
Saturdays and adjusting it from families' feedback.

**Structure and culture.** The food bank is functional: volunteers report to
the volunteer coordinator, not to the programs manager. So the project
manager will need to agree on volunteer time with the coordinator rather than
assign it. The school is a second organization with its own culture:
decisions go through the principal, and the district's facilities office has
rules about building use. Both are stakeholders, which is where Chapter 2
begins.

## Key terms

- **Project:** a temporary effort to create a unique result.
- **Operations:** ongoing, repeated work that sustains an organization.
- **Program:** related projects managed together toward a shared goal.
- **Portfolio:** the collection of programs, projects and operations an
  organization invests in to serve its strategy.
- **Project life cycle:** initiate, plan, execute, close.
- **Predictive (Waterfall):** plan up front, then execute in sequence.
- **Adaptive (Agile):** deliver in short cycles, planning a little ahead.
- **Hybrid:** a mix of predictive and adaptive approaches in one project.
- **Functional, matrix, projectized:** organizational structures that give a
  project manager less or more authority.
- **PMO:** a project management office.
- **Change management:** helping the people a project affects adopt what it
  delivers.

## Check yourself

Answer from memory before you look.

1. A city's parks department mows its lawns every week and is building a new
   skate park this year. Which is a project and which is operations, and why?
2. What's the difference between a program and a portfolio?
3. Name the four life cycle phases and the main thing initiation produces.
4. According to his own 1970 paper, did Winston Royce recommend the strict
   sequential model? What did he recommend instead?
5. A team is building an app for a client who isn't sure what features they
   want and can review work every two weeks. Predictive or adaptive? Give two
   reasons.
6. In which structure does a project manager usually have the least
   authority over team members: functional, matrix or projectized?
7. The Eastside pantry opens on time, but families keep going to the weekday
   site because nobody told them about the new one. Which part of the project
   manager's job failed?

## Answers

1. Mowing is operations: it's ongoing and repeats unchanged. The skate park
   is a project: it's temporary and produces a unique result.
2. A program groups related projects that share a goal. A portfolio is
   everything the organization invests in, which can include programs,
   separate projects and operations, grouped to serve its strategy.
3. Initiate, plan, execute, close. Initiation produces the project charter.
4. No. He described it, called it "risky" and said it "invites failure," and
   recommended iteration with feedback and an early prototype.
5. Adaptive. The requirements are uncertain, and the client can give feedback
   every two weeks. A third reason: changing an app's features between
   cycles is cheap.
6. Functional. Team members report to their department heads, and the
   project manager borrows them.
7. Change management, together with communication: the people affected by
   the change weren't helped to adopt it.
