## The budget

A **budget** is the estimated cost of the project, broken into parts and
spread over time. Build it from the work breakdown structure: estimate the
cost of each work package, using the same analogous, parametric, bottom-up or
three-point techniques as for time, and add them up.

Costs come in kinds worth separating:

- **Direct costs** belong to this project alone: shelving for the pantry.
  **Indirect costs** are shared: a share of the food bank's insurance or
  office rent.
- **Fixed costs** don't change with the amount of work: a permit fee.
  **Variable costs** do: printing, per flyer.
- **Labor** is often the largest cost, even when the labor is staff time the
  project doesn't pay for directly.

Then add reserves. A **contingency reserve** covers identified risks: if the
risk register says the delivery might need a rush fee, the contingency holds
money for it. A **management reserve** covers what nobody foresaw; it's held
by the sponsor, not the project manager, and released only with approval. The
approved budget without the management reserve is the **cost baseline**, the
line actual spending is measured against.

The budget workflow runs in four steps: estimate, set the baseline, track
actual spending against it, and re-forecast when reality departs from the
plan. Chapter 5 shows how to measure that departure.

## Procurement

**Procurement** is getting goods or services from outside the project team.
It starts with a **make-or-buy** decision: can the team do it, or should it
pay someone? Buy when outsiders do it better, cheaper or faster, or when the
team lacks the skill or time.

A procurement then follows a sequence:

1. **Plan:** decide what to buy and write a **statement of work (SOW)**
   describing it precisely enough for a vendor to price.
2. **Solicit:** ask vendors to respond. A **request for information (RFI)**
   gathers options; a **request for quote (RFQ)** asks for prices on a
   defined item; a **request for proposal (RFP)** asks vendors to propose how
   they would meet a need.
3. **Select** a vendor against criteria written before the responses arrive.
4. **Contract.** A **fixed-price** contract sets one price and puts cost risk
   on the vendor. A **cost-reimbursable** contract pays the vendor's actual
   costs plus a fee and puts the risk on the buyer. **Time and materials**
   pays an hourly rate plus materials, and suits small or unclear work.
5. **Manage** the vendor's performance, and **close** the contract when the
   work is accepted and paid.

Procurement is where money leaves the organization, so it's where ethics are
tested. PMI's code of ethics rests on four values: responsibility, respect,
fairness and honesty [@pmi-code-of-ethics]. In procurement they come down to
a few rules. Disclose any conflict of interest, such as a relative who owns a
bidding company, and step out of the decision. Don't accept gifts that could
sway a choice. Give every bidder the same information and deadline. Keep
bidders' prices confidential.

## Risk

A **risk** is an uncertain event that, if it happens, affects the project.
It hasn't happened yet. An **issue** is a problem that has happened and needs
handling now. Managing risk is how a project turns would-be issues into
planned responses.

Risk management runs in five steps:

1. **Identify.** Brainstorm with the team; review the assumptions in the
   charter, since every assumption is a risk that it's wrong; check lessons
   from past projects; look at every dependency on someone outside the team.
2. **Analyze.** Score each risk's **probability** and **impact**, often on a
   1 to 5 scale, and multiply them. The product ranks the risks.
3. **Respond.** For a threat, choose one of four responses:
   - **Avoid:** change the plan so the risk can't happen.
   - **Mitigate:** lower its probability or impact.
   - **Transfer:** shift its impact to someone else, as with insurance or a
     warranty.
   - **Accept:** do nothing now, and perhaps set aside contingency.

   PMI adds a fifth response, **escalate**, for a risk outside the
   project's authority: pass it to the person or group who owns it.

   Risks can also be good. For an **opportunity**, the responses are exploit
   (make sure it happens), enhance (raise its chance), share (partner with
   someone better placed to capture it), accept, and, again, escalate.
4. **Assign an owner.** Each risk gets one person who watches it and acts on
   its response. Record a **trigger**, the early sign that the risk is
   happening.
5. **Monitor** the risks at every status review, and add new ones as they
   appear.

The **risk register** is the table that holds all of this: each risk, its
scores, its response, its owner and its trigger. The **risk management
plan** is the short document that says how the project will do the five
steps: the scales, how often risks are reviewed, and who's involved.

## Communication and documentation

Most project problems are communication problems: someone didn't know
something in time. A **communication plan** prevents that by answering, for
each stakeholder: what do they need to know, how often, through which
channel, and from whom. Base it on the stakeholder analysis from Chapter 2:
those you manage closely get frequent, two-way contact; those you keep
informed get regular updates.

Choose channels by what the message needs. A decision that needs discussion
goes to a meeting. A status update goes in a written report. An urgent risk
goes by phone or direct message, followed by writing.

Documentation makes the project visible and accountable. Keep every project
document in one shared, organized place; name files consistently; keep one
current version of each and a record of changes; and make documents readable
by everyone who needs them, including people using screen readers. A
document nobody can find doesn't exist.

## Worked example: completing the Eastside plan

**Budget.**

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Shelving, delivered and installed (fixed-price quote) | $4,200 |
| Cold storage: two used commercial refrigerators | $2,600 |
| Volunteer supplies: aprons, gloves, carts | $900 |
| Food-safety training certificates | $600 |
| Insurance rider for the school site | $1,100 |
| Signs and outreach flyers | $900 |
| **Subtotal** | **$10,300** |
| Contingency reserve (for the risks below) | $1,200 |
| **Cost baseline** | **$11,500** |
| Management reserve, held by the director | $500 |
| **Total budget** | **$12,000** |

**Procurement.** Shelving is a buy: the team can't build commercial-grade
shelving. The programs manager writes a one-page SOW (dimensions, load
rating, delivery to the gym, installation, removal of packaging), sends an
RFQ to three vendors, and selects on price and delivery date under criteria
set in advance. The contract is fixed-price. One vendor is owned by a board
member's cousin; the board member discloses it and stays out of the decision.

**Risk register.**

| Risk | P | I | Score | Response | Owner | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Too few volunteers sign up | 3 | 4 | 12 | Mitigate: recruit 25 for 20 slots | Volunteer coordinator | Fewer than 12 by day 10 |
| School withdraws permission | 2 | 5 | 10 | Mitigate: signed agreement first (task A) | Programs manager | Principal delays signing |
| Shelving delivered late | 3 | 3 | 9 | Accept: 2 days of float; confirm date weekly | Programs manager | Vendor misses confirmation |
| Refrigerators fail inspection | 2 | 4 | 8 | Transfer: buy from a vendor who certifies and warrants them | Programs manager | Inspection booked less than a week out |
| A grocer offers surplus produce | 3 | +3 | 9 | Exploit: arrange Saturday pickups | Volunteer coordinator | Grocer replies to outreach |

**Communication plan.**

| Who | What | How often | Channel | From |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Director (sponsor) | Status, risks, decisions needed | Weekly | 30-minute meeting and written summary | Programs manager |
| Principal | Schedule of gym use; anything touching the school | Every two weeks, and before changes | Email, then a call | Programs manager |
| Volunteers | Training dates, shifts | Weekly | Group message | Volunteer coordinator |
| Families | Opening date, how to sign up | Twice before opening | Flyers home with students, school newsletter | Programs manager |

Every document (the charter, schedule, budget, register, plan) lives in one
shared folder with a naming rule: `eastside-<document>-<date>`.

- **Budget; cost baseline.**
- **Direct and indirect costs; fixed and variable costs.**
- **Contingency reserve** (for identified risks) and **management reserve**
  (for unforeseen ones).
- **Procurement; make-or-buy; SOW; RFI, RFQ, RFP.**
- **Fixed-price, cost-reimbursable and time-and-materials contracts.**

- **Risk; issue; probability and impact; risk register; trigger; risk
  owner.**

  share, accept** (opportunities); **escalate** (either).
- **Communication plan.**

## Check yourself

1. What's the difference between a contingency reserve and a management
   reserve, and who controls each?
2. The food bank signs a contract to pay a contractor's actual costs plus 10
   percent. What type is it, and who bears the risk of cost overruns?
3. Classify each: (a) "the shelving vendor might go out of business"; (b)
   "the shelving vendor went out of business yesterday."
4. Buying an extended warranty on the refrigerators is which risk response?
5. A risk scores probability 4 and impact 2. Another scores probability 1 and
   impact 5. Which ranks higher on a probability-times-impact score?
6. Name the four questions a communication plan answers for each
   stakeholder.
7. A bidder offers the programs manager free tickets to a game during the
   evaluation. What should the programs manager do?

## Answers

1. A contingency reserve covers identified risks and is part of the cost
   baseline; the project manager uses it. A management reserve covers
   unforeseen work, sits outside the baseline, and is released only by the
   sponsor or management.
2. Cost-reimbursable (cost plus a percentage fee). The buyer, the food bank,
   bears the risk.
3. (a) a risk: it hasn't happened. (b) an issue: it has.
4. Transfer: the impact of a failure shifts to the warranty provider.
5. The first: 4 × 2 = 8, against 1 × 5 = 5. (A team may still watch the
   second closely because its impact is severe; the score is a guide.)
6. What they need to know, how often, through which channel, and from whom.
7. Decline the gift, since it could sway or appear to sway the choice, and
   tell whoever oversees procurement, so the evaluation stays fair.
