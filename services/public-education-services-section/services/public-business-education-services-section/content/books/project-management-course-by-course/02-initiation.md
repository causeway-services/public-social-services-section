# Initiation: goals, scope, stakeholders and the charter

*Companion to Course 2, Project Initiation. Before you start: Chapter 1's
life cycle.*

## What you will be able to do

- Weigh a project's expected benefits against its costs, and say whether it's
  worth starting.
- Write goals that are specific and measurable, and turn them into
  deliverables and success criteria.
- Draw a scope line, and spot scope creep when it starts.
- Identify stakeholders, analyze their power and interest, and record who
  does what in a RACI chart.
- Write a project charter that someone with authority can sign.

## Is it worth doing?

Initiation begins with a question the sponsor has to answer: is this project
worth what it costs? A **sponsor** is the person who wants the project,
provides its funding or authority, and is accountable for it succeeding in the
organization's eyes. At Eastside that's the food bank's executive director.

A **cost-benefit analysis** lists what the project will cost and what it will
return, and compares them. Costs include money, people's time, and what else
those people could have done instead. Benefits can be financial (revenue,
savings) or not (families served, risk reduced, reputation). When both sides
are in money, **return on investment (ROI)** is the net gain divided by the
cost. When the benefits aren't in money, as for most nonprofit projects, the
comparison is a judgment the sponsor makes with the numbers in front of them.

## Goals, deliverables and success criteria

A goal like "help more families" can't guide a team: nobody can tell when
it's been reached. In 1981 George Doran proposed that a management objective
should be **SMART**: specific, measurable, assignable (someone is named to do
it), realistic, and time-related [@doran1981]. Later writers changed the A to
"achievable" and the R to "relevant"; the course may use either version. The
idea is the same: a good goal says what, how much, by whom and by when.

Some organizations write goals as **OKRs**, objectives and key results: an
objective states the direction, and two to five key results make it
measurable. "Reach families without transport" is an objective; "serve 60
households on each of the first four Saturdays" is a key result.

A goal turns into **deliverables**, the tangible things the project hands
over: a stocked pantry, a trained volunteer roster, a sign-up form. And the
project needs **success criteria**, the standards by which the sponsor will
judge it finished and successful. Write them down during initiation, while
everyone still agrees, because they're what settles arguments later.

## Scope and scope creep

**Scope** is the boundary of the project: the work and deliverables included,
and just as important, what's excluded. Writing an explicit **out of scope**
list prevents the most common argument in project management, "I assumed that
was included."

**Scope creep** is the gradual growth of a project's scope without matching
changes to its time, budget or people. It usually arrives as reasonable
requests: "while you're setting up, could the pantry also hand out school
supplies?" Each one is small; together they sink the schedule. The defense
isn't refusing every change. It's routing each change request through a
decision: what it adds, what it costs in time and money, and who approves it.
Chapter 5 covers that process. A related trap is **gold-plating**, when the
team itself adds features nobody asked for.

## Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is anyone who affects the project or is affected by it.
Missing one during initiation is how projects meet surprises at the end.
Identify them by asking who pays, who decides, who does the work, who uses
the result, who has to live with it, and who could block it.

Then analyze them. A common tool is the power-interest grid, which Gerry
Johnson and Kevan Scholes adapted from a matrix Aubrey Mendelow described
for strategic scanning [@mendelow1981; @johnson-scholes1993]. It places each
stakeholder on two axes: how much **power** they have over the project and
how much **interest** they have in it:

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely |
| **Low power** | Monitor | Keep informed |

The grid tells you where to spend your attention. A high-power,
high-interest stakeholder, like a sponsor, is consulted on decisions. A
low-power, high-interest one, like a volunteer, gets regular updates.

## Roles and the RACI chart

Next, decide who does what. A **RACI chart** is a table with tasks as rows,
people as columns, and one letter in each cell:

- **R, Responsible:** does the work.
- **A, Accountable:** owns the outcome and approves the work. Exactly one per
  task.
- **C, Consulted:** gives input before the work is done; two-way.
- **I, Informed:** told after decisions are made; one-way.

The rule that matters most is one A per task. Two accountable people means
nobody is.

## Resources, documents, tools and the charter

A project's **resources** are the people, money, materials, equipment and
facilities it needs. List them early; the ones that are scarce or shared set
the limits of the plan.

Projects run on documents, because documents outlast memory and settle
disputes. The first and most important is the **project charter**. It
formally authorizes the project and gives the project manager the authority
to use resources. A charter typically holds:

- the project's purpose and business case;
- goals, deliverables and success criteria;
- scope and out of scope;
- key stakeholders and roles;
- budget and major milestones;
- known risks, assumptions and constraints;
- the sponsor's approval.

Finally, choose tools. Small projects can run on a shared spreadsheet and a
document folder. Larger ones use project management software for task lists,
schedules and boards. Pick the simplest tool the team will actually use, and
put every document in one shared place.

## Worked example: initiating the Eastside pantry

**Benefits and costs.** Cost: the $12,000 budget, about 200 hours of the
programs manager's time, and 400 volunteer hours. Benefit: families without
transport get food on weekends, which the weekday site can't reach. The
director compares these and approves.

**A SMART goal.** "By the Saturday after the second term starts, open a
weekend pantry in the Eastside Middle School gym that serves at least 60
households on each of its first four Saturdays, led by the programs manager,
within $12,000."

**Deliverables:** a signed building-use agreement; installed shelving and
cold storage; a trained roster of 20 volunteers; a family sign-up process;
a first-Saturday opening.

**Success criteria:** 60 households or more on each of the first four
Saturdays; no safety findings on the district inspection; spending of $12,000
or less.

**Scope.**

| In scope | Out of scope |
|---|---|
| Setting up the gym pantry | School-supply giveaways |
| Recruiting and training volunteers | Home delivery |
| A paper-and-phone sign-up process | A mobile app |

**Stakeholders.**

| Stakeholder | Power | Interest | Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive director (sponsor) | High | High | Manage closely |
| School principal | High | Medium | Manage closely |
| District facilities office | High | Low | Keep satisfied |
| Volunteer coordinator | Medium | High | Manage closely |
| Families | Low | High | Keep informed |
| Neighbors of the school | Low | Low | Monitor |

**RACI, for three tasks.**

| Task | Programs manager | Volunteer coordinator | Principal | Director |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Building-use agreement | R | I | C | A |
| Volunteer training | A | R | I | I |
| Opening-day readiness | A | R | C | I |

Each row has exactly one A.

The charter collects all of this onto two pages, and the director signs it.

## Key terms

- **Sponsor:** the person who champions and funds the project and is
  accountable for it in the organization.
- **Cost-benefit analysis:** comparing a project's expected costs with its
  expected benefits.
- **ROI:** net gain divided by cost.
- **SMART goal:** specific, measurable, assignable (or achievable), realistic
  (or relevant), time-related.
- **OKR:** an objective with two to five measurable key results.
- **Deliverable:** a tangible output the project hands over.
- **Success criteria:** the standards for judging the project finished and
  successful.
- **Scope, out of scope, scope creep, gold-plating.**
- **Stakeholder; power-interest grid.**
- **RACI:** responsible, accountable, consulted, informed.
- **Project charter:** the document that authorizes the project.

## Check yourself

1. What does the A in RACI mean, and how many should each task have?
2. Rewrite "improve volunteer satisfaction" as a SMART goal.
3. A board member asks the Eastside team to add a coat drive "since you'll
   be in the gym anyway." What's this called, and what should the project
   manager do?
4. Where on the power-interest grid does the district facilities office sit,
   and what does that mean in practice?
5. Name five things a project charter usually contains.
6. What's the difference between a deliverable and a success criterion?
7. In Doran's original 1981 version, what did the A in SMART stand for?
8. A second pantry site would cost $8,000 and 300 volunteer hours and would
   let 40 more families get food each week. How should the sponsor decide
   whether it's worth doing?

## Answers

1. Accountable: the person who owns the outcome and approves the work.
   Exactly one.
2. One possible answer: "By the end of the first term of operation, raise the
   average score on the volunteer survey's satisfaction question from 3.6 to
   4.2 out of 5, with the volunteer coordinator responsible." Any answer that
   is specific, measurable, owned and dated works.
3. Scope creep. Don't refuse or accept on the spot. Record it as a change
   request, estimate what it adds in time, money and volunteers, and take it
   to the sponsor to decide.
4. High power, low interest: keep satisfied. Meet its requirements (such as
   the inspection) without overloading it with updates, and consult it before
   anything that touches its rules.
5. Any five of: purpose or business case; goals; deliverables; success
   criteria; scope and out of scope; stakeholders and roles; budget;
   milestones; risks, assumptions and constraints; the sponsor's approval.
6. A deliverable is a thing the project hands over, like a trained volunteer
   roster. A success criterion is a standard for judging the project, like
   "60 households served each Saturday."
7. Assignable: the objective names who will do it.
8. By comparing the costs (money, volunteer time, and what else those
   volunteers could do) with the benefit (40 more families fed weekly). With
   no money return, there's no ROI to compute; the sponsor judges whether
   the benefit justifies the cost, with the numbers in front of them.
