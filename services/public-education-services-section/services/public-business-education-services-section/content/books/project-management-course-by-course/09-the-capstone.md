## Checking a charter and stakeholder analysis

A charter is good when a sponsor could sign it and a new team member could
understand the project from it alone. Check that it has:

- a purpose stated in one or two sentences, with the reason the project is
  worth doing;
- goals that pass SMART, each with a number and a date;
- deliverables as nouns ("trained volunteer roster"), not activities;
- success criteria someone could measure at the end;
- an explicit out-of-scope list;
- stakeholders and roles, budget, milestones, and the main risks and
  assumptions.

**Common mistakes:** goals without numbers; success criteria that restate the
goals; no out-of-scope list; a charter that's many pages long. Aim for two.

A stakeholder analysis is good when it changes what you'll do. Place every
stakeholder on the power-interest grid [@johnson-scholes1993], and give each a
strategy (manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor) and a note
on what they care about. **Common mistake:** a list of names with no strategy.

## Checking a project plan

A plan is good when someone else could run the project from it. Check that:

- every deliverable in the charter has tasks that produce it, and no task
  falls outside the scope (the 100 percent rule);
- tasks are small enough to track weekly, and each has one owner;
- milestones mark the end of each phase or major deliverable;
- dependencies are recorded, so you can see the critical path;
- each duration came from a technique you can name.

The capstone asks for **three-point estimates with confidence ratings**. For
each task, record the optimistic, most likely and pessimistic durations and
the PERT expected time, (o + 4m + p) / 6 [@malcolm1959]. A confidence rating
(for example high, medium or low) records how sure you are of the estimate
and why. Low confidence is information, not failure: it tells the reader
where the schedule could move, and it's a candidate for the risk register.

**Common mistakes:** a pessimistic time barely above the most likely one;
every estimate rated high confidence; milestones with durations.

## Checking quality standards, a survey and a retrospective

Quality standards are good when each is measurable and tied to a
deliverable: "cold storage at or below 40°F at every check," not "food is
kept safe."

A survey measures whether the project met its customers' needs. Write the
evaluation questions first (what do you need to know?) and then the survey
items that answer them. Good survey items:

- ask one thing each, avoiding double-barreled items like "Was the pantry
  clean and well organized?";
- don't lead the respondent toward an answer ("How much did you enjoy...");
- use a consistent rating scale, such as 1 to 5 from strongly disagree to
  strongly agree;
- end with one open question, such as "What one thing should we change?"

When you present what the survey found, put the conclusion in each slide's
title and show only the data that supports it [@tufte2001].

A retrospective is good when it leads to changes. It should record what went
well, what didn't, and what will change, and each change should have an
owner. Keep the tone on process, not people [@kerth2001].

## Checking a closeout report and an impact report

A closeout report is good when it compares the project against what it
promised. It should cover each success criterion (met or not, with the
number), the final schedule and budget against the baseline, the deliverables
handed over and to whom, and the lessons learned. Report misses plainly,
with their causes.

An impact report is good when it shows what changed for the people served,
with evidence, in terms the audience cares about. It's shorter and more
outward-facing than a closeout report: one page, a few numbers, a quote or
two from the people served, and what comes next.

**Common mistakes:** a closeout report that lists only successes; an impact
report that describes the project's activities ("we held four trainings")
rather than its effects ("64 families fed on the first Saturday").

## Turning the artifacts into a portfolio

The capstone's documents are evidence of what you can do. To use them in a
job search:

- Put them in one place with a one-page index: the project in two sentences,
  then each artifact with a line on what it shows.
- Remove anything confidential. The course scenario is invented, so your
  documents are yours to share, but check the course's terms on sharing
  course materials.
- In interviews, tell each artifact as a short story: the situation, what you
  did, and what resulted.

## Worked example: reviewing a draft goal and estimate

A learner's draft charter goal reads: "Improve the office's recycling." Check
it against SMART: not specific (which office? improve how?), not measurable,
not assigned, no date. Revised: "By 31 March, raise the share of the office's
waste that's recycled from 20 to 50 percent, measured by the weekly waste
audit, with the facilities coordinator responsible."

The same learner's plan estimates "set up bins" at o = 2, m = 2, p = 3 days,
confidence high. The pessimistic time is barely above the most likely one.
What could go wrong? The bins must be ordered and delivered. Asking the
supplier gives p = 10 days. The expected time becomes (2 + 8 + 10) / 6 ≈ 3.3
days, the confidence becomes medium, and "bin delivery late" goes in the risk
register.

## Check yourself

1. Name the artifacts the capstone's four modules ask you to produce.
2. What's wrong with the success criterion "the project goes well"? Rewrite
   it for a project of your choosing.
3. A task's estimates are o = 3, m = 5, p = 13 days. What's the PERT expected
   time? What confidence rating would you give, and why?
4. Rewrite this survey item so it asks one thing and doesn't lead: "How
   helpful and friendly were our wonderful volunteers?"
5. What's the difference between an evaluation question and a survey item?
6. A closeout report says, "The team held six planning meetings and trained
   twenty volunteers." What's missing?
7. What three things should a retrospective record?
8. What goes in a portfolio's one-page index?

## Answers

1. A project charter and a stakeholder analysis; a project plan with
   three-point estimates and confidence ratings; quality standards, a survey
   with its evaluation questions, a presentation of findings and a
   retrospective; a closeout report and an impact report.
2. It can't be measured, so nobody can tell whether it was met. An example
   rewrite: "At least 90 percent of attendees rate the workshop 4 or 5 out of
   5 in the exit survey."
3. (3 + 20 + 13) / 6 = 6 days. Medium or low: the pessimistic time is far
   above the most likely one, which shows real uncertainty; note why in the
   rating.
4. For example, two items: "Volunteers answered my questions." and
   "Volunteers treated me with respect." Each rated from strongly disagree to
   strongly agree.
5. An evaluation question is what the project needs to find out; survey
   items are the questions respondents answer so you can find it out.
6. A comparison against the success criteria, schedule and budget, with
   numbers; what was handed over; and the lessons learned. It lists
   activities, not results.
7. What went well, what didn't, and what will change, with an owner for each
   change.
8. The project in two sentences, then each artifact with a line on what it
   shows about your skills.
