## Business communication

Business writing succeeds when the reader can act on it quickly. Five
principles do most of the work:

- **Know the reader:** what they already know, what they need, and what they
  must decide.
- **Lead with the point:** the request or conclusion first, then the
  reasons. Busy readers stop early.
- **Use plain language:** short sentences, common words, active voice, one
  idea per paragraph.
- **Set the right tone:** courteous and direct; match formality to the
  relationship.
- **Choose the channel:** email for records and non-urgent requests, a call
  for anything sensitive or complicated, a meeting for decisions that need
  discussion.

Adapt the same message to different readers: a supplier, a customer and your
own team need different details.

## Networking

A network is a set of relationships that pass on information, opportunities
and help. Mark Granovetter found that people often learned of the jobs they
got through acquaintances rather than close friends: **weak ties** reach into
circles your close friends share with you, so they carry new information
[@granovetter1973].

A networking strategy:

1. Decide what you're trying to learn or do.
2. Map your current network: close ties, weak ties, and gaps.
3. Find places where the people you need gather: associations, events,
   online communities.
4. Offer help first; relationships built on giving last longer.
5. Keep in touch lightly and regularly.

## Generating ideas

**Ideation** produces possible solutions or opportunities. Separate
generating from judging: first produce many ideas without criticism, then
evaluate. Useful prompts: customers' unmet needs and complaints; what
competitors don't do; combining two existing offers; removing a step
customers dislike. Then screen ideas by fit with strategy, demand, cost and
risk, and test the most promising cheaply before committing.

## Disruptive technologies

Clayton Christensen studied why leading firms lose to newcomers
[@christensen1997]. **Sustaining** innovations improve products for existing
customers; leaders are good at those. **Disruptive** innovations start as
worse on the measures leaders' best customers care about, but cheaper,
simpler or more convenient. They take root among low-end or new customers the
leaders ignore, then improve until they take the leaders' mainstream
customers. Leaders often see them coming and rationally decline to chase a
small, low-margin market.

The theory explains many cases and not all; critics have found that the
conditions it requires often don't hold. Test them before calling something
disruptive: does it start with overlooked customers, and is it improving fast
enough to reach the mainstream?

## Going international

Jan Johanson and Jan-Erik Vahlne, studying Swedish firms, found that
businesses usually internationalize gradually, committing more as they learn
[@johanson-vahlne1977]. The typical sequence:

1. No regular exports.
2. Exporting through independent agents or distributors.
3. A sales subsidiary abroad.
4. Production abroad.

Firms tend to start with countries at a small **psychic distance**, those
whose language, culture and business practices are familiar, and move
further as they gain experience. Each stage raises commitment, control and
risk together.

## Ethics frameworks

Ethical decisions in business are rarely between an obvious right and wrong;
they're between competing goods and duties. Manuel Velasquez sets out
frameworks that ask different questions [@velasquez2011]:

- **Utilitarian:** which option produces the greatest balance of benefit
  over harm for everyone affected?
- **Rights:** which option best respects the rights of those involved, such
  as the right to truthful information or to safety?
- **Justice:** which option distributes benefits and burdens justly?
- **Caring:** which option honors the relationships and responsibilities
  involved?
- **Virtue:** what would a person of good character do?

Using more than one framework together shows where they agree (a strong signal)
and where they conflict (the real dilemma). Then decide, and be able to
explain why.

## Worked example: Harbor Street widens its view

**Communication.** A first draft to cafés opened with the bakery's history
and reached the offer in the fourth paragraph. The revision leads with the
point: "We can deliver fresh pastries to your café by 7 a.m., six days a
week, starting next month. Would you like a free tasting box on Tuesday?"
Details follow; the call to action is one reply.

**Networking.** The owner's close ties are other bakers, who know what she
knows. Her plan: join the city's independent business association and a
restaurant owners' group, where café owners are weak ties, and offer a free
bread-making workshop for members.

**Ideas.** A brainstorm of 30 ideas is screened to three: a "bake-at-home"
dough kit for grocers, a pastry subscription for offices, and a gluten-free
line. The office subscription is tested with five offices first.

**Disruption.** Is frozen bake-off bread disrupting independent bakeries?
It started cheaper and lower in quality and has improved; supermarkets and
cafés adopted it for convenience. It fits the pattern; Harbor Street's
response is to compete where freshness still matters most, not on price.

**International.** A café chain across the border asks for pastries. At stage
1, the owner considers stage 2: a local distributor there, low commitment,
with a nearby, similar market (small psychic distance), rather than opening
a shop abroad.

**Ethics.** A grocer asks Harbor Street to label next-day bread "baked this
morning." Utilitarian: sales rise briefly, but customers are misled and trust
falls if found out. Rights: customers have a right to truthful labels.
Justice: honest competitors are disadvantaged. Caring: the bakery's
relationship with its customers depends on trust. Virtue: an honest baker
wouldn't. All five agree: decline, and offer a "baked yesterday, half price"
label instead.

## Answers

1. For example: "Could we supply your café with fresh pastries starting next
   month? Here's what we'd deliver and when."
2. Because acquaintances move in circles you don't, so they hear of
   opportunities your close friends, who know what you know, don't.
3. A sustaining innovation improves products for existing customers; a
   disruptive one starts worse on what mainstream customers value but
   cheaper or simpler, takes root with overlooked customers, and improves
   until it takes the mainstream.
4. No regular exports; exporting through independent agents; a sales
   subsidiary abroad; production abroad.
5. How different a foreign market's language, culture and business practices
   feel; firms usually go first to markets that feel close and move further
   as they learn.
6. For example: utilitarian (does the saving outweigh the harm to the small
   supplier and the risk of depending on one?) and caring (what do you owe a
   supplier who has supported you for years?). Any two frameworks, applied,
   is correct.
7. Because judging ideas as they appear stops people from offering unusual
   ones and ends the search too early.
8. For example: decide the goal (learn the field and meet people who hire);
   map your ties (who you know in health care, however slightly); find where
   consultants and clients gather (associations, conferences, online groups);
   offer help first (share an analysis, volunteer on a committee); keep in
   touch lightly and regularly.
