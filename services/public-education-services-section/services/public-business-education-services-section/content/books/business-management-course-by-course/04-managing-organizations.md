## Principles of organizing

Structure follows strategy: once a business knows what it must do well, it
arranges people so that work gets done and coordinated. The classic
principles:

- **Division of labor:** split work into specialized jobs, so people build
  skill, while keeping jobs whole enough to be meaningful.
- **Chain of command:** everyone knows whom they report to and who decides
  what.
- **Span of control:** how many people report to one manager; wider spans are
  cheaper and give more autonomy, narrower ones allow closer support.
- **Centralization:** how far decisions are made at the top rather than near
  the work.
- **Formalization:** how far work is governed by written rules and
  procedures.
- **Departmentalization:** grouping jobs by function (baking, front of house,
  finance), by product, by customer or by place, or in a **matrix** combining
  two.

Henry Mintzberg described how organizations coordinate work: by **mutual
adjustment** (people talking directly), **direct supervision**, and
**standardization** of work processes, of outputs, or of skills
[@mintzberg1979]. Small organizations run on mutual adjustment; as they grow,
they add supervision and standards. A bakery's recipe is a standardized work
process; its training of bakers standardizes skills.

## Jobs: analysis, description and specification

A **job analysis** studies a job to find out what it involves: its tasks,
responsibilities, working conditions, and the knowledge, skills and abilities
it takes. It produces two documents:

- A **job description:** the job's purpose, main duties, reporting line and
  conditions.
- A **person specification:** the qualifications, skills, experience and
  personal qualities needed, split into essential and desirable.

Good job documents attract the right candidates and give a fair basis for
selection, because every applicant is judged against the same stated
requirements.

## The human resource management process

Human resource management covers the employee's whole path through the
organization:

1. **Workforce planning:** how many people with which skills, when.
2. **Recruiting:** attracting candidates.
3. **Selecting:** choosing among them against the person
   specification, with structured interviews and work samples.
4. **Onboarding:** welcoming and preparing new people.
5. **Training and development.**
6. **Performance management:** setting goals, giving feedback, appraising.
7. **Compensation and benefits.**
8. **Motivation and retention:** keeping good people engaged (Chapter 5).
9. **Separation:** handling departures with respect and due process.

## Organizational culture

**Culture** is the pattern of shared assumptions that tells people how
things are really done. Edgar Schein described three levels
[@schein1985]:

- **Artifacts:** what you can see and hear: the shop's layout, dress, how
  people talk to customers, rituals.
- **Espoused values:** what the organization says it values, in mission
  statements and posters.
- **Basic underlying assumptions:** the unspoken beliefs people act on
  without thinking.

When the levels agree, culture guides behavior reliably. When espoused
values say one thing and assumptions another ("we value work-life balance,"
but people who leave on time are passed over), employees believe the
assumptions and distrust the organization, which lowers satisfaction.

## Power

Power is the capacity to influence others. John French and Bertram Raven
distinguished five bases [@french-raven1959]: **legitimate** (from a
position), **reward**, **coercive**, **expert** (from knowledge) and
**referent** (from being respected or liked). Raven later added a sixth,
**informational** power, from controlling or providing information
[@raven1965]. Power in
organizations flows through formal structure, and also around it: the baker
everyone asks for advice has expert power whatever the organization chart
says. Recognizing who holds which kind of power tells you how decisions really
get made, and how you're affected by them.

## The organizational brief

An **organizational brief** sets out how a business will organize to carry
out its strategy: the structure and reporting lines, key roles with their
job descriptions, the HR processes for staffing them, the culture to build,
and the power and decision rights involved. Chapter 7 gives a checklist.

## Worked example: organizing Harbor Street's wholesale line

**Structure.** Today the bakery is organized by function under the owner:
a head baker (bakers), a front-of-house manager (three shop leads), and a
bookkeeper. Wholesale adds a **wholesale coordinator** reporting to the
owner, who works across baking (orders) and delivery (van driver). The head
baker's span grows from 9 to 11 with the early-shift bakers; the owner's
span grows from 3 to 4.

**Coordination.** Wholesale orders are standardized (a fixed order form due
at 2 p.m.), baking is standardized by recipe, and the coordinator and head
baker use mutual adjustment for exceptions.

**Job description (wholesale coordinator).** Purpose: grow and serve
wholesale accounts. Duties: take and confirm orders; plan the delivery route;
handle account questions and replacements; report weekly sales and margin.
Reports to: owner. **Person specification.** Essential: experience in food
service; organized; comfortable with spreadsheets; driving license.
Desirable: sales experience.

**Culture.** Artifacts: flour-dusted aprons, bakers greeting regulars by
name. Espoused values: "fresh, local, generous." Assumption: the shops come
first. Risk: wholesale staff may feel second-class. Response: the owner
announces wholesale as part of the bakery's purpose, and wholesale results
are shared at the weekly all-staff meeting.

**Power.** The head baker holds legitimate power over bakers and expert power
everyone respects; if she opposes the early bake, it fails. So the owner
involves her in designing it.

2. A bakery trains every baker in the same techniques so they can work

3. What's the difference between a job description and a person

4. Put these HR steps in order: selecting, onboarding, workforce planning,

5. A company's posters say "we value open communication," but people who
   question decisions are sidelined. Which of Schein's levels conflict, and
   which will employees believe?

   kind of power does that give her?
7. Why does the owner involve the head baker in designing the early bake?
8. Name four things an organizational brief covers.

## Answers

1. Wider spans cost less and give people more autonomy; narrower spans allow
   closer supervision and support.
2. Standardization of skills.
3. A job description describes the job (purpose, duties, reporting,
   conditions); a person specification describes the person needed
   (qualifications, skills, experience).
4. Workforce planning, recruiting, selecting, onboarding.
5. Espoused values conflict with basic underlying assumptions; employees
   believe the assumptions, shown by what actually happens.
6. Informational power (and expert power).
7. Because she holds legitimate power over the bakers and expert power they
   respect; without her support the change would fail.
8. Any four of: structure and reporting lines; key roles with job
   descriptions; HR processes for staffing them; the culture to build; power
   and decision rights (and the strategy it serves).
