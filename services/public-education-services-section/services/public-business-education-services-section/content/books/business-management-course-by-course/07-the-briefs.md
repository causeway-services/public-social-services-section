# The briefs: a guide to the course projects

*Companion to the projects in DYS552 and DYS554. Before you start: Chapters 2
and 4.*

## What you will be able to do

- Say what the strategic marketing brief and the organizational brief each
  must contain.
- Check a draft of either brief against a list of what makes it strong.
- Avoid the mistakes that most often weaken them.

## What the courses ask for

eCornell's course pages describe two written projects
[@ecornell-bme-2026]: in Marketing Fundamentals, a strategic marketing brief
built in three parts, and in Managing Organizations, an organizational brief
built in five parts, one for each step of structuring a firm. This chapter doesn't describe
the courses' templates or grading. It gives a checklist for each kind of
document, drawn from the chapters that teach its parts, so you can judge your
own draft. Use your own organization, as the courses ask, or a business you
know well.

## Checking a strategic marketing brief

A strong brief lets a reader who has never met your business see who it
serves, why they would choose it, and what you'll do. Check that it has:

- **A situation** in a few sentences: the business, the market, and the
  specific opportunity or problem, with at least one number.
- **Segments** described by the needs that distinguish them, not only by
  demographics, and the **target** chosen with reasons: size, fit, and
  competition (Chapter 2).
- **A positioning statement** that names the target, the need, the benefit
  and the alternative.
- **A marketing mix** in which each P serves the positioning and fits the
  others; price decisions checked against margins (Chapter 1).
- **Acquisition and retention plans** with measures: customer acquisition
  cost, retention rate, lifetime value.
- **A budget and timeline.**

**Common mistakes:** targeting "everyone"; a positioning statement with no
alternative named; a price chosen without checking the margin it leaves;
acquisition plans with no retention plan.

## Checking an organizational brief

A strong organizational brief shows how the organization will be arranged to
carry out its strategy. Check that it has:

- **The strategy it serves,** in a sentence (Chapter 3).
- **A structure:** how work is grouped, the reporting lines, spans of
  control, and where decisions are made (Chapter 4).
- **Coordination:** which work is standardized and where people adjust
  directly.
- **Key roles** with job descriptions and person specifications.
- **HR processes** for recruiting, selecting, onboarding, developing and
  keeping people.
- **Culture:** the values to build and how artifacts and practice will show
  them, not only statements.
- **Power and decision rights:** who holds which kind of power, and how the
  plan wins the support of those whose cooperation it needs (Chapter 5).

**Common mistakes:** an organization chart without the reasons for it; job
descriptions that list tasks but no purpose; culture described only as
slogans; ignoring the informal power that decides whether change sticks.

## Worked example: reviewing a draft

A draft marketing brief for a neighborhood gym reads: "Target market: adults
18 to 65 who want to be healthy." Against the checklist: that's nearly
everyone, described by demographics, with no distinguishing need. Revised:
"Target: shift workers within two miles who want to exercise at odd hours and
are poorly served by gyms that close at 10 p.m. About 1,400 people, per the
hospital's and warehouse's staffing." The positioning, hours and pricing now
follow from a real need.

A draft organizational brief for the same gym proposes "a friendly culture."
Against the checklist: a slogan. Revised: "Artifacts: staff learn members'
names in the first week; a board celebrates members' milestones. Practice:
new staff shadow the night manager for two shifts."

## Check yourself

1. Name the six parts of a strategic marketing brief.
2. What's wrong with "our target market is everyone who eats bread"?
3. Why should a price in the brief be checked against margin?
4. Name five things an organizational brief should cover.
5. Rewrite "we will have an innovative culture" so it can be checked.

## Answers

1. Situation; segments and target; positioning statement; marketing mix;
   acquisition and retention plans with measures; budget and timeline.
2. It isn't a target: it names no distinguishing need and gives no basis for
   choosing a product, price, place or promotion.
3. Because a price that attracts customers but leaves too little margin can
   grow revenue while shrinking profit, as Harbor Street's first year of
   wholesale showed.
4. Any five of: the strategy it serves; structure and reporting; coordination;
   key roles with job documents; HR processes; culture in practice; power and
   decision rights.
5. For example: "Every team spends Friday afternoons testing one improvement
   idea and reports the result at the monthly meeting."
