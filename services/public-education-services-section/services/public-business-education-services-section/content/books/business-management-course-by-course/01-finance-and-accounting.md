## Accounting and finance

**Accounting** records what has happened to a business's money and reports it
in standard forms, so that owners, lenders, tax authorities and managers can
trust and compare the numbers. **Finance** uses those numbers to decide what
to do next: where to invest, how to pay for it, and how much risk to take.
Accounting looks back and makes the record; finance looks forward and makes
choices with it.

Accounting rests on **double-entry bookkeeping**: every transaction is
recorded twice, once as a debit and once as a credit of equal size. When the
bakery buys $5,000 of flour on credit, its inventory rises by $5,000 and what
it owes suppliers rises by $5,000. Because every entry has a matching entry,
the books always balance.

## The accounting equation and the balance sheet

The balance of the books is the **accounting equation**:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

**Assets** are what the business owns or controls that will bring future
benefit: cash, money customers owe (**accounts receivable**), inventory,
ovens, the fit-out of the shops. **Liabilities** are what it owes: suppliers
(**accounts payable**), loans, wages earned but not yet paid. **Equity** is
what's left for the owners: the money they put in plus the profits kept in
the business (**retained earnings**).

The **balance sheet** shows the equation at one moment, usually the end of a
year. It separates **current** items, expected to turn into cash or be paid
within a year, from **noncurrent** ones.

## The income statement

The **income statement** shows performance over a period:

- **Revenue:** what customers were charged.
- minus **cost of goods sold (COGS):** the direct cost of what was sold
  (flour, butter, coffee beans, packaging).
- equals **gross profit.**
- minus **operating expenses:** wages, rent, utilities, marketing.
- equals **operating income.**
- minus **interest** and **taxes**, equals **net income** (profit).

Most businesses use **accrual accounting**: revenue is recorded when it's
earned and expenses when they're incurred, whether or not cash has moved yet.
So a profitable business can still run out of cash. That's why a third
statement, the **cash flow statement**, shows where cash actually came from
and went, in operating, investing and financing activities.

## Ratios

A number alone says little: is $97,500 of profit good? Ratios compare
numbers to each other so they can be compared across years and businesses
[@subramanyam2014]:

| Ratio | Formula | Tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Current ratio** | Current assets / current liabilities | Can short-term debts be paid? |
| **Quick ratio** | (Current assets − inventory) / current liabilities | The same, without relying on selling inventory |
| **Gross margin** | Gross profit / revenue | How much of each sales dollar is left after direct costs |
| **Operating margin** | Operating income / revenue | Profit from the business's operations |
| **Net margin** | Net income / revenue | Profit after everything |
| **Return on assets (ROA)** | Net income / total assets | Profit earned on everything the business uses |
| **Return on equity (ROE)** | Net income / equity | Profit earned on the owners' money |
| **Inventory turnover** | COGS / inventory | How many times inventory is used up in a year |
| **Days sales outstanding** | Accounts receivable / revenue × 365 | How long customers take to pay |
| **Debt-to-equity** | Total liabilities / equity | How much the business relies on borrowing (some analysts use only interest-bearing debt) |

Read ratios in context: against the same business last year (a **trend**, or
horizontal analysis), against similar businesses, and against each other. A
high ROE from heavy borrowing is riskier than the same ROE from high margins.

**Common-size** (vertical) analysis expresses every line as a percentage of
revenue, which makes years of different size comparable.

## Worked example: Harbor Street's two years

**Income statement.**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $1,800,000 | $2,000,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | 630,000 | 760,000 |
| **Gross profit** | 1,170,000 | 1,240,000 |
| Operating expenses | 990,000 | 1,080,000 |
| **Operating income** | 180,000 | 160,000 |
| Interest | 20,000 | 30,000 |
| Income before tax | 160,000 | 130,000 |
| Tax (25%) | 40,000 | 32,500 |
| **Net income** | 120,000 | 97,500 |

**Balance sheet, end of Year 2.**

| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $90,000 | Accounts payable | $70,000 |
| Accounts receivable | 60,000 | Current portion of loans | 55,000 |
| Inventory | 50,000 | Accrued wages and taxes | 35,000 |
| **Current assets** | **200,000** | **Current liabilities** | **160,000** |
| Equipment and fit-out, net | 600,000 | Long-term loans | 240,000 |
| | | **Total liabilities** | **400,000** |
| | | Equity | 400,000 |
| **Total assets** | **800,000** | **Total liabilities and equity** | **800,000** |

The equation holds: $800,000 = $400,000 + $400,000.

**Trends.** Revenue grew (2,000,000 − 1,800,000) / 1,800,000 ≈ 11.1 percent.
Profit fell:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 1,170 / 1,800 = 65.0% | 1,240 / 2,000 = 62.0% |
| Operating margin | 180 / 1,800 = 10.0% | 160 / 2,000 = 8.0% |
| Net margin | 120 / 1,800 ≈ 6.7% | 97.5 / 2,000 ≈ 4.9% |

**Year 2 ratios.** Current ratio 200 / 160 = 1.25. Quick ratio (200 − 50) /
160 ≈ 0.94. ROA 97.5 / 800 ≈ 12.2 percent. ROE 97.5 / 400 ≈ 24.4 percent.
Inventory turnover 760 / 50 = 15.2, about every 24 days (365 / 15.2).
Days sales outstanding 60 / 2,000 × 365 ≈ 11 days. Debt-to-equity 400 / 400 =
1.0.

**Interpretation and recommendation.** The bakery grew, but each dollar of
sales now earns less. Gross margin fell three points: the Year 2 trial of
wholesale sales carries lower prices, and flour costs rose. Receivables
appeared because wholesale customers pay on terms; retail customers pay at
the till. The business can pay its short-term debts (current ratio above 1),
though with little to spare once inventory is set aside. Recommendation:
before expanding wholesale, price it so its gross margin covers the extra
delivery and credit costs, and set payment terms of no more than 14 days.

## Apply it to your organization

1. Get your organization's latest income statement and balance sheet, or a
   public company's.
2. Calculate the current ratio, gross and net margins, ROE and
   debt-to-equity.
3. Compare each with the year before. Which moved most, and why?
4. Write one recommendation the numbers support.

## Check yourself

1. The bakery takes out a $50,000 loan and deposits the cash. What happens
   to each side of the accounting equation?
2. What's the difference between gross profit and operating income?
3. How can a profitable business run out of cash?
4. A business has current assets of $300,000, inventory of $120,000 and
   current liabilities of $200,000. What are its current and quick ratios?
5. Revenue is $500,000 and net income $40,000. What's the net margin?
6. Two bakeries each have an ROE of 25 percent. One has a debt-to-equity of
   0.5, the other 3.0. Which is riskier, and why?
7. Harbor Street's revenue grew but its net margin fell. Name two likely
   causes from the worked example.
8. In one sentence each, what does accounting do in a business, and what does
   finance do?
9. A business has COGS of $600,000 and inventory of $80,000. What's its
   inventory turnover, and about how many days of stock does it hold?
10. A café's revenue grew 15 percent while its gross margin fell 4 points
    and its days sales outstanding doubled. What would you recommend before it
    expands further?

## Answers

1. Assets rise by $50,000 (cash) and liabilities rise by $50,000 (the loan);
   equity doesn't change.
2. Gross profit subtracts only the direct costs of what was sold; operating
   income also subtracts operating expenses such as wages and rent.
3. Under accrual accounting, revenue can be recorded before customers pay,
   and cash can be tied up in inventory or equipment, so profit on paper
   doesn't guarantee cash in the bank.
4. Current ratio 300 / 200 = 1.5; quick ratio (300 − 120) / 200 = 0.9.
5. 40,000 / 500,000 = 8 percent.
6. The one with debt-to-equity of 3.0: its return depends on borrowing, so a
   fall in profit could leave it unable to meet its debts.
7. Lower-priced wholesale sales and higher flour costs (together lowering
   gross margin), plus higher interest.
8. Accounting records what has happened to the business's money and reports
   it in standard forms; finance uses those numbers to decide what to do next,
   such as where to invest and how to pay for it.
9. 600,000 / 80,000 = 7.5 turns a year, about 365 / 7.5 ≈ 49 days of stock.
10. For example: find out why margin fell and fix pricing or costs first, and
    tighten payment terms to bring cash in sooner, before adding volume that
    would deepen both problems.
