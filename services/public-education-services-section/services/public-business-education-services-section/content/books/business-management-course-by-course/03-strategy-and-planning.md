## Generic strategies

A strategy is a set of choices about where to compete and how to win. Michael
Porter argued that a business can gain a lasting advantage in one of three
broad ways [@porter1980]:

- **Cost leadership:** be the lowest-cost producer in the industry, and win
  on price or margin.
- **Differentiation:** offer something customers value and will pay more for:
  quality, design, service, brand.
- **Focus:** serve a narrow segment better than broad competitors, either at
  lower cost (cost focus) or with a tailored offer (differentiation focus).

Porter warned that a business trying to do both cost and differentiation
risks being **stuck in the middle**, beaten on price by the cost leader and on
quality by the differentiator. Later studies found businesses that combine
the two successfully, often because quality and scale lower costs together
[@miller-dess1993]. The risk is real; the rule isn't absolute. The question
to ask is whether each choice reinforces the others.

## The value chain

Porter's **value chain** breaks a business into the activities that create
value for customers [@porter1985]. **Primary activities** move the product to
the customer:

1. **Inbound logistics:** receiving and storing inputs.
2. **Operations:** turning inputs into products.
3. **Outbound logistics:** getting products to customers.
4. **Marketing and sales.**
5. **Service:** support after the sale.

**Support activities** make the primary ones possible: **firm infrastructure**
(management, finance, planning), **human resource management**, **technology
development** and **procurement** (buying inputs).

The **margin** is what customers pay minus the cost of all these activities.
Mapping the chain shows where a business's advantage comes from and where
money leaks out. Costs add up along the chain, so a new activity (such as
delivery) must earn its place.

## SWOT

A **SWOT analysis** lists a business's internal **strengths** and
**weaknesses** and its external **opportunities** and **threats**. The idea
comes from the Harvard business policy teachers of the 1960s, who framed
strategy as fitting what a firm does well to what its environment offers
[@learned1965]; the often-repeated credit to a Stanford Research Institute
project has no primary source.

A SWOT is useful only if it leads to choices. Cross the quadrants:

- **Strengths × opportunities:** use strengths to seize opportunities.
- **Weaknesses × opportunities:** fix weaknesses that block opportunities.
- **Strengths × threats:** use strengths to blunt threats.
- **Weaknesses × threats:** limit exposure where both meet.

Keep each entry specific and evidenced: "gross margin fell from 65 to 62
percent" rather than "costs are high."

## Shareholders and stakeholders

Whose interests should a business serve? In 1970 Milton Friedman argued that
a business's social responsibility is to increase its profits, within the
rules of the game [@friedman1970]. In 1984 R. Edward Freeman argued that
managers must account for all **stakeholders**, the groups who affect or are
affected by the firm: employees, customers, suppliers, communities and
owners [@freeman1984]. In 2019 the Business Roundtable, a group of
large-company chief executives, committed to deliver value to all stakeholders, not
only shareholders [@brt2019].

These are positions, not findings, and a manager needs a way to decide in
practice. One approach: list the stakeholders; state what each expects;
judge how much each can affect the business and how much the decision
affects them; look for options that serve more than one at once; and when interests
conflict, decide openly and say why. Sustained profit usually depends on
treating employees, customers and suppliers well, so the two views often
agree in the long run and disagree in the short run.

## Forecasting for planning

Plans need estimates of the future. Simple methods, in order of effort:

- **Naive:** next period equals this period.
- **Moving average:** the average of the last few periods, which smooths out
  noise.
- **Trend:** extend the recent rate of growth.
- **Scenarios:** a pessimistic, an expected and an optimistic case, each with
  its assumptions stated.

Forecasts are always wrong by some amount. State the assumptions, give a
range rather than a single number, and compare the forecast with what
happens.

## Worked example: Harbor Street's strategy

**Generic strategy.** Harbor Street can't be the cost leader against
supermarket bakeries and national suppliers. Its position is
**differentiation focus**: freshness, local ingredients and neighborhood
cafés for a local market.

**Value chain for wholesale.** Inbound: flour from a regional mill.
Operations: an extra 4 a.m. bake. Outbound: a new delivery van and driver, a
new activity with new costs. Marketing and sales: tasting boxes and a
wholesale contact. Service: replacing damaged goods. The chain shows that
delivery costs must come out of a lower wholesale price, which is why
Chapter 2 priced wholesale at a 50 percent gross margin.

**SWOT.**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Freshness and local reputation; loyal retail customers; skilled bakers | Gross margin down from 65 to 62 percent; ovens near capacity at peak; no delivery experience |

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| About 60 independent cafés and grocers nearby served by frozen products | Flour prices rising; a supermarket in-store bakery opening nearby |

Crossing them: strengths × opportunities, offer same-morning fresh pastries
to cafés that now use frozen products; weaknesses × opportunities, add an
early bake to use oven capacity off-peak; weaknesses × threats, lock in flour
prices with a one-year contract before expanding.

**Stakeholders.** Owner (profit and growth); employees (early shifts need
pay and volunteers, not assignment); retail customers (no drop in quality);
the regional mill (a larger, steadier order); neighbors (a van at 5 a.m.).
The plan pays a 15 percent premium for the early shift, recruits volunteers
for it, loads the van on the side street away from homes, and signs a
one-year flour contract. Profit is served, and so are the groups whose
cooperation it depends on.

**Forecast.** Wholesale revenue in the first year: pessimistic 8 accounts,
expected 15, optimistic 25, each at $20,000 a year: $160,000, $300,000 or
$500,000, with the assumption that an account averages $400 a week.

## Answers

1. Cost leadership, differentiation, focus.
2. A business pursuing both low cost and differentiation without achieving
   either. Not always: some businesses combine them successfully, but it's a
   real risk.
3. A threat: it's outside the business and could hurt it. Weaknesses are
   internal.
4. Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales,
   service.
5. For example: run book clubs for students and stock course-related reading
   for the new university.
6. Friedman: a business's social responsibility is to increase its profits
   within the rules. Freeman: managers must account for all the groups who
   affect or are affected by the firm.
7. Naive: 120. Moving average: (100 + 110 + 120) / 3 = 110.
