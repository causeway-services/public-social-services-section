## What marketing is

Marketing is every decision that lets a product exist in the world and be
bought: what it is, who it's for, what it costs, where people can get it,
and how they hear of it. Advertising is one small part. A useful test of a
marketing decision is whether it starts from a customer's need rather than
from what the business happens to make.

## Segmentation, targeting and positioning

No offer suits everyone. In 1956 Wendell Smith described **market
segmentation**: treating a market as groups of customers with different
needs, rather than one mass market, and shaping offers for chosen groups
[@smith1956]. Marketers now describe the work in three steps, **segmentation,
targeting and positioning** [@kotler-keller2016]:

1. **Segment:** divide the market by characteristics that change what people
   need. **Demographic** (age, income, household), **geographic** (where they
   live or work), **psychographic** (values, lifestyle) and **behavioral**
   (how often they buy, what benefit they want). Business customers are
   segmented by industry, size and buying process.
2. **Target:** choose the segments to serve, by their size and growth, how
   well the business can serve them, and how much competition there is.
3. **Position:** decide how the offer should be seen in the target's mind
   relative to alternatives, in one sentence: "For [target] who [need],
   [offer] is the [category] that [benefit], unlike [alternative]."

## The marketing mix

Once the target is set, the **marketing mix** is the set of decisions that
deliver the positioning. E. Jerome McCarthy grouped them as the **4 Ps**
[@mccarthy1960]:

- **Product:** what's offered, including quality, range, packaging and
  service.
- **Price:** list price, discounts, payment terms, and how price signals
  value.
- **Place:** where and how customers get it: shops, delivery, wholesale
  partners, online.
- **Promotion:** how customers learn about it: advertising, social media,
  events, word of mouth, sales.

Services often add three more, which Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner
proposed: **people**, **process** and **physical evidence** (the setting
where the service happens) [@booms-bitner1981]. Each P must fit the
target and fit the others. A premium product sold at a discount in a
cluttered shop sends mixed signals.

## Acquiring and keeping customers

**Acquisition** brings new customers in; **retention** keeps them. Both cost
money, and they pay back differently.

- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** is what it costs, on average, to win a
  customer: marketing and sales spending divided by new customers won.
- **Customer lifetime value (CLV)** is the profit a customer brings over the
  whole relationship: in the simplest form, yearly profit from the customer
  times the expected years they stay. A business is growing soundly when CLV
  is well above CAC.

Frederick Reichheld and W. Earl Sasser found that keeping customers is often
worth more than winning new ones: in the service businesses they studied,
reducing the share of customers who leave by 5 percentage points raised
profits by 25 to 85 percent [@reichheld-sasser1990]. Long-standing customers
buy more, cost less to serve, and bring others. So a marketing plan needs both
an acquisition plan and a retention plan: loyalty programs, service that
solves problems, regular contact that's useful rather than noisy.

## The strategic marketing brief

A **marketing brief** is a short document that turns this analysis into a
plan. Its usual parts:

1. **Situation:** the business, the market, the problem or opportunity.
2. **Target segments** and what each needs.
3. **Positioning statement.**
4. **Marketing mix:** decisions for each P.
5. **Acquisition and retention plans,** with measures (CAC, retention rate,
   CLV).
6. **Budget and timeline.**

## Worked example: marketing Harbor Street's wholesale bread

**Segments.** Retail: morning commuters (speed, coffee), weekend families
(treats, a table), remote workers (a place to sit, Wi-Fi). Wholesale: small
grocers (daily fresh bread, reliable delivery), independent cafés (pastries
they can't bake themselves), restaurants (dinner rolls, consistent size).

**Target.** For the wholesale trial: independent cafés and small grocers
within 10 miles, a segment large enough to matter (about 60 businesses),
reachable with one delivery van, and served today mainly by a national
supplier's frozen products.

**Positioning.** "For independent cafés who want to offer fresh local
pastries without a pastry kitchen, Harbor Street Wholesale is the local bakery
that delivers them baked the same morning, unlike frozen bake-off products."

**Mix.** Product: 12 bestselling items, packed for display. Price: 30 percent
below retail, with volume tiers. With retail's direct costs at 35 percent
of the retail price, that keeps wholesale gross margin at 50 percent: (0.70 −
0.35) / 0.70 (Chapter 1's warning). Place: delivery by 7 a.m., six days a week. Promotion:
free tasting boxes delivered to 30 cafés; the Harbor Street name on café
counters.

**Acquisition and retention.** The tasting boxes cost $1,200 and win 8 new
accounts: CAC $150. A typical café account buys $400 of pastries a week, 50
weeks a year: $20,000 of revenue, $10,000 of gross profit at a 50 percent
margin. If an account stays two years on average, CLV is about $20,000
against a CAC of $150. Retention plan: a standing weekly order, a named
contact, and a replace-it-free promise for any damaged delivery.

## Answers

1. Because marketing includes every decision about the product, its price,
   where it's sold and who it's for; advertising is one part of promotion.
2. Behavioral.
3. For example: "For families looking for a weekend treat together, Harbor
   Street is the neighborhood bakery with a table for everyone and pastries
   baked that morning, unlike chain coffee shops."
4. CAC = 6,000 / 40 = $150. CLV = 100 × 3 = $300. Yes: each customer is worth
   twice what it cost to win them.
5. That in the service businesses they studied, cutting the share of
   customers who leave by 5 percentage points raised profits by 25 to 85
   percent.
6. Price and product. It isn't necessarily a problem: the premium packaging
   supports the cafés' own prices and carries the brand; it's a problem only if
   its cost pushes wholesale margin below target.
7. Situation; target segments; positioning; marketing mix; acquisition and
   retention plans with measures; budget and timeline.
