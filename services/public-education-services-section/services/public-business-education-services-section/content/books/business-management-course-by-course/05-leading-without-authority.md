## Satisfaction and dissatisfaction

In 1959 Frederick Herzberg and colleagues asked engineers and accountants to
describe times they felt especially good and especially bad about their jobs
[@herzberg1959]. The good times centered on **motivators**: achievement,
recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement and growth. The bad
times centered on **hygiene factors**: company policy, supervision, pay,
relationships at work and working conditions. Herzberg concluded that the two
work differently: fixing hygiene factors removes dissatisfaction but doesn't
create satisfaction; only motivators do that.

The distinction is useful: a raise won't make a boring job engaging, and an
engaging job won't survive unfair pay. But its strong form is contested.
Robert House and Lawrence Wigdor showed that the split depends on the
interview method, which invites people to credit themselves for good events
and blame others for bad ones; other methods found the same factors can both
satisfy and dissatisfy [@house-wigdor1967]. Use it as a checklist, not a law.

## Designing for motivation

Three well-tested ideas turn motivation into design choices:

- **Job design.** Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham found that work is more
  motivating when it has **skill variety**, **task identity** (doing a whole
  piece of work), **task significance**, **autonomy** and **feedback**
  [@hackman-oldham1976].
- **Expectancy.** Victor Vroom argued that effort depends on three beliefs:
  that effort will lead to performance (**expectancy**), that performance
  will lead to a reward (**instrumentality**), and that the reward is valued
  (**valence**) [@vroom1964]. If any is near zero, motivation collapses.
- **Goals.** Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's decades of research found that
  specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague ones like
  "do your best," provided people accept the goal, have the ability, and get
  feedback [@locke-latham2002].

None of these requires formal authority. Anyone leading a project can give
colleagues whole pieces of work, show how effort pays off, and agree on
specific goals with feedback.

## Kinds of capital

A team's capacity rests on more than money. Three kinds of capital matter:

- **Human capital:** the knowledge, skills and experience of individuals,
  which Gary Becker showed can be invested in like physical capital
  [@becker1964].
- **Social capital:** the relationships, trust and norms among people, which
  make cooperation easier; James Coleman described how it helps create human
  capital, as mentors and networks pass on skill [@coleman1988].
- **Structural (organizational) capital:** the knowledge built into
  processes, systems and documents, which stays when people leave
  [@edvinsson-malone1997].

A high-performing team uses all three: skilled people (human), who trust and
help each other (social), with shared tools and methods (structural). A team
of stars who don't trust each other has human capital without social
capital, and underperforms.

## High-performing teams

Jon Katzenbach and Douglas Smith defined a real team as a small number of
people with complementary skills, committed to a common purpose, performance
goals and approach, for which they hold themselves mutually accountable
[@katzenbach-smith1993]. To form and support one:

- Keep it small and choose for complementary skills.
- Agree on a purpose that matters and specific performance goals.
- Agree on how the team will work: roles, meetings, decisions.
- Build **psychological safety**, the shared belief that it's safe to speak
  up, admit mistakes and disagree, which Amy Edmondson linked to team learning
  [@edmondson1999].
- Hold each other, not only the leader, accountable.

## Resolving conflict

Conflict in a team isn't always bad. Karen Jehn distinguished **task
conflict** (disagreement about the work) from **relationship conflict**
(personal friction): moderate task conflict can improve decisions on complex
work, while relationship conflict consistently hurts [@jehn1995]. The goal is
to keep disagreement about the work and stop it becoming personal.

Two tools help:

- The Thomas-Kilmann model names five responses: **competing**,
  **collaborating**, **compromising**, **avoiding** and **accommodating**
  [@thomas-kilmann1974]. Collaborating fits important issues with time to
  work them out; the others fit particular situations.
- Roger Fisher and William Ury's principled negotiation: separate the people
  from the problem; focus on **interests**, not **positions**; invent options
  for mutual gain; and use objective criteria [@fisher-ury1981]. A position is
  what someone says they want; an interest is why they want it.

## Worked example: leading the early bake

The wholesale coordinator has no authority over the bakers, who report to
the head baker. He needs volunteers for a new 4 a.m. shift.

**Satisfaction and motivation.** Hygiene: the 15 percent shift premium and a
guaranteed ride home in bad weather remove reasons to refuse. Motivators:
the early bakers own the whole wholesale order (task identity), see their
bread on café counters around town (significance), and plan the bake order
themselves (autonomy). Expectancy: the coordinator shows the order volumes, so
bakers can see the work is steady.

**Goals.** Specific and agreed: "all wholesale orders packed by 6:30 a.m.,
99 percent of items correct," reported weekly.

**Capital.** Human: two bakers who've run early shifts elsewhere. Social:
the head baker vouches for the plan. Structural: a written wholesale bake
sheet so anyone can cover a shift.

**Conflict.** Two bakers clash over whether to bake croissants first or last.
Positions: "first" against "last." Interests: one wants the ovens hottest for
croissants; the other wants them freshest at pickup. Option: bake them third,
after the ovens peak, and pack them last. Task conflict improved the process;
the coordinator kept it from turning personal by asking each for their
reason.

## Answers

1. No; pay is a hygiene factor, which can remove dissatisfaction but not
   create satisfaction. Motivators such as achievement, recognition,
   responsibility and the work itself would.
2. That the split between motivators and hygiene factors depends on the
   interview method, which invites people to credit themselves for good
   events and blame others for bad ones; other methods find the same factors
   can both satisfy and dissatisfy.
3. Instrumentality: the belief that performance leads to reward.
4. For example: "Deliver 98 percent of orders by 7 a.m. each week this
   quarter, reviewed every Friday."
5. Human capital (a baker's skill), social capital (trust between shifts),
   structural capital (a written bake sheet).
6. Task conflict is disagreement about the work; relationship conflict is
   personal friction. Relationship conflict is consistently more harmful;
   moderate task conflict can improve decisions.
7. "What would a second van let you do?" (asking why, to find the interest,
   such as faster deliveries or a backup when one van breaks down).
8. Any three of: agree on a purpose and specific performance goals; agree on
   how the team will work (roles, meetings, decisions); build psychological
   safety by inviting disagreement and admitting your own mistakes; make
   members accountable to each other, not only to you.
