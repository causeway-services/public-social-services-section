# Domain 1, part 1: projects, life cycles and processes

*Covers Domain 1, Task 1: "Demonstrate an understanding of the various
project life cycles and processes." No prerequisites.*

## What you will be able to do

- Distinguish a project from a program, a portfolio and operations.
- Distinguish predictive, iterative, incremental, adaptive and hybrid life
  cycles, and say which fits a situation.
- Name the five process groups and the ten knowledge areas, and explain why
  process groups aren't phases.
- Recognize PMBOK 7's principles and performance domains.
- Tell issues, risks, assumptions and constraints apart.
- Review a scope statement for gaps, and tell product scope from project
  scope.
- Apply PMI's code of ethics to a scenario, and explain how a project
  delivers change.

## Projects, programs, portfolios and operations

The Riverside Clinic has four kinds of work going on at once. Front-desk staff
book appointments every day. The booking project will replace the phone line
with online booking. The clinic's parent organization runs that project
alongside a new patient portal and a records upgrade, all aimed at "digital
access to care." And its board decides each year which of those efforts, plus
new buildings and new services, get money.

The *PMBOK Guide* names these [@pmbok7]:

- A **project** is a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product,
  service or result. The booking project.
- **Operations** are ongoing work that sustains the organization. Daily
  booking at the front desk.
- A **program** is a group of related projects (and sometimes operations)
  managed together to get benefits that managing them separately wouldn't.
  The digital-access program.
- A **portfolio** is the collection of projects, programs and operations
  managed as a group to achieve strategic objectives. The board's annual
  choices.

The exam's favorite distinction is project against operations: a project ends
and hands its result over; operations continue. When the booking system goes
live, running it becomes operations.

**A project is a vehicle for change.** Every project moves an organization
from a current state to a desired future state. PMBOK 7 makes this one of its
principles, "enable change to achieve the envisioned future state," and it
means the project's work includes helping people adopt the result: training
the front desk, telling patients, and retiring the old phone-only process.

## Life cycles and development approaches

A **project life cycle** is the series of phases a project passes through from
start to finish. Within it, the **development approach** is how the product
itself gets made. The *Agile Practice Guide* sorts approaches by two
questions: how often the requirements are revisited, and how often something
is delivered [@agile-practice-guide2017].

| Life cycle | Requirements | Delivery | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predictive** | Fixed early | Once, at the end | Stable, well-understood work |
| **Iterative** | Refined by repeated cycles | Once, at the end | Complex work where feedback improves a design before release |
| **Incremental** | Fixed per piece | In pieces, each usable | A product that can be released in parts |
| **Adaptive (Agile)** | Refined each cycle | Frequently, each usable | Uncertain requirements and cheap change |
| **Hybrid** | Mixed | Mixed | Projects with both stable and uncertain parts |

Agile is iterative and incremental at once: it refines through cycles *and*
delivers something usable at the end of each.

The exam asks you to pick an approach from a scenario. Read for three clues:
how certain the requirements are, how costly late change is, and how often
users can give feedback. The Riverside vendor contract and hardware are
stable and costly to change: predictive. The booking screens need patients'
feedback and are cheap to change: adaptive. The project is hybrid.

## Process groups and knowledge areas

PMI organizes project management processes into five **process groups**
[@pmi-process-groups2022]:

1. **Initiating:** define a new project or phase and get authorization. The
   charter and the stakeholder register come from here.
2. **Planning:** establish scope, objectives and the course of action. The
   project management plan comes from here.
3. **Executing:** do the work in the plan.
4. **Monitoring and Controlling:** track, review and regulate progress;
   identify and make changes.
5. **Closing:** formally complete the project, phase or contract.

Process groups aren't phases. A phase is a stretch of the life cycle ("design,"
"build"). The process groups can all happen inside any phase: a design phase
is initiated, planned, executed, monitored and closed. Monitoring and
controlling runs alongside all the others.

The same guide sorts the processes a second way, into ten **knowledge areas**:
integration, scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk,
procurement and stakeholders. **Integration** is the one that ties the rest
together: the charter, the project management plan, directing the work,
change control and closing all belong to it. The guide describes 49 processes
across the grid of groups and areas; you don't need to memorize them, but you
should be able to say which group and area a common activity belongs to.
Building a WBS, for example, is planning and scope.

## PMBOK 7: principles and performance domains

The seventh edition of the *PMBOK Guide* reorganized PMI's standard around
**principles** rather than processes, so that it applies to predictive and
adaptive work alike [@pmbok7]. Its twelve principles:

1. **Stewardship:** care responsibly for the resources, people and trust
   placed in the project.
2. **Team:** build a collaborative team environment.
3. **Stakeholders:** engage stakeholders actively throughout.
4. **Value:** keep the focus on the value the project delivers.
5. **Systems thinking:** see how the project's parts, and its surroundings,
   affect one another.
6. **Leadership:** show leadership behaviors, whatever your role.
7. **Tailoring:** fit the approach to the context.
8. **Quality:** build quality into processes and deliverables.
9. **Complexity:** navigate complexity rather than ignore it.
10. **Risk:** make risk responses proportionate and effective.
11. **Adaptability and resiliency:** adjust to change and recover from
    setbacks.
12. **Change:** enable the change that reaches the envisioned future state.

And eight **performance domains**, groups of related activities that matter
to delivering outcomes: stakeholders, team, development approach and life
cycle, planning, project work, delivery, measurement, and uncertainty.

The two views coexist on the exam. Questions about "what should the project
manager do next" often lean on principles (focus on value, engage
stakeholders); questions about documents lean on process groups.

## Issues, risks, assumptions and constraints

Four words that sound alike and aren't:

- A **risk** is an uncertain event that, if it occurs, affects an objective.
  It's in the future. "The vendor may deliver late." Recorded in the risk
  register.
- An **issue** is a current problem that needs action. "The vendor delivered
  late." Recorded in the issue log.
- An **assumption** is something believed true without proof, for planning.
  "Patients have phones that receive texts." Recorded in the assumption log.
  Every assumption is also a risk that it's false.
- A **constraint** is a limit the project must work within. "The budget is
  $80,000"; "the system must go live before flu season." Also recorded in the
  assumption log.

A risk that happens becomes an issue. An assumption that proves false becomes
an issue too.

## Reviewing scope

**Product scope** is the features and functions of the result: online
booking, text reminders. **Project scope** is the work needed to deliver it:
selecting a vendor, configuring, testing, training. The **scope statement**
describes both, with the deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions,
constraints and assumptions.

To critique a scope statement, look for what's missing: deliverables without
acceptance criteria, no exclusions list, vague words ("user-friendly"),
assumptions no one has checked, and product features with no project work to
build them.

## The code of ethics

PMI's *Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct* rests on four values:
**responsibility**, **respect**, **fairness** and **honesty**
[@pmi-code-of-ethics]. Each has aspirational standards (what practitioners
strive for) and mandatory standards (what they must do). Mandatory standards
include, for example, complying with laws and reporting unethical conduct,
disclosing conflicts of interest, and not deceiving others.

Ethics questions on the exam are usually scenarios. The right answer follows
the rules and involves the right person: disclose the conflict, report the
violation, tell the truth about status, refuse the gift. Wrong answers usually
either ignore the problem or take drastic action alone.

## Worked example: classifying Riverside's situation

**Project or operations?** Replacing phone booking is a project; daily
booking afterward is operations.

**Life cycle?** Hybrid: predictive for vendor selection, hardware and
training; adaptive for the booking screens.

**Process groups in the vendor-selection phase:** initiating (confirm the
phase's goal and budget), planning (write the requirements and the
selection criteria), executing (issue the request for proposals, hold demos),
monitoring and controlling (compare progress and spending with the plan),
closing (sign the contract, close the phase).

**Sorting four statements:**

| Statement | Kind |
|---|---|
| "The go-live must happen before 1 October." | Constraint |
| "Staff will be available for two hours of training each." | Assumption |
| "The texting provider may raise its prices." | Risk |
| "The test server crashed this morning." | Issue |

**Critiquing a draft scope line:** "The system will be easy to use." It has no
measure. Revised: "In usability testing, 8 of 10 patients aged 65 or over book
an appointment without help within five minutes."

**An ethics scenario.** A vendor offers the project manager a free conference
trip during evaluation. Under fairness, the project manager declines, discloses
the offer to the director, and keeps the evaluation as scored.

## Check yourself

1. A hospital runs its pharmacy every day, is building a new wing, and
   manages the wing alongside a parking garage and a road project because
   they share a construction schedule and a city permit. The wing, the garage
   and the road together are best described as:
   - A. A portfolio
   - B. A program
   - C. Operations
   - D. A phase
2. A team delivers a working part of a product every three weeks, changes
   priorities between deliveries based on feedback, and releases each part to
   users. Which life cycle is this?
   - A. Predictive
   - B. Iterative only
   - C. Incremental only
   - D. Adaptive
3. Which statement about process groups is correct?
   - A. They're the phases of a predictive project, in order.
   - B. They can all occur within a single phase.
   - C. Monitoring and controlling happens only after executing.
   - D. Agile projects don't use them.
4. "Our lead developer may leave for another job" is:
   - A. An issue
   - B. A constraint
   - C. A risk
   - D. An assumption
5. A scope statement lists "online booking" and "text reminders" but no
   testing or training. What's missing?
   - A. Product scope
   - B. Project scope
   - C. The charter
   - D. The risk register
6. During vendor selection, the project manager learns that a team member's
   spouse works for one of the bidders. What should the team member do?
   - A. Nothing, as long as they score fairly
   - B. Disclose the relationship and step out of the evaluation
   - C. Quietly give that bidder an average score
   - D. Resign from the project
7. Which PMBOK 7 principle most directly calls for training the front desk
   and retiring the old phone-only process?
   - A. Tailor based on context
   - B. Navigate complexity
   - C. Enable change to achieve the envisioned future state
   - D. Build quality into processes and deliverables

## Answers

1. **B.** Related projects managed together for shared benefits form a
   program. A portfolio (A) is broader and organized around strategy; the
   pharmacy is operations (C); a phase (D) is part of one project's life
   cycle.
2. **D.** Delivering usable parts often and refining requirements each cycle
   is adaptive, which is iterative and incremental together. B and C each
   describe only half of it.
3. **B.** Process groups aren't phases (A); monitoring and controlling runs
   throughout (C); adaptive projects still initiate, plan, execute, monitor
   and close, in shorter loops (D).
4. **C.** It hasn't happened and it's uncertain. If the developer resigns,
   it becomes an issue (A).
5. **B.** Those are features of the product (product scope); the work to
   test and train is project scope.
6. **B.** PMI's code requires disclosing conflicts of interest and not
   letting them influence decisions. A and C leave the conflict hidden; D is
   an overreaction.
7. **C.** Helping people adopt the result and move to the future state is
   the "enable change" principle.
