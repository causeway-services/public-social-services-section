## The project management plan and its parts

The **project management plan** is the document that says how the project
will be executed, monitored, controlled and closed. It's assembled from
**subsidiary plans**, one for each area that needs managing
[@pmi-process-groups2022]:

| Plan | Says how the project will... |
|---|---|
| Scope management plan | define, validate and control scope |
| Requirements management plan | gather, track and change requirements |
| Schedule management plan | build and control the schedule |
| Cost management plan | estimate, budget and control costs |
| Quality management plan | set and meet quality standards |
| Resource management plan | acquire and manage people and materials |
| Communications management plan | inform stakeholders |
| Risk management plan | identify, analyze and respond to risk |
| Procurement management plan | buy from outside |
| Stakeholder engagement plan | involve stakeholders |
| Change management plan | handle change requests |

It also holds three **baselines**, the approved versions that progress is
measured against: the **scope baseline** (scope statement, WBS and WBS
dictionary), the **schedule baseline** and the **cost baseline**. Together
they're the **performance measurement baseline**. Changing a baseline requires
change control.

Each plan exists to answer a question before it becomes an argument. Cost
planning prevents surprise overruns; quality planning prevents disputes about
"good enough"; risk planning turns surprises into prepared responses;
schedule planning shows what must happen when.

**Project plan or product plan?** A project management plan covers one
project, from start to close. A **product management plan** covers a product
across its whole life, often through many projects and releases, with a
roadmap and a business owner. Riverside's booking project ends at go-live;
the booking product continues, with its own roadmap, for years.

## Milestones, durations and resources

A **task duration** is how long an activity takes: "configure the booking
screens: 10 days." A **milestone** is a significant point with zero duration:
"booking live for patients." Milestones mark ends and decision points.

**Resources** are people (with skills and availability), equipment,
materials, facilities and money. To determine the number and type, work from
the WBS: each work package needs certain skills for certain hours. Compare
that with **resource calendars** (when each resource is available), and
record assignments in a **responsibility assignment matrix**, such as a RACI
chart. Riverside needs two front-desk staff for 20 hours of testing each; a
vendor configuration specialist for 10 days; and a trainer for three
sessions.

## The risk register and the stakeholder register

The **risk register** records each identified risk with its description,
probability, impact, priority, owner, response and trigger. Use it when
something changes: a trigger fires, a new risk appears, or a response is
carried out. The owner acts; the register is updated.

The **stakeholder register** records each stakeholder's identification
(name, role, contact), assessment (requirements, expectations, influence,
interest) and classification (for example on a power-interest grid). Use it to
plan engagement and communication and to make sure no one is forgotten. It
starts in initiating and is updated throughout.

## Closure and transition

**Closing** a phase or project means confirming all work is done and
accepted, and ending it formally. The steps: get formal acceptance of
deliverables; **transition** the result to operations (documents, training,
support contacts); close procurements; release resources; record **lessons
learned**; archive project records; and report final performance. A project
can also close early, when it's cancelled; it still needs closing, and its
lessons may be the most valuable.

## Roles: sponsor, project manager and team

| Role | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Provides funding and authority; champions the project in the organization; approves the charter; decides escalated issues and major changes; owns the business case and benefits |
| **Project manager** | Leads the team to the project's objectives; plans, coordinates, communicates; manages scope, schedule, cost, risk and stakeholders day to day |
| **Project team** | Does the project work; estimates it; raises risks and issues; on adaptive teams, self-organizes around the work |

When a decision exceeds the project manager's authority, such as adding budget
or changing a major objective, the sponsor decides. When the team is blocked
by something outside the project, the project manager escalates to the
sponsor.

The outline names roles a project manager plays: **initiator** (starts
action), **negotiator** (for resources and agreements), **listener**,
**coach** (develops the team), **working member** (on small projects, does
some work) and **facilitator** (runs meetings and workshops so the group
decides well).

**Leadership and management.** John Kotter argued that management and
leadership are different and both needed: management copes with complexity
(planning and budgeting, organizing and staffing, controlling and problem
solving), while leadership copes with change (setting a direction, aligning
people to it, motivating and inspiring them) [@kotter1990]. A project manager
does both.

**Emotional intelligence** (EQ) is the ability to recognize and manage your
own emotions and to recognize and influence others'. Daniel Goleman described
it in components such as self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy
and social skill [@goleman1995; @goleman1998]. On projects, EQ is what lets a project
manager stay calm in a crisis, read a stakeholder's worry behind their
objection, and build the trust that influence depends on.

**Team development.** New teams tend to pass through stages Bruce Tuckman
named in 1965: **forming** (polite and unsure), **storming** (disagreement
over goals and roles), **norming** (settling rules and roles) and
**performing** (working well with little direction) [@tuckman1965]; he and
Mary Ann Jensen later added **adjourning**, the team's end
[@tuckman-jensen1977]. Storming is normal, not failure. A project manager
directs more while a team is forming and steps back as it performs.

**Conflict** is normal on projects. The Thomas-Kilmann model names five
responses to it: **competing**, **collaborating**, **compromising**,
**avoiding** and **accommodating** [@thomas-kilmann1974]. Collaborating, which
works out a solution that meets both sides' underlying concerns, is usually
the best choice when there's time; the others fit particular situations, such
as avoiding a trivial issue or competing in an emergency.

## Following the plan

Plans exist to be followed. When something the plan anticipated happens,
carry out the planned response:

- A risk's trigger fires: the risk owner carries out the response in the risk
  register.
- A stakeholder needs an update: send it as the communications plan says.
- A deliverable is ready: validate it by the acceptance criteria in the scope
  baseline.

When something the plan didn't anticipate happens, don't improvise a change to
the baseline. Assess the impact, and submit a change request or escalate.

**Initiation and benefits planning.** A project starts from a **business
case**, which justifies it: the need, the options and the recommended one, and
the expected costs and benefits. The **benefits management plan** says what
benefits the project should bring, who owns each, when they should appear
(often after the project closes) and how they'll be measured. The **charter**
then authorizes the project and names the project manager.

## Problem-solving tools and meetings

The outline names these techniques:

- **Brainstorming** generates many ideas quickly, without criticism, then
  sorts them.
- A **focus group** brings together a small group of stakeholders, often
  users, with a facilitator, to discuss their expectations or reactions.
- A **stand-up** (daily scrum) is a short, daily meeting where the team
  synchronizes and raises blockers.
- **Root-cause analysis** finds why a problem happened: the **five whys**
  (ask why repeatedly until the cause is actionable) and the **cause-and-effect
  diagram** (fishbone or Ishikawa), which groups possible causes by category.

A meeting is effective when it has a clear purpose and agenda, the right
people, stays on time, and ends with decisions and action items that have
owners and dates. To evaluate one, ask whether it produced what it was called
for.

## Worked example: Riverside in planning and execution

**The plan.** The project manager assembles the project management plan from
the subsidiary plans above, with a scope baseline (scope statement, a WBS
of 34 work packages, and a WBS dictionary), a 15-week schedule baseline, and a
cost baseline of $72,000 inside the $80,000 budget.

**A stakeholder register entry.**

| Name | Role | Requirements | Influence | Interest | Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Front-desk supervisor | Operations lead | Booking must not slow check-in; staff trained before go-live | High | High | Manage closely: weekly check-in, in every design review |

**A trigger fires.** The risk register holds: "Vendor may lose key
configuration staff. Trigger: vendor misses two status calls. Response:
contract clause requiring a named replacement within five days." The vendor
misses two calls. The owner invokes the clause; the register is updated.

**Something unplanned.** The clinic's director asks for Spanish and
Vietnamese screens. That's a scope change: the project manager estimates
three weeks and $6,000, and submits a change request to the sponsor.

**A focus group.** Before designing the reminder texts, the business analyst
runs a focus group of eight patients, including four over 70. They learn
that patients want to reply "C" to confirm. Brainstorming with the team then
produces six reminder designs to test.

**A root cause.** Test bookings fail at night. Five whys: bookings fail →
the system rejects them → the calendar is locked → a nightly backup locks it →
the backup was scheduled during open booking hours. Fix: move the backup.

## Check yourself

1. Which document says how the project will identify, analyze and respond
   to risks, rather than listing the risks themselves?
   - A. Risk register
   - B. Risk management plan
   - C. Issue log
   - D. Assumption log
2. A software product has a five-year roadmap with a release every quarter.
   The upcoming release is run as a project. Which document covers the
   roadmap?
   - A. The project charter
   - B. The project management plan
   - C. The product management plan
   - D. The scope baseline
3. A team member reports that a risk's trigger has occurred. What should the
   project manager do first?
   - A. Call a meeting to decide what to do
   - B. Ensure the risk owner carries out the planned response
   - C. Submit a change request
   - D. Escalate to the sponsor
4. The team needs $15,000 beyond the approved budget to meet a new
   regulation. Who decides?
   - A. The project manager
   - B. The team
   - C. The sponsor, through change control
   - D. The vendor
5. According to Kotter, which activity is leadership rather than management?
   - A. Preparing the budget
   - B. Staffing the team
   - C. Setting a direction and aligning people to it
   - D. Controlling variances
6. Two team members argue over whose design to use; the project manager helps
   them find a design that meets both of their main concerns. Which conflict
   mode is this?
   - A. Compromising
   - B. Collaborating
   - C. Accommodating
   - D. Avoiding
7. Which tool best finds why a defect keeps recurring?
   - A. Stand-up
   - B. Focus group
   - C. Five whys
   - D. Stakeholder register
8. Which document records when and how a project's benefits will be
   measured, often after the project ends?
   - A. Benefits management plan
   - B. Project charter
   - C. Lessons learned register
   - D. Communications management plan

## Answers

1. **B.** The plan says how risk is managed; the register (A) lists the risks.
2. **C.** The product's whole-life roadmap belongs to the product management
   plan; the project management plan (B) covers only this release's project.
3. **B.** The response was planned; carry it out. A meeting (A) delays a
   decision already made; a change request (C) or escalation (D) is for what
   the plan didn't anticipate or can't cover.
4. **C.** Changing the cost baseline beyond the project manager's authority
   goes through change control to the sponsor.
5. **C.** Kotter assigns direction-setting, aligning and motivating to
   leadership; A, B and D are management.
6. **B.** Collaborating meets both parties' underlying concerns.
   Compromising (A) gives each side part of what they wanted.
7. **C.** Five whys is a root-cause technique. The others don't analyze
   causes.
8. **A.** The benefits management plan names each benefit, its owner, its
   timing and its measure.
