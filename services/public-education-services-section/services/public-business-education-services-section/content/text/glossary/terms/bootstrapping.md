Bootstrapping means funding a business entirely from the owner's personal resources and the business's own revenue. No investors, no bank loans, no SBA financing. The business grows only as fast as its cash flow allows.

### Why bootstrap

- **Full ownership**: No equity given away, no board to answer to, no investor expectations about growth rate or exit timeline.
- **No debt**: No monthly loan payments, no [interest expense](../terms/debt-service.md), no [collateral](./collateral.md) at risk.
- **Discipline**: When every dollar is your own, spending decisions are sharper. Bootstrapped businesses tend to reach profitability faster because they have to — there's no cushion of investor money.
- **Simplicity**: No loan applications, no investor pitches, no term sheets, no cap tables.

### The constraints

- **Slower growth**: A business that could grow faster with $50,000 in equipment may take 18 months to save for that equipment from revenue. The opportunity cost of slower growth is real but hard to measure.
- **Personal financial risk**: The owner's savings are the business's capital. If the business fails, the savings are gone — without the partial protection that investor money provides.
- **Limited scale**: Some businesses cannot be bootstrapped because the minimum viable operation requires significant capital (a restaurant build-out, manufacturing equipment, licensed premises).
- **Cash flow sensitivity**: With no credit reserve, a single bad month can threaten the business. A [line of credit](./line-of-credit.md) — even if unused — provides a safety net that pure bootstrapping doesn't have.

### Bootstrapping in practice

Most [side hustles](./side-hustle.md) and [home-based businesses](./home-based-business.md) are bootstrapped by default — the owner invests a few hundred or a few thousand dollars of personal money, earns revenue, and reinvests the profit. The decision to seek external financing typically comes when growth requires capital that revenue can't fund quickly enough.
