Cottage food laws permit individuals to make and sell specific food products from a home kitchen without the commercial kitchen licensing, health department inspections, and food handler certifications that commercial food businesses require. Every U.S. state has some form of cottage food law, but the rules vary dramatically.

### What varies by state

**Allowed products**: Most states permit "non-potentially hazardous" foods — items that don't require refrigeration and are unlikely to cause foodborne illness. Common examples: baked goods (bread, cookies, cakes without cream fillings), jams and jellies, honey, dried herbs, candy, granola, roasted nuts. Most states prohibit: meat, dairy, canned low-acid foods (requires pressure canning knowledge), foods requiring refrigeration.

**Sales channels**: Some states allow only direct-to-consumer sales (farmers' markets, roadside stands, online with in-person pickup). Others allow sales to retail stores or restaurants. A few allow shipping within the state.

**Revenue caps**: Many states set annual revenue limits — $25,000, $50,000, or $75,000 are common thresholds. Some states have no cap. Exceeding the cap typically requires transitioning to a licensed commercial kitchen.

**Labeling requirements**: Most states require a label stating: the product name, ingredients, allergens, the producer's name and address, and a disclaimer such as "Made in a home kitchen that is not inspected by [state health department]."

### Finding your state's rules

Search "[your state] cottage food law" or check your state's department of agriculture website. The rules are specific and non-negotiable — selling a prohibited product or exceeding the revenue cap can result in fines and a cease-and-desist order.

### Why cottage food matters

Cottage food laws are the lowest barrier to entry in the food business. They allow someone to start selling baked goods or preserves with zero commercial infrastructure — no lease, no build-out, no commercial kitchen investment. For many food entrepreneurs, cottage food is the first step: validate the product, build a customer base, and generate revenue before investing in a commercial operation.
