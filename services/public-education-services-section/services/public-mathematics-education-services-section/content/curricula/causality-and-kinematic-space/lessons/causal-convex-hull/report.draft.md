# Causal Convex Hulls in Kinematic Space

## Executive answer

Causal order can define a meaningful convex-hull operation in kinematic space, but only after “convex” is given an order-theoretic meaning. The natural definition is:

> A subset is causally order-convex if, whenever it contains two causally comparable points, it contains every point causally between them.

The causal convex hull of a set is then the smallest order-convex set containing it. Equivalently, it is obtained by repeatedly filling all causal intervals between comparable members.

For the standard two-dimensional kinematic space of boundary intervals, this construction is particularly concrete. A kinematic point represents an interval \(I=(u,v)\), or, in a holographic setting, the boundary-anchored geodesic subtending that interval. Causal comparability is interval containment. If \(I_1\subseteq I_2\), the order interval between their kinematic points is a rectangular causal diamond in endpoint coordinates. Thus the two-point causal hull is exactly that diamond.

This answer has important qualifications. Causal order alone does not define affine combinations, straight segments, angles, area, proper time, or a unique metric. It therefore does not produce an ordinary Euclidean convex hull. It gives an order hull. Its boundary is generated by null directions, and its content records interval inclusion rather than metric proximity. Additional information—such as an entropy-derived Crofton form—is required to assign sizes or information-theoretic weights to the hull.

## 1. Prerequisites: three spaces that must not be confused

The construction involves three distinct levels.

First is the **boundary spatial slice**, often a line or circle. Its connected subregions are intervals \(I=(u,v)\).

Second is a **bulk spatial geometry**, such as a time-symmetric slice of \(\mathrm{AdS}_3\). In the simplest holographic example, a boundary interval selects a boundary-anchored bulk geodesic. The Ryu–Takayanagi proposal relates the entropy of a boundary region \(A\) to the area of a corresponding minimal bulk surface,

\[
S(A)=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\gamma_A)}{4G_N},
\]

which in three bulk dimensions means the length of a geodesic divided by \(4G_N\). This is a result within the semiclassical holographic regime, not a universal identity for arbitrary quantum systems; the original proposal and its scope are given by [Ryu and Takayanagi](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603001).

Third is **kinematic space** \(K\). A point of \(K\) is not generally a bulk event. It represents an entire boundary interval or an entire bulk geodesic. Czech, Lamprou, McCandlish, and Sully define the relevant two-dimensional kinematic space as the space of oriented boundary-anchored geodesics and show that its causal structure encodes containment among boundary intervals ([Czech et al., *Integral Geometry and Holography*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)). Consequently, “timelike” in kinematic space normally means “related by interval containment,” not “capable of sending a physical signal through the original bulk spacetime.”

That distinction prevents a common category error: a kinematic causal diamond is a family of intervals or geodesics, whereas a physical causal diamond is a set of spacetime events.

## 2. Causal order before metric distance

A partial order on a set \(P\) is a relation \(\preceq\) satisfying:

1. **Reflexivity:** \(x\preceq x\).
2. **Antisymmetry:** if \(x\preceq y\) and \(y\preceq x\), then \(x=y\).
3. **Transitivity:** if \(x\preceq y\) and \(y\preceq z\), then \(x\preceq z\).

Two points are **comparable** if either \(x\preceq y\) or \(y\preceq x\). A partial order need not compare every pair.

For boundary intervals, choose the convention

\[
I_1\preceq I_2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
I_1\subseteq I_2.
\]

For intervals on a line,

\[
I_1=(u_1,v_1),\qquad I_2=(u_2,v_2),
\]

this is equivalent to

\[
u_2\le u_1
\quad\text{and}\quad
v_1\le v_2.
\]

The containing interval begins no later and ends no earlier. Reversing the order convention would merely interchange “future” and “past”; it would not change which pairs are comparable or which points lie between them.

This containment relation supplies genuine causal structure in kinematic space. Sharing one endpoint gives a null relation, strict containment gives a timelike relation, and overlapping or disjoint intervals for which neither contains the other are spacelike-separated in the kinematic sense. The identification of this partial order with kinematic causality is part of the construction in [Czech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515).

Order does not, by itself, supply a numerical distance. This limitation is structural. If \(g\) is a Lorentzian metric, then a positive conformal rescaling

\[
g'=\Omega^2 g,\qquad \Omega>0,
\]

leaves light cones and causal comparability unchanged while changing lengths, areas, volumes, and proper times. In suitably distinguishing spacetimes, causal structure can determine topology and conformal geometry, but not the missing conformal scale; the relevant reconstruction result was established by [Malament in 1977](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/jmp/article-abstract/18/7/1399/460709/The-class-of-continuous-timelike-curves-determines). One should therefore reject any inference of the form “these points have the same order relation, so their metric separation is known.”

## 3. From order intervals to an order-convex hull

Given comparable elements \(x\preceq z\) of a poset, define their **order interval**

\[
[x,z]_{\preceq}
=
\{y\in P:x\preceq y\preceq z\}.
\]

This definition uses only the relation \(\preceq\). It contains no metric and no assumption about straight lines.

A subset \(C\subseteq P\) is **order-convex** when

\[
x,z\in C,\quad x\preceq y\preceq z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y\in C.
\]

The **causal order-convex hull** of \(A\subseteq P\) may then be defined by

\[
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(A)
=
\bigcap\{C\subseteq P:
A\subseteq C
\text{ and }C\text{ is order-convex}\}.
\]

The intersection is well defined because \(P\) itself is order-convex. This is a hull operator in the ordinary abstract sense:

\[
A\subseteq \operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(A),
\]

\[
A\subseteq B
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(A)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(B),
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}
\bigl(\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(A)\bigr)
=
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}(A).
\]

For computation, begin with \(A_0=A\) and repeatedly fill intervals:

\[
A_{n+1}
=
A_n\cup
\bigcup_{\substack{x,z\in A_n\\x\preceq z}}
[x,z]_{\preceq}.
\]

The union of the stages, continued until no new elements appear, is the order-convex closure. In an infinite poset, one should define the result by the intersection formula above or allow transfinite continuation rather than assume that a finite number of rounds always suffices.

This construction is not identical to every use of “causal convex hull” in Lorentzian geometry. In spacetime causality theory, a causally convex set is commonly required to contain every causal curve whose endpoints lie in the set. Hounnonkpe and Minguzzi use causal convex hulls of compact sets in their characterization of global hyperbolicity for general cone structures ([Hounnonkpe and Minguzzi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11701)). In well-behaved two-dimensional kinematic settings, filling causal intervals and containing causal segments agree naturally, but in more general spaces definitions based on curves, order intervals, closure, openness, or topology can differ. Any claim about “the causal hull” must therefore state its convention.

## 4. Explicit construction in interval kinematic space

Let

\[
p=(u_p,v_p),\qquad q=(u_q,v_q)
\]

represent intervals \(I_p=(u_p,v_p)\) and \(I_q=(u_q,v_q)\). Suppose

\[
I_p\subseteq I_q,
\]

so that

\[
u_q\le u_p,\qquad v_p\le v_q.
\]

A third interval \(I=(u,v)\) lies causally between them precisely when

\[
I_p\subseteq I\subseteq I_q.
\]

In endpoint coordinates, this becomes

\[
u_q\le u\le u_p,
\qquad
v_p\le v\le v_q.
\]

Therefore,

\[
[p,q]_{\preceq}
=
[u_q,u_p]\times[v_p,v_q],
\]

subject to the requirement \(u<v\) and any global identifications imposed by a circular boundary. The order interval is a rectangle in the \((u,v)\) chart. Because \(u\) and \(v\) are null coordinates for the kinematic metric, this rectangle is a causal diamond.

### Worked example

Take

\[
I_p=(2,5),\qquad I_q=(0,8).
\]

Since \((2,5)\subseteq(0,8)\), the causal hull of the two associated kinematic points is

\[
0\le u\le2,\qquad 5\le v\le8.
\]

It contains, for example, \((1,6)\), corresponding to the interval \((1,6)\), because

\[
(2,5)\subseteq(1,6)\subseteq(0,8).
\]

It does not contain \((3,7)\): although \((3,7)\subseteq(0,8)\), it does not contain \((2,5)\), since its left endpoint lies to the right of \(2\).

The null sides have constant \(u\) or constant \(v\). Moving along one such side changes only one endpoint of the represented interval. This is why two intervals that share an endpoint are null-related in kinematic space.

### Incomparable endpoints

Now take

\[
I_a=(0,4),\qquad I_b=(2,6).
\]

Neither interval contains the other. Consequently, \(a\) and \(b\) are not causally comparable, and there is no order interval \([a,b]_{\preceq}\). Under the minimal order-convex definition,

\[
\operatorname{Hull}_{\preceq}\{a,b\}=\{a,b\}.
\]

This apparently sparse result is not a defect; it reveals what causal order can and cannot supply. Euclidean convexity fills a segment between every pair because vector-space interpolation exists. A partial order supplies “between” points only for comparable pairs.

One could instead define a different closure by adjoining common upper bounds, common lower bounds, their causal diamonds, or lattice joins and meets when those exist. For the two overlapping intervals above, their smallest containing interval is \((0,6)\), while their set-theoretic intersection is \((2,4)\). Adjoining those objects and then filling between them produces a nontrivial rectangle. But that is a **lattice-generated** or **bounding-diamond** hull, not the minimal order-convex hull. Causal order alone does not force the extra rule.

## 5. Why the diamond is Lorentzian rather than Euclidean

The kinematic construction equips the interval space with a metric of the schematic form

\[
ds_K^2
=
\frac{\partial^2 S(u,v)}
{\partial u\,\partial v}\,du\,dv,
\]

up to normalization and sign conventions. Its associated two-form is

\[
\omega
=
\frac{\partial^2 S(u,v)}
{\partial u\,\partial v}\,du\wedge dv.
\]

Czech and collaborators identify \(\omega\) as the Crofton form and interpret its infinitesimal cells in terms of conditional mutual information; in holographic geometries, integrating the Crofton form with the appropriate intersection number reconstructs lengths of bulk curves ([Czech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)). The causal order and the Crofton density are related but logically distinct: order specifies which directions are causal, while the density determines how regions are weighted.

For the vacuum of a \(1+1\)-dimensional conformal field theory on an infinite line, the regulated interval entropy has the form

\[
S(u,v)
=
\frac{c}{3}
\log\!\left(\frac{v-u}{\epsilon}\right),
\]

where \(c\) is the central charge and \(\epsilon\) is a short-distance cutoff. Hence

\[
\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial u\,\partial v}
=
\frac{c}{3(v-u)^2}.
\]

The density increases as the interval becomes short. The cutoff contributes an additive term to \(S\) but disappears from this mixed derivative.

On a circle, it is convenient to write

\[
u=\theta-\alpha,\qquad
v=\theta+\alpha,
\]

where \(\theta\) is the interval center and \(\alpha\) its half-width. Then

\[
du\,dv=d\theta^2-d\alpha^2.
\]

Thus changing scale \(\alpha\) while holding the center fixed is timelike, while changing the center at an appropriate rate gives null directions. For the vacuum on a circle, the resulting kinematic geometry is two-dimensional de Sitter space, as explicitly developed in [*Integral Geometry and Holography*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515).

This does not mean that increasing interval size is physical time evolution. It means that the inclusion hierarchy has a Lorentzian mathematical representation.

## 6. Information carried by a causal hull

The order hull answers a precise question:

> Which intervals are intermediate under inclusion between the selected intervals?

For comparable endpoints \(I_p\subseteq I_q\), every kinematic point in the hull represents a possible intermediate region \(I\) satisfying

\[
I_p\subseteq I\subseteq I_q.
\]

In quantum theory, the reduced state on a smaller region can be obtained from the reduced state on a larger region by tracing out degrees of freedom in the difference. Czech and collaborators accordingly interpret kinematic causal order as organizing such partial-trace relations ([Czech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

If the entropy function is sufficiently regular, integrating the Crofton density over a rectangular diamond gives an alternating entropy combination. For

\[
D=[u_0,u_1]\times[v_0,v_1],
\]

\[
\int_D
\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial u\,\partial v}\,du\,dv
=
S(u_1,v_1)-S(u_0,v_1)-S(u_1,v_0)+S(u_0,v_0),
\]

with orientation determining the overall sign convention. Suitable arrangements of adjacent intervals identify this finite difference with conditional mutual information. Its non-negativity follows from strong subadditivity, which is why the entropy Hessian can serve as a positive Crofton density in the standard setup. This correspondence is established for the kinematic construction in [Czech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515).

The distinction is essential:

- The **order hull** depends only on containment.
- Its **kinematic volume** depends on \(S(u,v)\).
- Its **bulk interpretation** additionally assumes an appropriate holographic dictionary and suitable geodesics.

Two states can possess the same interval-containment order but different entropy functions and hence different kinematic volumes. Order alone therefore cannot determine entanglement entropy.

## 7. What the construction does not establish

### It is not an affine convex hull

There is no expression analogous to

\[
\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y,\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1,
\]

unless kinematic space is separately given affine structure. The order hull is invariant under order-preserving relabelings that may drastically distort metric shapes.

### It does not determine distance

Knowing that \(p\preceq q\) identifies a causal relation but not a numerical separation. A conformal rescaling preserves the order while changing the metric scale. Malament’s reconstruction result supports precisely this distinction: under causal regularity assumptions, causal information fixes conformal rather than fully normalized metric geometry ([Malament](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/jmp/article-abstract/18/7/1399/460709/The-class-of-continuous-timelike-curves-determines)).

### It can be trivial for spacelike-separated seeds

Two incomparable points generate no between-points under minimal order convexity. A richer hull requires an additional choice, such as adjoining common bounds. Different choices produce different hull operators.

### Global topology matters

On a circle, an ordered pair of endpoints can describe an oriented interval and its complementary interval. One must specify orientation, allowed interval sizes, and identifications such as \((u,v)\sim(u+2\pi,v+2\pi)\). Near intervals covering the whole circle, a single rectangular coordinate chart may cease to represent the global geometry faithfully.

### Geodesic correspondence can fail to be one-to-one

The simplest kinematic dictionary assumes a controlled family of boundary-anchored geodesics. In geometries with multiple geodesics for the same endpoints, horizons, entanglement shadows, phase transitions between extremal surfaces, or conjugate points, “one interval, one geodesic” may fail or require branch labels. Czech and collaborators explicitly restrict strong reconstruction claims, noting the importance of the absence of conjugate points in the bulk reconstruction problem ([Czech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

### Higher dimensions are not a direct copy

A spatial interval in one dimension has two endpoints, giving a two-dimensional parameter space with a simple product order. Higher-dimensional boundary regions have shape degrees of freedom, and their extremal surfaces form a much larger space. Region inclusion still defines a partial order, but it need not yield a two-dimensional Lorentzian manifold or rectangular causal diamonds.

## 8. Resolvable exercises

### Exercise 1: test comparability

Let

\[
A=(1,4),\qquad B=(0,6),\qquad C=(3,7).
\]

Determine which pairs are comparable.

**Solution.** Since \(0\le1\) and \(4\le6\), \(A\subseteq B\), so \(A\preceq B\). Neither \(A\subseteq C\) nor \(C\subseteq A\), and neither \(B\subseteq C\) nor \(C\subseteq B\). Thus only \(A\) and \(B\) are comparable.

### Exercise 2: construct a two-point hull

For \(A=(1,4)\subseteq B=(0,6)\), describe the causal order interval.

**Solution.**

\[
[A,B]_{\preceq}
=
\{(u,v):0\le u\le1,\;4\le v\le6\}.
\]

It is a rectangle in null coordinates and hence a kinematic causal diamond.

### Exercise 3: locate the represented objects

Does the point \(X=(0.4,5)\) belong to that hull? What does it represent?

**Solution.** Yes, because \(0\le0.4\le1\) and \(4\le5\le6\). It represents the entire interval \((0.4,5)\), not a boundary location and not a bulk event.

### Exercise 4: negative control—order is not distance

Suppose two kinematic metrics are related by

\[
g'=\Omega^2(u,v)g
\]

for a nonconstant positive function \(\Omega\). Do they define the same causal hulls? Must they assign the same proper time or volume to those hulls?

**Solution.** They define the same causal cones, comparability relations, and order hulls because a positive conformal factor does not change causal directions. They generally assign different proper times and volumes. Therefore inferring metric distance or hull volume from causal order alone is invalid.

### Exercise 5: incomparable seeds

Let \(A=(0,4)\) and \(B=(2,6)\). Find their minimal order-convex hull. Then identify one additional rule that would produce a larger hull.

**Solution.** The intervals are incomparable, so their minimal order-convex hull is \(\{A,B\}\). A larger construction could first adjoin their intersection \((2,4)\) and smallest common containing interval \((0,6)\), then fill the order interval between those bounds. That result uses an extra lattice-style rule and is not forced by order convexity alone.

### Exercise 6: entropy density

For

\[
S(u,v)=\frac{c}{3}\log\!\left(\frac{v-u}{\epsilon}\right),
\]

compute the mixed derivative.

**Solution.**

\[
\partial_uS=-\frac{c}{3(v-u)},
\qquad
\partial_v\partial_uS
=
\frac{c}{3(v-u)^2}.
\]

The cutoff cancels from the derivative. This density supplies a measure on kinematic space, whereas the interval-containment order did not.

## 9. Conclusion

Causal order can define a convex hull in kinematic space, provided the phrase means **order-convex hull**. For comparable interval-points, the construction is exact and useful: it fills every intermediate interval under containment and produces a causal diamond bounded by null lines of fixed endpoint. It requires no metric and correctly preserves the distinction between causal comparability and quantitative separation.

The construction is not a universal replacement for ordinary convex geometry. Incomparable seeds need not generate any new points; alternative nontrivial hulls require extra closure axioms. Nor does the hull’s order structure determine its size. Entropy, the Crofton form, or another measure must be supplied to turn a set of kinematic points into a quantitatively weighted region. Finally, holographic interpretations add assumptions about minimal surfaces and bulk reconstruction that do not follow from the partial order itself.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore conditional but strong: **causal order defines a canonical minimal order-convex hull, and in two-dimensional interval kinematic space the hull of comparable points is a causal diamond. It does not, without further structure, define an affine, metric, or uniquely information-weighted convex hull.**
