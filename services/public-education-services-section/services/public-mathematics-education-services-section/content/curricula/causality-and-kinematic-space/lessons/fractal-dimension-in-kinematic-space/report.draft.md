# Fractal Dimension in Kinematic Space: What It Can—and Cannot—Measure

## Executive answer

The fractal dimension of a subset of kinematic space can characterize one important aspect of its geometric complexity: how the number or total cost of small-scale coverings grows as resolution improves. A non-integer dimension can distinguish a sparse, scale-repeating family of intervals or geodesics from a smooth curve or a two-dimensional region. Dimension can also be useful for comparing ensembles generated by different dynamical rules, provided that the same definition, metric, cutoff, and scaling range are used.

That answer requires several qualifications. Kinematic space is normally Lorentzian, whereas Hausdorff and box-counting dimensions require a genuine nonnegative distance. Lorentzian separation is not such a distance: distinct null-related points have zero interval, spacelike and timelike separations have different signs, and the usual triangle inequality does not hold. Consequently, “the fractal dimension in kinematic space” is not defined until one supplies a positive-definite auxiliary metric, a family of measuring cells, or an explicitly causal alternative. Causal order alone provides interval containment and light-cone structure, not lengths or resolutions.

Even after a measurement convention is chosen, one number is not a complete description of complexity. Sets with very different topology, lacunarity, directional structure, probability distributions, or information content may have the same dimension. In holographic applications, the Crofton measure derived from entropy gives additional physically meaningful weighting, but fractal dimension does not by itself equal entropy, bulk length, or the dimension of spacetime.

Most importantly, the foundational kinematic-space literature establishes the interval/geodesic construction, its Lorentzian causal structure, its Crofton measure, and its relationship to entanglement. It does **not** establish a general theorem that a fractal dimension of arbitrary kinematic subsets is a physical observable. The proposal is mathematically coherent when carefully defined, but its physical interpretation is presently conditional and model-dependent.

## 1. Prerequisites: order, Lorentzian geometry, and dimension

### 1.1 Causal order is not metric distance

A partial order is a relation \(\preceq\) satisfying:

1. reflexivity: \(x\preceq x\);
2. antisymmetry: \(x\preceq y\) and \(y\preceq x\) imply \(x=y\);
3. transitivity: \(x\preceq y\preceq z\) implies \(x\preceq z\).

In Lorentzian spacetime, causal precedence is modeled by whether one event can influence another along a future-directed causal curve. Two spacelike-separated events need not be comparable. Thus a causal order says which pairs are directionally related, but it assigns no numerical distance to either comparable or incomparable pairs.

A causal diamond between comparable events \(p\preceq q\) is the intersection

\[
J^+(p)\cap J^-(q),
\]

where \(J^+(p)\) is the causal future of \(p\) and \(J^-(q)\) is the causal past of \(q\). Its definition depends on causal reachability, not on a Euclidean radius.

This distinction is essential because a fractal dimension is a statement about scaling with size. Order by itself contains no unit of size. If all that is known is that \(A\subseteq B\subseteq C\), there is no basis for concluding that the “distance” from \(A\) to \(B\) is smaller than that from \(B\) to \(C\). Any such conclusion silently adds a metric or measure.

### 1.2 Lorentzian separation is also not an ordinary metric

In a local flat Lorentzian chart with coordinates \((t,x)\), the squared interval may be written

\[
ds^2=-dt^2+dx^2
\]

under the mostly-plus convention. Timelike, null, and spacelike displacements have \(ds^2<0\), \(ds^2=0\), and \(ds^2>0\), respectively. This structure supplies light cones and proper time, but \(\sqrt{|ds^2|}\) is not a metric-space distance: distinct null-separated points can have zero separation, and no single positive-definite triangle inequality captures all causal types.

A Hausdorff dimension therefore cannot be obtained by simply inserting the Lorentzian interval into the standard Hausdorff definition. One must first specify a positive-definite distance or another covering rule.

### 1.3 What a fractal dimension measures

Let \((X,d)\) be a metric space and \(E\subseteq X\). For \(s\geq0\) and scale \(\delta>0\), define the \(s\)-dimensional Hausdorff content at resolution \(\delta\) by

\[
\mathcal H^s_\delta(E)
=
\inf\left\{
\sum_i \bigl(\operatorname{diam}U_i\bigr)^s:
E\subseteq\bigcup_i U_i,\ 
\operatorname{diam}U_i\leq\delta
\right\}.
\]

Then

\[
\mathcal H^s(E)=\lim_{\delta\to0}\mathcal H^s_\delta(E),
\]

and the Hausdorff dimension is

\[
\dim_H E
=
\inf\{s:\mathcal H^s(E)=0\}
=
\sup\{s:\mathcal H^s(E)=\infty\}.
\]

Falconer’s treatment emphasizes this transition from infinite to zero Hausdorff measure and also notes that a set of dimension \(s\) need not have finite, nonzero \(s\)-dimensional measure; having dimension \(s\) is weaker than being an \(s\)-set ([Falconer, *The Geometry of Fractal Sets*, Chapter 1](https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~jswlamb/M345PA46/F85%20chap%201.pdf)).

A more computationally accessible quantity is box-counting dimension. If \(N_\epsilon(E)\) is the minimum number of cells of diameter at most \(\epsilon\) needed to cover \(E\), then

\[
\underline{\dim}_B E
=
\liminf_{\epsilon\to0}
\frac{\log N_\epsilon(E)}{\log(1/\epsilon)},
\qquad
\overline{\dim}_B E
=
\limsup_{\epsilon\to0}
\frac{\log N_\epsilon(E)}{\log(1/\epsilon)}.
\]

When these agree, their common value is \(\dim_B E\). Hausdorff and box dimensions need not agree, and the mathematical literature treats box, packing, and Hausdorff dimensions as distinct definitions with different properties ([Falconer, “Alternative Definitions of Dimension”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/0470013850.ch3)).

Dimension is therefore a scaling exponent, not a visual judgment that a set “looks jagged.”

## 2. What kinematic space is

### 2.1 Points that represent intervals or geodesics

Consider oriented boundary intervals on a one-dimensional spatial boundary. An interval can be labeled by its endpoints

\[
(u,v),\qquad u<v.
\]

The pair \((u,v)\) is a **single point** of a two-dimensional parameter space. It is often convenient to use center and half-width coordinates,

\[
\theta=\frac{u+v}{2},
\qquad
\alpha=\frac{v-u}{2},
\]

so that \(u=\theta-\alpha\) and \(v=\theta+\alpha\). Here \(\theta\) records location and \(\alpha\) records scale. The kinematic-space literature explicitly identifies these coordinates with interval position and half-width ([Czech, Lamprou, McCandlish, and Sully, “Tensor Networks from Kinematic Space”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01548)).

In the static \(\mathrm{AdS}_3/\mathrm{CFT}_2\) setting, the same point labels a boundary-anchored bulk geodesic. Thus kinematic space admits two readings:

\[
\text{point }(u,v)
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{boundary interval }[u,v]
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{bulk geodesic }\gamma_{u,v}.
\]

This equivalence is not universal. The interval description can be used information-theoretically, while the geodesic interpretation requires an appropriate holographic bulk and the relevant extremal-surface prescription. The foundational construction studies time-reflection-symmetric, asymptotically \(\mathrm{AdS}_3\) spatial slices and notes additional reconstruction conditions, including the absence of conjugate points in the class considered ([Czech et al., “Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

### 2.2 Containment becomes causality

If \(I_1=[u_1,v_1]\) and \(I_2=[u_2,v_2]\), then

\[
I_1\subseteq I_2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u_2\leq u_1\ \text{and}\ v_1\leq v_2.
\]

Changing one endpoint while holding the other fixed gives a null direction in the usual kinematic geometry. Changing both endpoints so that an interval grows or shrinks gives a timelike direction. Changing them in the same spatial direction while neither interval contains the other gives a spacelike direction.

Accordingly, the causal structure of kinematic space geometrizes interval inclusion. The original construction states that its partial ordering reflects containment and that, in information-theoretic language, a reduced state on a contained interval can be obtained from the larger interval’s state by partial trace ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)). This is a powerful encoding of comparability, but it still does not say how many meters—or even how many invariant “units”—separate two kinematic points.

### 2.3 The metric and Crofton form

Let \(S(u,v)\) denote the entropy assigned to the interval \([u,v]\). Up to convention-dependent constants and signs, the kinematic two-form is

\[
\omega
=
\frac{\partial^2 S(u,v)}{\partial u\,\partial v}\,
du\wedge dv,
\]

and the associated mixed-signature line element has the schematic form

\[
ds_K^2
\propto
\frac{\partial^2 S(u,v)}{\partial u\,\partial v}\,du\,dv.
\]

The precise normalization must be fixed for the application; it should not be inferred from causal order alone. In the holographic construction, \(\omega\) is an exact Crofton form providing a measure on the space of geodesics, while finite kinematic rectangles encode conditional mutual information ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)). Positivity is related to strong subadditivity, although degeneracy can occur when the relevant conditional mutual information is saturated.

For the vacuum of a two-dimensional CFT on a line,

\[
S(u,v)
=
\frac{c}{3}\log\frac{v-u}{a},
\]

where \(c\) is the central charge and \(a\) is a UV cutoff. The equivalent short-interval result \(S_A=(c/3)\log(l/a)\) is reproduced by a regulated geodesic in Poincaré \(\mathrm{AdS}_3\) ([Ryu and Takayanagi, “Holographic Derivation of Entanglement Entropy from AdS/CFT”](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603001)). Differentiation gives

\[
\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial u\,\partial v}
=
\frac{c}{3(v-u)^2}.
\]

Thus, apart from overall convention,

\[
ds_K^2\propto\frac{du\,dv}{(v-u)^2}.
\]

For a circular boundary, the entropy instead contains the finite-size factor

\[
S(l)=\frac{c}{3}
\log\left[
\frac{L}{\pi a}
\sin\left(\frac{\pi l}{L}\right)
\right],
\]

with interval length \(l\), circumference \(L\), and cutoff \(a\); Ryu and Takayanagi recover this expression from the regulated bulk geodesic length ([Ryu and Takayanagi](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603001)). In that vacuum example, the resulting two-dimensional kinematic geometry is de Sitter space, as stated and developed in the integral-geometry construction ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

## 3. Defining a fractal dimension on this space

### 3.1 The auxiliary-metric route

The cleanest mathematical procedure is:

1. choose a kinematic domain \(K\), such as \(u<v\);
2. choose a positive-definite metric \(h\) on \(K\);
3. regard the target family \(F\) of intervals or geodesics as a subset of \((K,h)\);
4. compute \(\dim_H^h F\) or a box dimension defined from \(h\).

In one coordinate chart, a simple choice is

\[
d_E\bigl((u,v),(u',v')\bigr)
=
\sqrt{(u-u')^2+(v-v')^2}.
\]

A more geometrically adapted choice can be obtained by turning the signs of the Lorentzian metric into a Riemannian metric in a specified frame. That conversion is additional structure; it is not canonical merely because the Lorentzian metric exists.

On compact regions away from coordinate singularities and degeneracies, smooth positive-definite metrics are locally comparable by finite multiplicative bounds. The identity map is then locally bi-Lipschitz, so Hausdorff dimension is unchanged. This makes the dimension reasonably robust under ordinary smooth coordinate changes and reasonable auxiliary metrics on a well-behaved compact patch.

That robustness fails near singular boundaries or under transformations that are not bi-Lipschitz. For example, the boundary \(v-u=0\) corresponds to vanishing interval size, while the vacuum Crofton density diverges as \((v-u)^{-2}\). A coordinate grid that treats all endpoint increments equally and a Crofton-adapted grid need not define the same finite-resolution counting problem near that boundary.

### 3.2 A causal-cover route

One might instead cover a subset by causal diamonds and use a positive size assigned to each diamond. But causal order alone supplies only the diamond’s membership. Its size still requires something such as:

- coordinate side lengths;
- Crofton volume;
- an entropy increment;
- the number of elements in a discrete causal model; or
- a cutoff-dependent lattice depth.

A “causal fractal dimension” can therefore be defined, but it is always dimension relative to the selected size functional. Calling it order-theoretic without naming that added structure would be misleading.

### 3.3 Crofton-weighted scaling

A particularly relevant holographic proposal is to use the positive Crofton density

\[
d\mu_K
=
\left|
\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial u\,\partial v}
\right|du\,dv
\]

where it is nondegenerate, and ask how the measure of an \(\epsilon\)-neighborhood of \(F\) scales:

\[
\mu_K(F_\epsilon)\sim C\epsilon^{D_{\mathrm{amb}}-D}.
\]

Here \(D_{\mathrm{amb}}\) is the dimension of the ambient measuring geometry and \(D\) is a Minkowski-type dimension. This proposal incorporates the entropy-derived weighting rather than treating all coordinate cells as equal.

It still requires a positive-definite notion of the neighborhood \(F_\epsilon\), and it may fail to produce a single exponent if the scaling oscillates or crosses between regimes. Moreover, Crofton measure has a direct holographic interpretation only under the assumptions of the kinematic construction. It should not automatically be read as a universal probability measure or microscopic count.

## 4. Worked examples

### 4.1 Smooth families: useful calibration controls

Fix the interval center:

\[
F_{\theta_0}=\{(\theta,\alpha):\theta=\theta_0,\ \alpha_0\leq\alpha\leq\alpha_1\}.
\]

This is a smooth one-parameter curve, so its Hausdorff and box dimensions are ordinarily \(1\). It represents all intervals centered at \(\theta_0\) over a range of scales.

A two-parameter open family,

\[
R=\{(\theta,\alpha):\theta_0<\theta<\theta_1,\ 
\alpha_0<\alpha<\alpha_1\},
\]

has dimension \(2\). These are calibration cases: a proposed estimator that does not return approximately \(1\) and \(2\), respectively, under controlled sampling should not be trusted on more intricate data.

### 4.2 A genuinely fractal interval family

Let \(C\subset[0,1]\) be the middle-thirds Cantor set. At construction depth \(n\), it consists of \(2^n\) intervals of length \(3^{-n}\), giving the similarity exponent

\[
d_C=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}.
\]

Now define a kinematic subset by choosing one endpoint from each of two separated Cantor copies:

\[
F=\{(u,v):u\in C/3,\quad v\in 2/3+C/3\}.
\]

The separation guarantees \(u<v\), so every pair represents a valid interval. With the Euclidean endpoint metric, this set is a product of two scaled Cantor sets. At scale \(3^{-n}\), approximately \(2^n\) choices are needed for each endpoint, hence about \(4^n\) cells cover \(F\). Therefore

\[
\dim F
=
\frac{\log 4}{\log 3}
=
2\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}
\approx1.26186.
\]

This value has a clear interpretation: the interval family is more complex than a smooth one-parameter curve but does not fill a two-dimensional kinematic region. It does **not** mean that any individual boundary interval or bulk geodesic has dimension \(1.26186\). The fractal object is the *set of geodesics or intervals represented by the selected kinematic points*.

### 4.3 Why the same dimension is not the same geometry

Two subsets may both have dimension \(1.26186\) while differing in:

- which scales contain gaps;
- how gaps are distributed;
- whether the set is connected;
- whether it is preferentially aligned with null directions;
- its Crofton measure;
- the probability assigned to its points; and
- which bulk locations its geodesics intersect.

Fractal dimension captures an asymptotic rate, not these additional structures. Lacunarity can describe gap texture; multifractal spectra can describe nonuniform measures; directional dimensions can reveal anisotropy; homology or connectivity can capture topology; and Crofton integrals can connect a geodesic family to bulk lengths.

## 5. Relation to information and bulk geometry

The Ryu–Takayanagi proposal assigns

\[
S_A=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\gamma_A)}
{4G_N}
\]

to a boundary region \(A\) in a static holographic setting, with \(\gamma_A\) the corresponding bulk minimal surface ([Ryu and Takayanagi](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603001)). In \(\mathrm{AdS}_3\), the surface is a geodesic. Kinematic space then packages entropies of many intervals into a geometry whose volume form records second entropy variations and whose causal order records inclusion ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

This yields several possible uses for fractal analysis:

- A fractal subset may describe a scale-invariant selection of boundary intervals.
- Its image under the interval–geodesic correspondence may be a scale-invariant family of bulk probes.
- A dimension varying with state, temperature, or cutoff may diagnose how an interval ensemble occupies kinematic space.
- A Crofton-weighted multifractal analysis might distinguish regions where entropy variations are concentrated from regions that are merely dense in coordinate space.

These are plausible research programs, not consequences of the established entropy formula. In particular,

\[
\dim_H F \neq S(F),
\qquad
\dim_H F \neq \text{bulk length},
\]

in general. Bulk curve length is recovered through an appropriate Crofton integral over geodesics intersecting the curve, not by taking the dimension of that geodesic set; the integral-geometric construction explicitly distinguishes kinematic volumes from the causal ordering of intervals ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

Likewise, the fact that tensor networks can resemble a discretization of kinematic space does not make a fitted network dimension automatically universal. Czech and collaborators argue that MERA is naturally viewed as discretizing the space of intervals or bulk geodesics rather than the bulk spatial slice itself, and they caution that the geodesic interpretation belongs to holographic cases while the interval interpretation can also be used for non-holographic CFTs ([“Tensor Networks from Kinematic Space”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01548)).

## 6. Limitations and failure cases

### Metric dependence

A dimension estimate is incomplete unless it states the positive-definite metric, cell system, or measure used. Lorentzian causal structure alone is insufficient.

### Cutoff dependence

Physical and numerical data provide only a finite interval of scales. A straight segment on a log–log plot is evidence for an effective scaling regime, not proof of an asymptotic fractal. UV cutoffs are especially significant here: the CFT interval entropy explicitly depends on a short-distance regulator \(a\), although differentiating may remove additive cutoff terms in idealized formulas ([Ryu and Takayanagi](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603001)).

### Sampling bias

A cloud sampled nonuniformly from a smooth curve can mimic anomalous scaling. Conversely, sparse sampling of a true fractal can make it look zero-dimensional. Confidence intervals should reflect sample size, noise, boundary effects, and uncertainty in the fitted scale range.

### Degenerate information geometry

Where conditional mutual information vanishes, the entropy-derived density can degenerate. Standard metric-fractal arguments cannot simply be transferred across such loci without specifying how zero-density regions are handled; the kinematic-space paper itself notes the link between nondegeneracy and nonsaturation of strong subadditivity ([“Integral Geometry and Holography”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05515)).

### Restricted holographic scope

The best-developed correspondence concerns boundary intervals and geodesics in static \(\mathrm{AdS}_3/\mathrm{CFT}_2\)-type settings. Higher dimensions replace geodesics by higher-dimensional extremal surfaces, increasing the dimension and complexity of the relevant parameter space. Time dependence also requires a covariant treatment. Results proved for the static two-dimensional kinematic space cannot be assumed to hold unchanged.

### No evidence for a universal observable

The sources inspected establish kinematic geometry and standard notions of fractal dimension, but they do not report a universal “fractal dimension of kinematic space” that characterizes all quantum states or spacetimes. The defensible conclusion is narrower: dimension is a viable statistic of a *specified subset under a specified measuring structure*.

## 7. A rigorous workflow for an empirical study

A reproducible analysis should report:

1. **Objects:** What does each point represent—an interval, an oriented geodesic, an extremal surface, or a network element?
2. **Domain:** Which coordinate patch and which ranges of position and scale are included?
3. **Subset rule:** How is \(F\) selected? Is it deterministic, thresholded, dynamical, or sampled?
4. **Distance:** What positive-definite metric defines diameter and neighborhoods?
5. **Weight:** Is counting uniform, probability-weighted, or Crofton-weighted?
6. **Estimator:** Hausdorff bounds, box counting, correlation dimension, or a multifractal spectrum?
7. **Scaling window:** Over which values of \(\epsilon\) is the exponent fitted?
8. **Controls:** Does the method recover dimensions \(1\) and \(2\) for smooth calibration sets?
9. **Robustness:** Does the estimate persist under resolution changes, coordinate reparameterizations, and reasonable auxiliary metrics?
10. **Interpretation:** Is the result only geometric, or is a separate theorem supplied that connects it to entropy or a bulk observable?

For box counting, one may fit

\[
\log N_\epsilon(F)
=
D\log(1/\epsilon)+b
\]

over a declared range. Curvature in the residuals, substantial variation under shifting the grid, or disagreement between upper and lower effective slopes indicates that a single limiting dimension has not been demonstrated.

## 8. Exercises with resolutions

### Exercise 1: order is not distance

Let

\[
A=[0,10],\quad B=[2,8],\quad C=[3,7].
\]

The containment order gives \(C\subset B\subset A\). Can one infer from this alone that the distance from \(C\) to \(B\) is smaller than the distance from \(B\) to \(A\)?

**Resolution:** No. Containment establishes causal comparability in interval kinematic space but supplies no numerical distance. With the Euclidean endpoint metric, the distances are \(\sqrt2\) and \(2\sqrt2\); after a nonlinear or differently weighted positive-definite metric is chosen, their ratio can change. The invalid inference is treating order as though it already contained metric scale.

### Exercise 2: causal relationships

For \(I_1=[1,4]\), \(I_2=[0,5]\), and \(I_3=[2,6]\), determine the containment relations.

**Resolution:** \(I_1\subset I_2\), so their kinematic points are causally comparable. Neither \(I_1\subset I_3\) nor \(I_3\subset I_1\); they overlap but are not nested, so they are spacelike-related in the interval-containment interpretation.

### Exercise 3: construct a kinematic point

Map \(I=[2,8]\) into center–half-width coordinates.

**Resolution:**

\[
\theta=\frac{2+8}{2}=5,
\qquad
\alpha=\frac{8-2}{2}=3.
\]

The entire interval becomes the single kinematic point \((5,3)\).

### Exercise 4: calculate a fractal dimension

At construction depth \(n\), a kinematic subset requires \(4^n\) cells of side \(3^{-n}\). Find its box dimension.

**Resolution:**

\[
D
=
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{\log 4^n}{\log 3^n}
=
\frac{\log4}{\log3}
\approx1.26186.
\]

### Exercise 5: distinguish dimension from information

If two kinematic subsets have the same box dimension, must they have the same Crofton volume or encode the same conditional mutual information?

**Resolution:** No. Box dimension records a covering exponent. Crofton volume integrates an entropy-derived density, so it depends on location and weight as well as occupancy. Equal dimension does not imply equal measure, entropy data, topology, or physical interpretation.

## Conclusion

Fractal dimension can characterize the geometric complexity of a subset in kinematic space, but only in a precise and limited sense. It measures the subset’s small-scale covering behavior once a positive-definite distance or explicit measuring convention has been supplied. It can distinguish smooth one-parameter families, area-filling collections, and intermediate fractal ensembles of intervals or geodesics.

It cannot be extracted from causal order alone, because containment supplies comparability rather than scale. Nor can the Lorentzian interval be used uncritically as a metric-space distance. A careful study must name its auxiliary metric, cutoff, estimator, and scaling range and must test robustness against coordinate and sampling choices.

In holographic examples, the Crofton form offers a natural entropy-derived measure, making weighted fractal or multifractal analysis an interesting possibility. Yet the established correspondence connects entropy to extremal-surface area and bulk lengths to Crofton integrals—not to fractal dimension by itself. The strongest evidence-based conclusion is therefore conditional: fractal dimension is a useful descriptor of selected kinematic subsets, potentially revealing scale-invariant organization, but it is neither a complete complexity measure nor an established universal observable of spacetime or quantum information.
