# Worked constructions, failure cases, and open questions

## Teaching question

How do we test a proposed kinematic-space construction, recognize when its
coordinates or reconstruction fail, and distinguish an open research question
from an analogy that has lost its hypotheses?

## Construction assay

A usable construction should name at least five things: the objects represented
as kinematic points; the domain and identifications of their coordinates; the
relations, metric, or measure placed on that domain; the observables from which
those structures are derived; and the theorem or prescription connecting them
back to the original system. Removing one item creates a specific uncertainty,
not permission to fill the gap with visual resemblance.

Czech and collaborators test the interval/geodesic construction through
integral geometry, OPE blocks, and tensor-network structures. The results show
several different reconstruction channels: Crofton integrals recover selected
bulk geometric quantities; OPE blocks behave as fields on kinematic space in
the relevant CFT treatment; and MERA can discretize structural features of the
space of geodesics. These channels share a kinematic organization but do not
have identical inputs, approximations, or conclusions.

Asplund, Callebaut, and Zukowski compare vacuum, thermal, and nontrivial
three-dimensional gravity cases. Thermal quotients, entanglement plateaux, and
phase changes illustrate how a smooth vacuum picture can require extra
branches or lose access to some bulk information. “Kinematic space exists” is
therefore not the end of an assay: one must ask which regions and observables
are represented faithfully and which reconstruction problem remains
underdetermined.

## Three failure modes

1. **Coordinate failure.** Endpoint coordinates are used without specifying
   orientation, periodic lifts, or quotient identification. Distinct intervals
   are collapsed or one interval is counted twice.
2. **Branch failure.** Multiple geodesics or entropy phases share boundary
   labels. Choosing only the minimal branch discards information needed for a
   proposed reconstruction.
3. **Category failure.** A discretization of the space of geodesics is read as
   a literal lattice of bulk points. Incidence and adjacency are confused.

## Open-question discipline

An open question should state a missing map or failed test. Examples include:
which additional observables resolve a nonminimal branch; which classes of
states admit a sufficiently smooth kinematic description; how quantum and
finite-central-charge corrections deform the entropy-derived measure; or how
much bulk geometry is reconstructible from restricted interval data. These are
research directions, not conclusions supplied by the cited leading-order
constructions.

## Negative control

Invalid inference: “the vacuum example produces a de Sitter metric, so every
dataset of pairwise relations has a hidden de Sitter geometry.” A coordinate
manifold, differentiable entropy function, positivity properties, state, and
duality hypotheses all do real work in the established result.

## Cinematic direction

Build a five-stage assay as a mechanical instrument. Feed it the vacuum
interval construction and let every stage lock into place. Then feed it a
quotient or phase-transition example: split branches, expose missing regions,
and show which outputs remain warranted. Finally feed it an arbitrary relation
dataset; stop at the first absent hypothesis rather than drawing a decorative
de Sitter surface. End with explicit open questions attached to missing maps.
