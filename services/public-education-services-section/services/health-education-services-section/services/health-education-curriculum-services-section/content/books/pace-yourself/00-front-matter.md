## Who this book is for

This book is for the reader who has already read *Understanding
Infection-Associated Chronic Illness* -- or who does not need to, because
they already live it. You know what post-exertional malaise feels like.
You have already had the conversation where a test came back normal and
nobody could say why you still can't get through a day. That first book
gave you the map: what is established, what is a well-supported
hypothesis, what is genuinely contested. This book gives you the
practice built on that map -- four verbs, run daily, for staying inside
the energy you actually have.

## The word PACE

"PACE" was the name of a 2011 UK clinical trial of graded exercise
therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic fatigue syndrome.
Its reported recovery rates shaped clinical guidance for a decade. It
also had real methodological problems, documented in the published
record, not argued here as advocacy: outcome measures changed partway
through the trial, the thresholds for counting a participant "recovered"
overlapped with the thresholds for enrolling them, and the trial never
measured post-exertional malaise, the symptom this book is built around.
In 2021 the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence set
aside its old graded-exercise recommendation for myalgic
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome and named pacing instead
[@nice2021ng206].

This book takes the same four letters back for the opposite idea. PACE
Yourself is not that trial, and it is not a graded, scheduled increase in
activity regardless of how you feel. It is pacing -- the strategy the
PACE trial's approach ignored, built on staying inside the energy you
actually have rather than pushing through to expand it.

## How the four verbs work

**Pause** means stopping before the limit, not at it. Post-exertional
malaise arrives twelve to seventy-two hours after the exertion that
triggers it, so waiting to feel the edge means you have already crossed
it; Chapter 1 shows you how to set a ceiling from your own history instead.

**Attune** means reading your own baseline, never a population
threshold. Chapter 2 shows you how to notice when your heart rate,
sleep, or symptoms drift from what is ordinary for you, and how to treat
every rule of thumb -- including the heart-rate heuristics already in
circulation -- as a heuristic, never a diagnosis.

**Care** means relation-repair, never extraction: no upsell, no guilt,
no streak punishment, no diagnosis sold as certainty. Chapter 3 covers
the practical work inside your envelope -- orthostatic management, sleep,
hydration, sensory load -- each named honestly by what its evidence
actually supports.

**Engage** means re-entering activity on your body's terms, never a
calendar's. Chapter 4 shows you how to advance only once you have fully
recovered from your last exertion, the mechanistic opposite of a graded,
scheduled increase.

## How this book is organized

Chapter 1, Pause, covers why the felt limit arrives too late to be
useful and how to set your ceiling ahead of it. Chapter 2, Attune, covers
reading your own baseline across your history and the honest evidence
state of the biometric tools available to help you do it. Chapter 3,
Care, covers the daily practices that repair rather than extract inside
the envelope Chapters 1 and 2 help you find. Chapter 4, Engage, covers
re-entering activity on symptom-contingent terms and why that differs
from graded exercise. Chapter 5, The Loop, brings the four verbs
together into the shape of a day -- and of the second day, when the
loop is audited -- and how to re-estimate your envelope weekly.

## A note on honesty

The controlled evidence for pacing itself is still limited. As Chapter 4
of *Understanding Infection-Associated Chronic Illness* lays out, most of
what supports pacing is patient experience, mechanistic reasoning, and
observational data, not the large randomized trials that would say with
confidence how much it helps or for whom. This book teaches pacing
anyway, for reasons stated plainly: the mechanistic case is sound,
patient experience across decades points the same direction, and the
documented harm of the opposite approach -- pushing through regardless
of symptoms -- is better evidenced than pacing's benefit is. Where a
step here rests on a single small study or an unvalidated heuristic
rather than an established finding, it says so, the same way the first
book did.
