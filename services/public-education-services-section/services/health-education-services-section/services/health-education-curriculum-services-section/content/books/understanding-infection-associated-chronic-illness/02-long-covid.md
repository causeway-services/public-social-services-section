# Chapter 2: Long COVID — Definitions and Why They Disagree

## The definition problem, plainly stated

If you read three different reports about how common long COVID is, or what the typical symptoms are, you may see three different numbers. This is not because the researchers disagree about the facts — it's because they're measuring different populations using different definitions. This sounds like a technical detail. It's not. It changes what any single study can actually tell you.

Here is the central fact that shapes everything else in this chapter: long COVID is not one thing measured three different ways. It is three different case definitions, each built for a different purpose, producing three different populations [@soriano2022; @thaweethai2023; @nasem2024longcovid]. A finding from one is not automatically a finding about the other two.

## Three definitions, three purposes

### The WHO clinical case definition (2021–2022)

In 2021 and 2022, the World Health Organization convened a Delphi consensus — a structured expert process where 265 participants (patients, clinicians, researchers, and WHO staff) evaluated 14 different domains across two rounds and converged on a shared definition [@soriano2022]. The WHO's definition is built for clinical diagnosis. If you walk into a doctor's office saying you've had COVID and you're still sick, this is what that doctor would use to figure out whether you have long COVID:

You have probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection; your symptoms started usually within three months of that infection; the symptoms have lasted at least two months; and they cannot be explained by an alternative diagnosis (a thyroid problem, depression, heart disease — something else that your doctor can identify and might explain your symptoms instead). The symptoms that matter most are fatigue, shortness of breath, and cognitive problems like brain fog — but you may have others. The symptoms generally impact your everyday functioning and may be new symptoms that appeared after you got better from the acute COVID-19 illness, or they may be symptoms that just never went away [@soriano2022].

This definition is designed for diagnosis in a clinic and for comparing populations across countries. It is a consensus artifact — what the field agreed to call the condition — not a claim about what causes it.

### The RECOVER research definition (2023)

The NIH RECOVER initiative took a different approach. Rather than starting with expert consensus, they started with data. They recruited 9,764 people — 8,646 who had been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and 1,118 who had not — and asked both groups about 44 different symptoms at six months or more after infection. Then they used statistical methods to figure out which symptoms actually separated the infected group from the uninfected group. From that analysis, they ended up with 12 symptoms that had the most power to distinguish the two groups [@thaweethai2023]:

Post-exertional malaise (getting worse after activity), fatigue, brain fog, dizziness, gastrointestinal symptoms, heart palpitations, changes in sexual desire or capacity, loss of or change in smell or taste, thirst, chronic cough, chest pain, and abnormal movements [@thaweethai2023].

The RECOVER definition is not a diagnosis — it is a research instrument, a weighted composite score that researchers use to make sure they're studying the same population across multiple studies. It exists to give researchers a consistent outcome variable, a number they can measure and track. In the most methodologically rigorous subgroup RECOVER studied — participants first infected on or after December 2021 and enrolled within 30 days of infection — 10% were classified as having long COVID by this score at six months [@thaweethai2023].

### The NASEM definition (2024)

In June 2024, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine issued a definition for use by health systems and health policy [@nasem2024longcovid]. This definition is deliberately broader. According to NASEM, long COVID is an infection-associated chronic condition that appears after SARS-CoV-2 infection and is present for at least three months as a continuous, relapsing-and-remitting (coming and going), or progressive disease state affecting one or more organ systems. It can present as a single symptom or as multiple diagnosable conditions — interstitial lung disease, heart arrhythmias, POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome), ME/CFS (myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome), diabetes, autoimmune disorders, and others.

The NASEM definition widens the frame from "a symptom cluster" to "an umbrella disease state." It folds in entities like ME/CFS and POTS that have their own entire research histories and their own case-definition debates. This changes what any prevalence or mechanism study can be read to claim about "long COVID" as a whole.

## Why these definitions matter

The three definitions differ in timing, scope, and evidence base. The WHO definition requires symptoms to appear within about three months of infection and last at least two months — roughly a five-month window, and it is a consensus about what clinicians agree to call the condition [@soriano2022]. The RECOVER definition assesses symptoms at six months or later post-infection with no separate timing requirement, and it is data-derived, showing which symptoms actually distinguish infected from uninfected people [@thaweethai2023]. The NASEM definition requires three months duration with no upper bound, and it explicitly allows for the condition to improve, worsen, or come and go over time, rather than requiring a fixed course [@nasem2024longcovid].

Because these three instruments select different populations, findings produced by one study cannot be pooled with findings from another without that study stating which definition it used. This is the same validity problem that has been documented for myalgic encephalomyelitis for years — different case definitions of ME/CFS (Fukuda, Canadian Consensus, and the 2015 Institute of Medicine criteria) select measurably different populations, and a finding true for one is not automatically true for the other [@iom2015]. Long COVID inherited this same structural problem.

## Prevalence: why the numbers disagree, and what the real uncertainty is

When you see a statistic about how many people have long COVID, stop and ask: what population was it measuring, and using which definition?

### The CDC Household Pulse Survey: 6–7% of US adults

The CDC periodically asks a representative sample of noninstitutionalized US adults "Have you ever been told you have long COVID?" and "Do you currently have long COVID?" This is a self-report survey with no clinical confirmation behind the answer. In June 2022, about 7.5% of US adults said they currently had long COVID. By June 2023, that figure had dropped to 6%, and among adults who had ever had COVID-19, the figure fell from 18.9% to 11.0% over the same interval [@cdc2023pulse]. A March 2024 wave put it at roughly 7% again — about 17 million adults [@cdcpulsetracker2024]. This number tells you what people self-report, which is useful for understanding how common people perceive the condition to be, but it does not tell you how many people actually meet a verified clinical case definition. It is a tracking trend, not a clinical case count.

### The Global Burden of Disease modeling: 144.7 million people, with massive uncertainty

An international collaboration looked at long COVID across ten cohort studies in ten countries and modeled global prevalence. They organized the condition around three symptom clusters (fatigue, cognitive problems, and respiratory symptoms) and estimated that 144.7 million people experienced at least one cluster in 2020–2021 [@wulfhanson2022]. But here is the critical number that most discussions skip: the 95% uncertainty interval was 54.8 million to 312.9 million. That is a near-6-fold spread. The wide interval reflects real uncertainty in the data — different studies used different methods, different populations, different definitions. The finding is not "144.7 million people have long COVID." The finding is "we don't actually know the number, but we know it is large, and the true figure could be anywhere from one-third to more than two times the middle estimate."

### The meta-analysis: 45% of survivors symptomatic at four months

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled studies of people who had COVID-19 and looked at what fraction had unresolved symptoms at approximately four months after infection. The pooled answer was 45% — nearly half of survivors had at least one lingering symptom [@omahoney2023]. But this figure drew published criticism in the same journal for pooling across inconsistent symptom-duration thresholds and including many studies with no comparison group of uninfected people. Some of the included studies used a very loose threshold (any lingering symptom at any point), while others required more sustained symptom persistence. The 45% is real within the pool of studies that met the inclusion criteria, but it may be inflated compared to a stricter, more consistent definition.

### What these numbers actually tell you

Read together, these three approaches give you different answers to different questions. The CDC survey asks "What do people think they have?" and the answer is "6–7% self-report it" [@cdc2023pulse]. The global modeling asks "If we tried to quantify the scope of the problem across all countries," and the answer is "somewhere between 55 million and 313 million, with a central estimate of 145 million, but we're genuinely uncertain" [@wulfhanson2022]. The meta-analysis asks "What fraction of COVID survivors have some lingering symptom at some point in the first four months?" and the answer is "45%, but only if we pool across studies that used different thresholds" [@omahoney2023]. None of these is wrong, and none is directly comparable to the other two.

This is not a flaw in the researchers. This is the cost of the definitional heterogeneity. Until the field converges on a single agreed case definition that researchers use consistently — the way they did for, say, diabetes or heart disease — any claim about prevalence will come with this caveat: "depends on which definition, and the definitions are still evolving."

## The symptom clusters: what people actually experience

Across the three definitional frameworks, certain symptom domains appear consistently. These clusters matter because they are what people who have long COVID actually live with. This is not the mechanism — we do not yet know why these symptoms occur — but this is the phenomenology, the actual experience [@davis2023].

### Fatigue and post-exertional malaise

The symptom that appears in nearly every definition is fatigue that is not relieved by rest [@thaweethai2023]. People with long COVID describe an exhaustion that improves with sleep or resting but returns quickly, not a tiredness that gets better with downtime the way ordinary fatigue does. For a substantial fraction of people, the fatigue is accompanied by post-exertional malaise: when you undertake a physical activity, a cognitive task, or sometimes even an emotional exertion (like stress or worry), your symptoms get worse, often in a disproportionate way and often delayed — not right away, but 12 to 72 hours later. The worsening can last days to weeks. This is distinct from ordinary muscle soreness (which goes away in a day or two) and distinct from deconditioning (where a task produces immediate fatigue proportional to the effort). It is one of the most disabling features of long COVID for many people.

### Brain fog and cognitive dysfunction

Memory problems, difficulty concentrating, word-finding difficulties, and a general sense of mental cloudiness — described by patients as "brain fog" — appear across the WHO, RECOVER, and NASEM frameworks [@soriano2022; @thaweethai2023; @nasem2024longcovid]. Some people describe it as feeling like their thinking moves at half speed or their attention is foggy. Others report significant memory loss or persistent difficulty retrieving information they know they know. The severity ranges from noticeable but manageable to profoundly disabling.

### Respiratory symptoms

Shortness of breath on exertion that is not explained by deconditioning, and chronic cough, appear consistently across definitions and cohorts [@wulfhanson2022; @thaweethai2023]. Some people experience dyspnea (difficulty breathing) even at rest or with minimal activity. Others notice the breathlessness only on exertion but find it more severe than it was before COVID.

### Cardiovascular and autonomic symptoms

Heart palpitations, a sensation of the heart racing or skipping, appear in the RECOVER research definition [@thaweethai2023]. A subset of people develop or worsen postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), a condition where your heart rate increases excessively when you stand up, and where you may feel dizzy, light-headed, or like you might pass out on standing. A prospective study of highly symptomatic long COVID clinic patients found that 31% met formal POTS criteria on standardized testing, with a further 27% having orthostatic symptoms without meeting full diagnostic thresholds [@bjornson2025]. The broader category of autonomic symptoms — symptoms related to the nervous system that controls heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, and other automatic functions — appears in a majority of people with long COVID who have been carefully evaluated in that same study.

### Gastrointestinal symptoms

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and abdominal pain appear in both the RECOVER research definition and in clinical cohorts [@thaweethai2023; @davis2023]. Some people develop new food intolerances or changes in appetite.

### Sensory changes

Loss of or change in sense of smell or taste was common early in the pandemic and appears in the RECOVER definition [@thaweethai2023]. Some people recover these senses; others report persistent dysfunction months or years after infection. In addition, some people report sensory changes like tingling, numbness, or pins-and-needles sensations [@davis2023].

## Symptom clusters, not one symptom in everyone

No single symptom is present in all people with long COVID. Some people have prominent fatigue and cognitive problems but minimal respiratory symptoms. Others have severe autonomic dysfunction and respiratory issues but less cognitive involvement. Some have profound post-exertional malaise; others have fatigue without the characteristic delay and worsening that defines post-exertional malaise. The global modeling work organized the condition around three clusters specifically — fatigue, cognitive problems, and respiratory symptoms — because those three had the strongest evidence across multiple studies, but this is not an exhaustive or universal list [@wulfhanson2022].

The NASEM definition acknowledges this explicitly: long COVID can present as a single symptom or as multiple diagnosable conditions [@nasem2024longcovid]. This heterogeneity is real. It means that if you have long COVID, your specific symptoms may not match someone else's profile, and that does not mean one of you does not actually have the condition. You both do; you just experience different aspects of it.

## What this means if you're reading this

If you or someone you care about has had COVID-19 and is still experiencing symptoms, you now know three important things:

First, if a study says "45% of people have this" or "long COVID affects 7% of adults," you know to ask which definition they used and what population they measured. The same honest answer is usually "different definitions give different answers, and that's because the definitions are measuring different things for different purposes."

Second, you know that fatigue, post-exertional malaise, cognitive problems, shortness of breath, heart symptoms, and autonomic symptoms are real and documented, not rare edge cases or people being exhausted from anxiety or depression (though anxiety and depression are common alongside long COVID and are their own separate concern).

Third, you know that there is no single symptom profile for long COVID. If your symptoms do not match what a friend with long COVID experiences, that does not mean yours are not real or not long COVID. The condition is not one thing — it is a heterogeneous cluster of post-viral conditions that share a trigger (SARS-CoV-2 infection) and overlapping symptom domains.

The mechanisms — why these symptoms occur, what is happening in the body to cause them — are the subject of the next chapter. Here, the takeaway is simpler: long COVID is real, the symptoms are well-documented, the definitions are still evolving to be more precise, and the prevalence numbers disagree because the definitions disagree, not because researchers cannot count.
