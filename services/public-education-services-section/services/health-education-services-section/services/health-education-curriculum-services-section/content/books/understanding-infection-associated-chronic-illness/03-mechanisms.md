# Chapter 3: What Researchers Think Is Happening

When you recover from an infection, your immune system's job is to clear the virus and then stand down — return to baseline, stop the inflammation, let your body rebuild. That usually works. Occasionally it doesn't. When someone develops infection-associated chronic illness, something goes wrong with that stand-down. The question researchers are asking now is: what?

This chapter explains three lines of investigation into what might be happening. None of these is proven fact; all three are active research questions with real evidence pointing toward them but without final answers. What matters for you is understanding what the evidence shows, what remains uncertain, and how researchers are thinking about the problem.

## The Persistence Theory: The Virus Isn't Entirely Gone

The first theory is the simplest to understand: maybe the virus isn't actually gone. Maybe somewhere in your body — in tissues, in organs, in the gut — viral material persists for months or years after the acute infection ended. Your immune system can see it but can't quite clear it. The virus keeps triggering low-grade inflammation, and that ongoing stimulus is what produces the chronic symptoms.

### What's actually been found

Researchers have detected viral RNA and viral proteins in tissue samples from people with long COVID months after infection. Using ultrasensitive detection methods (tools so precise they can identify single molecules), scientists found circulating viral spike antigen in blood samples from about 25 percent of people studied at various points in the first 14 months after infection. In gut tissue biopsies, viral nucleocapsid protein (part of the virus's structure) was found in about 30 percent of patients sampled, even up to 30 months after infection.

In one particularly striking study, researchers took skin biopsies from the rectosigmoid colon of five long-COVID patients and found viral RNA in all five — and double-stranded RNA (which suggests active viral replication, not just leftover RNA) in three of them. At autopsy, researchers have recovered actual replication-competent virus — virus that can still reproduce — from multiple tissues including the brain, up to over seven months after the acute illness.

This is established: viral or viral-related material can persist in tissue and sometimes in blood for months to years. The persistence is real.

### What remains unsettled

But persistence is not the same as causation. Just because viral material is still there doesn't automatically mean it's what's driving your symptoms. To establish that, you would need to show that clearing the virus also clears the symptoms. That test has been partly done: in a randomized controlled trial published in early 2025, researchers gave long-COVID patients either an antiviral drug (nirmatrelvir-ritonavir) or placebo for 28 days. The antiviral successfully reduced viral markers and broke the virus's replication cycle. Yet patients taking the antiviral did not improve more than the placebo group did. Their symptoms stayed the same.

This is a key finding: it suggests that whatever role persisting virus plays, killing it alone is not the full answer. That doesn't mean persistence is irrelevant — it could mean the antiviral was the wrong drug, the dose was wrong, the timing was wrong, or the damage is already done and the virus is no longer the active driver even though it's still present. But the simple story "virus persists → virus drives symptoms → kill virus → symptoms resolve" did not hold in this trial.

### The evidence state

Viral persistence is **established as a real phenomenon**. Whether it is the primary driver of chronic symptoms remains a **mechanistic hypothesis still being tested**. One antiviral trial found no benefit for symptom resolution, but this is a single trial with a single drug and does not close the question.

## The Immune Dysregulation Theory: The Immune System Is Running Wrong

The second major theory proposes something more complicated: the immune system itself is dysregulated. Not fighting an enemy anymore, but running in a strange, persistent state of partial activation — cells exhausted and activated at the same time, antibodies attacking the body's own tissue, inflammatory signals going out when there's nothing acute to fight.

### What's actually been found

The immune dysregulation story divides into several parts, each with different evidence strength.

**T-cell exhaustion.** Researchers have found that a type of immune cell called CD8+ T cells, which normally fight viral infections, shows signs of exhaustion in long-COVID patients — specifically, high levels of PD-1, a protein that marks an exhausted immune cell that has been fighting for too long without clearing the enemy. These exhausted T cells appear in long-COVID cohorts and have been replicated in a second independent cohort by the same research group. This is the most robust single finding in the immune dysregulation literature, though independent replication by different labs is still in progress.

**Autoantibodies.** Several studies have found that long-COVID patients have elevated levels of antibodies that attack the body's own cells. In one striking experiment, researchers took antibodies from long-COVID patients, injected them into mice, and the mice developed fatigue, lost their balance, showed pain responses similar to human central sensitization, and developed damage to their small nerve fibers. This is genuine causal evidence in an animal model — injecting these antibodies causes the disease in the animals. However, causal evidence in mice is not the same as proof of causation in humans, and whether these autoantibodies are the primary driver of long COVID in people remains an inference from the mouse data.

**Complement dysregulation.** The complement system is part of your innate immune machinery — a cascade of proteins that mark invaders for destruction and kill them. In long-COVID patients, multiple steps of the complement cascade are persistently activated: the classical pathway, the alternative pathway, and the terminal pathway all show markers of dysregulation. This finding has been replicated across multiple studies and is established as a real phenomenon. However, different patients show different patterns of pathway activation, and the clinical significance of which exact pathways are dysregulated remains contested — no study has yet shown that targeting a specific pathway improves symptoms.

**EBV reactivation.** Epstein-Barr virus is a common virus that infects most humans by adulthood and then lives silently in the body for life. In some long-COVID patients, EBV reactivates — comes back out of hibernation — as if the immune system lost the ability to keep it suppressed. Studies have found that evidence of EBV reactivation appears more frequently in long-COVID patients than in fully recovered controls, and specific patterns of EBV antibodies are associated with fatigue and cognitive dysfunction in long-COVID populations. However, EBV reactivation also occurs in some people without long COVID, and a substantial minority of long-COVID patients show no evidence of EBV reactivation at all — meaning it is associated with symptoms but is neither necessary nor sufficient to cause them. The question of whether EBV reactivation is driving the symptoms or whether the chronic illness is simply creating an environment where EBV can reactivate (two very different stories) remains open.

**The contested finding: microclots.** One research group has reported that blood from long-COVID patients contains microscopic clots with an unusual structure — amyloid-form fibrin that doesn't break down normally — and that these microclots impair oxygen delivery. Another group published a direct critique arguing that microclots are not specific to long COVID, that the methods for detecting and counting them lack standardization, and that the inference from seeing microclots to microclots causing symptoms is not evidence-based. The originators responded with a rebuttal. This is an **unresolved scientific dispute** — both sides are named and present arguments in the published literature, and no independent, large-scale, controlled human trial has settled the question. This line is one to watch, but it is not established fact.

### The evidence state

Immune dysregulation broadly is **well-supported as an associated finding** — multiple types of immune dysfunction occur in long COVID. T-cell exhaustion is **replicated but not yet independently confirmed by different labs**. Autoantibodies are **associated with long COVID and have causal evidence in animal models**, though human causality remains an inference. EBV reactivation is **established as an association with specific symptom clusters** but causality remains **mechanistic hypothesis**. Complement dysregulation is **established as a real phenomenon** but **contested in terms of clinical significance and which pathway matters most**. The microclot theory is **explicitly contested** and does not yet have standardized measurement methods or independent large-scale replication.

## The Dysautonomia Theory: The Autonomic Nervous System Is Misfiring

Your autonomic nervous system runs your automatic functions — heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, sweating, breathing — the things you don't consciously control. It has two main arms: the sympathetic system (gas pedal, speeds things up) and the parasympathetic system (brake, slows things down). In healthy people, these two arms stay balanced: they rev up in response to stress or exertion, then calm down when the stress ends.

In dysautonomia, this balance breaks. The most common dysautonomic condition in long COVID is postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or POTS — a state where standing up produces an excessive heart-rate increase and can cause dizziness, brain fog, fatigue, and other symptoms. But dysautonomia is broader than POTS alone; it's a general dysregulation of the autonomic system.

### What's actually been found

Autonomic dysfunction is very common in long COVID. In studies of symptomatic long-COVID patients presenting to specialty clinics, about 31 percent meet formal diagnostic criteria for POTS, and an additional 27 percent have orthostatic symptoms that don't quite meet the formal criteria. In the general population of people who had COVID-19, the rate drops to about 1 percent — much lower, but still higher than the rate in vaccinated people (0.04 percent), so infection does increase the risk.

The proposed mechanisms are two:

**G-protein-coupled receptor autoimmunity.** Your cells talk to each other through receptors — protein machines on the cell surface that receive signals. G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) control things like heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing. Several single-center studies have found that long-COVID patients with POTS have antibodies that attack these receptors. A larger study of 194 long-COVID patients found high rates of these antibodies and showed associations between specific antibodies and specific symptoms like dizziness and concentration difficulty. However, one large, standardized case-control study using commercially available assay methods found no significant difference in GPCR autoantibodies between POTS patients and healthy controls. This discrepancy appears to trace to differences in how the antibodies are measured (functional bioassays that test whether the antibodies activate receptors versus binding assays that just test whether they stick to the receptor), not to a true disagreement about biology. **The mechanism is plausible but technically unresolved** — the evidence for the antibodies exists, but their causal role is contested.

**Small-fiber neuropathy.** The small nerve fibers in your skin that control autonomic functions (vasoconstriction, sweating) and carry pain signals can be damaged. In post-COVID populations, skin biopsy studies consistently find elevated rates of small-fiber denervation — loss of these delicate nerve fibers at the skin surface. One study found small-fiber neuropathy in 94 percent of post-COVID patients sampled, compared to much lower rates in uninfected controls. Critically, however, small-fiber neuropathy was present at similar high rates in vaccinated controls (79 percent), which complicates simple infection-specific explanations. A small retrospective study found that long-COVID patients with small-fiber neuropathy who received intravenous immunoglobulin (a blood product containing antibodies) showed improvement in neuropathic symptoms at much higher rates than an untreated comparison group, which is suggestive but comes from an unblinded, uncontrolled retrospective design. The elevation of small-fiber neuropathy in post-COVID populations is **established as a real finding**, but whether this neuropathy is causally responsible for the orthostatic symptoms or whether it is a parallel consequence of the infection remains **mechanistic hypothesis**.

Management evidence is sparse. A randomized trial of ivabradine (a drug that slows heart rate) for long-COVID POTS found that the drug successfully reduced heart rate but did not improve symptoms or quality-of-life measures. A secondary finding was that patients who also received coordinated nonpharmacologic care (pacing, compression garments, increased fluid intake) reported more symptom improvement than those on medication alone. This suggests that addressing dysautonomia in long COVID may require more than targeting the heart-rate problem alone.

### The evidence state

Dysautonomia prevalence in long COVID is **well-established**. The proposed mechanisms — GPCR autoimmunity and small-fiber neuropathy — are both **associated with dysautonomia but mechanistically contested**. Whether either one is the primary driver versus an epiphenomenon or a parallel consequence is **not yet established**. Management evidence is limited; one antiarrhythmic drug worked pharmacologically but not symptomatically, and nonpharmacologic support appeared more beneficial in preliminary data.

## How These Three Theories Might Fit Together

These three major lines of investigation — persistence, immune dysregulation, dysautonomia — are presented separately here, but in reality they may not be separate phenomena. Persisting virus could trigger immune dysregulation. Immune dysregulation could cause the autoantibody production that leads to dysautonomia. Small-fiber neuropathy could amplify dysautonomic symptoms. The three could be serial: one causes the next. Or they could be parallel: the initial infection damages multiple body systems in different ways, producing independent but coincident problems.

The research to distinguish these possibilities is ongoing. What matters now is understanding that **no single mechanism is proven as the cause of long COVID**, that **long COVID is likely mechanistically different in different patients**, and that **multiple lines of investigation are pointing at real biological abnormalities** even if the causal stories aren't complete yet.

---

## The Bottom Line on Mechanisms

Researchers have identified real, measurable abnormalities in long-COVID patients:

- Viral or viral-related material persists in tissue for months to years in a subset of patients.
- Multiple types of immune dysregulation occur: T-cell exhaustion, autoantibodies, complement activation, and EBV reactivation.
- Dysautonomia, particularly small-fiber neuropathy and dysregulated autonomic reflexes, occurs at elevated rates.

**None of these has been conclusively proven to be the driving cause of all long-COVID symptoms.** One antiviral trial did not produce symptom improvement despite successfully lowering viral markers. Immune dysfunction occurs in long COVID but also in recovered patients, sometimes at similar rates. Dysautonomic findings are reproducible but could be causes, consequences, or parallel effects of the infection.

**This is not a failure of the research — it is honest science.** The researchers studying these mechanisms are holding the line between "here is what we found" and "therefore this causes the disease." That line is the difference between good science and false confidence, and it is worth respecting.

What this means for living with these conditions is that **no single treatment is likely to work for everyone**, because the underlying drivers are probably not the same in everyone. It also means **treatment research is still early** — we are at the stage of identifying candidate mechanisms, not at the stage of having proven targets for therapeutic intervention. But it means the research is real, the findings are substantive, and the field is asking the right questions even if the final answers are not yet in.
