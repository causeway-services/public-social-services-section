# Chapter 2: Attune

## Your own history is the instrument

Somewhere in the last few months, you have probably been handed a number that was supposed to mean something and didn't. A resting heart rate flagged "elevated" by a smartwatch that has no idea what your resting heart rate actually runs. A lab value inside the reference range, delivered as reassurance, on a day you could barely stand at the sink. Population thresholds are built for populations. You are not a population. You are one person with one history, and the only baseline that tells you anything true about today is the one built from your own yesterday.

Attune is the practice of reading that history. Not a single measurement, but the pattern behind it: your resting heart rate over the last month, not just this morning's reading; how last night's sleep compares with your usual nights, not with eight hours of textbook sleep; how today's fatigue and brain fog sit against the run of days before it. A population threshold can tell you whether you are unusual compared with strangers. Only your own rolling history can tell you whether today is unusual compared with you.

This matters more, not less, in infection-associated chronic illness, because the whole point of pacing is staying inside a capacity that is specific to you and that moves — week to week, sometimes day to day. A "normal" resting heart rate for the general population is a wide range that says nothing about whether 76 beats per minute is an ordinary Tuesday for you or the first sign that today is not going to be an ordinary Tuesday. The instrument that answers that question is not a chart in a textbook. It's the log of your own days.

**What this means today:** if you're tracking anything at all — heart rate, sleep, symptoms, steps — the number that matters is not today's reading against a general chart. It's today's reading against your own last few weeks.

## What the tools can honestly tell you

There is a real and growing set of tools that read heart rate, heart-rate variability (HRV — a measure of the tiny variation in time between heartbeats), sleep, and activity, and turn them into pacing guidance. Some of them are worth using. None of them can do what a headline about them sometimes implies. Here is the honest version, plainly stated, of what the current evidence actually supports.

Start with the two-day exercise test, the closest thing this field has to an objective, repeatable signature of post-exertional malaise: a maximal exercise test, repeated twenty-four hours later, to see whether the body reproduces its own day-one performance. Two studies found that people with ME/CFS characteristically could not — their oxygen uptake, heart rate, and workload at peak effort all dropped measurably on day two, in a pattern healthy people and people with ordinary deconditioning do not show [@keller2014; @snell2013]. That looked, for a while, like a durable biological marker. Then a larger 2026 study using the same protocol, with both patients and healthy controls, found no such decline at all, and its authors said so directly: their data did not support using the two-day test to define post-exertional malaise or disability [@mancini2026]. This is not one study being wrong and another being right. It is a live, unresolved disagreement in the published record, and no tool built on top of it — biometric or otherwise — can honestly claim to detect post-exertional malaise from a two-day decline. The most it can do is describe what happened on day two.

Now the newer, smaller signals. A 2025 study built a model that predicted next-day fatigue from HRV, sleep, and activity data with real accuracy — but in sixty-eight people with post-COVID fatigue, with no separate group used to check whether the model still works on people it wasn't built from [@aboagye2025]. A 2024 feasibility study had thirteen people with long COVID practice slow breathing with HRV feedback for four weeks and found real, statistically meaningful improvement in autonomic symptoms and disability scores — again, thirteen people, no comparison group, and the authors' own conclusion was that the small sample limits how far the result generalizes [@corrado2024heartloc]. Both are genuine, promising, single-cohort findings. Neither has been confirmed by an independent team, in a different group of people, using a different device. That doesn't make them worthless. It makes them early.

And then there are the rules of thumb already circulating in clinics and patient communities — most commonly, stay below your resting heart rate plus fifteen beats per minute during activity. This heuristic comes from a rehabilitation practice's clinical experience, and it may well be useful. But a direct search of the published literature for a study that tested this specific threshold against post-exertional-malaise outcomes turns up nothing. It is a heuristic in wide informal use, not a validated instrument, and it should be offered to you as exactly that — never with the authority of a tested measurement.

**What this means today:** any tool, including the pacing calculator this book will point you to, can honestly do three things with your biometric data — show it to you, notice when it drifts from your own pattern, and offer a heuristic labeled as a heuristic. It cannot diagnose post-exertional malaise from a heart-rate reading, and you should be skeptical of anything that claims it can.

## The second day is the reading that matters

If there is one habit worth building out of everything in this chapter, it's this: check tomorrow, and the day after, not just today. Post-exertional malaise characteristically arrives twelve to seventy-two hours after the exertion that triggers it and then lasts for days [@stussman2020]. That delay is the whole reason a felt-in-the-moment approach fails — Chapter 1 covered why the pause has to be set in advance — and it's also why attunement can't stop at "how do I feel right now." The read that actually tells you whether yesterday's activity was inside your envelope is not yesterday. It's the day after tomorrow.

In practice, this means holding two days in your head at once: what you did, and what happened after. Did Tuesday's grocery run get followed by an ordinary Wednesday, or did Thursday arrive with the fog and the ache that mean the run cost more than it looked like it cost at the time? You cannot answer that on Tuesday. You can only answer it by checking back. Over weeks, that habit — activity, then a look at the second day, then a look at the day after that — is what actually builds an accurate picture of your envelope. A single day's data point tells you almost nothing. The relationship between an activity and what follows it, tracked over time, tells you a great deal.

**What this means today:** when you log an activity, set a reminder — a note, an alarm, whatever works — to check back in two and three days. That delayed check is worth more than anything you can feel in the moment.

## Watching relations, not one number

There's a reason attunement is about more than any single reading, and it comes from how your body's stress response actually works. Two systems answer the same demand — standing up, walking to the mailbox, a hard conversation — on two different clocks. One is fast and neural: it fires in seconds, raises your heart rate, and gets your blood moving. The other is slow and hormonal: it takes fifteen to thirty minutes to ramp up, and its job is to fund the fast system's demand, hold your blood vessels responsive to it, and eventually shut the whole response down. In a body where these two arms are working together, that's invisible — it just works. Current research on ME/CFS proposes that in this illness, the two arms come apart: the fast system fires normally, but the slow system that's supposed to fund and end it does not keep up. That is a well-supported hypothesis, not yet a settled fact, but it explains a lot that a single number never could.

It's a useful way to understand something you may already have lived: an orthostatic test — standing up while your blood pressure is measured — comes back normal, and yet standing still makes you feel dizzy, foggy, or wrong in a way the number doesn't capture. One small study measuring blood flow to the brain directly, rather than just blood pressure at the arm, found that people with post-COVID symptoms and people with POTS (a related dysautonomia) both showed roughly a 20% drop in blood flow to the brain on standing, against about 3% in healthy people — even in cases where blood pressure itself looked unremarkable [@novak2022]. The prediction this points to is specific: it's not that your blood pressure will fall, it's that something further downstream can go wrong while the blood-pressure cuff says everything is fine. That's a real gap between what a standard test measures and what you actually feel, and it's worth naming to a clinician in exactly those terms.

This is also why HRV — the tiny beat-to-beat variation this chapter mentioned earlier — is interesting as a relationship, not a single score. One 2026 study tracked continuous HRV in long-COVID patients over several days and found that exertion near a person's own aerobic threshold was followed by a suppressed HRV that lingered for twenty-four hours afterward, more so with harder or longer exertion [@ruijgt2026]. That's one observational study, not yet independently repeated, and its own authors were careful to frame it as supporting general overexertion awareness, not a validated threshold for detecting post-exertional malaise. But it fits the larger pattern: attunement isn't about staring at one number until it crosses a line. It's about noticing how the systems that are supposed to work together are relating to each other, over time, against your own baseline.

**What this means today:** if a test comes back "normal" but you don't feel normal, that is not a contradiction to dismiss — current research on how these two response systems interact gives a real, honest reason why a normal reading and a real symptom can coexist. Bring both to your clinician.

## The tools built for this

Two tools exist specifically to support this kind of attunement, and it's worth knowing exactly what each one is and isn't.

**pacing.561.group** is a free calculator, built to run locally: nothing you enter leaves your own device unless you choose to export it. It's built to hold your own rolling history — heart rate, sleep, activity, symptoms, however much or little you choose to log — and hand it back to you as a pattern, not a verdict. When you want your data, it's yours to export as a file, in full, at any time. It does not diagnose anything, and it will not tell you today is "good" or "bad" — it will show you how today compares with your own recent past and let you draw the conclusion.

**PACER** — Personal Agent Collecting Energy Readings — is a companion agent designed to sit alongside a tool like the calculator: picture a tree frog living in a culvert, watching the water level it actually lives in, not a lake somewhere else. PACER is specified in detail, including exactly how it would handle your data, but as of this writing it has not been built. It's named here so you know it's coming and what it's meant to do, not because you can use it yet: readings would never leave your device except as a short summary, and it would read the calculator's own exported history so your data moves between tools without you losing custody of it.

**What this means today:** you can start using the calculator today, for free, with your data staying on your own device. PACER is a real plan, not yet a real product — check back for it rather than looking for it now.

## Keeping notes on a bad day

You do not need a perfect log. On a day when getting to the kitchen is the whole agenda, a perfect log is not a realistic ask, and this practice was never meant to add another burden to a day that already has too much in it. What you need is the minimum that lets your future self, or your clinician, see the pattern later.

That minimum is small. A resting heart rate reading, if you have a way to take one, once a day. A rough symptom note — better, same, worse than yesterday, in a word or two. What you did, in the plainest terms: "walked to the mailbox," "video call, 20 minutes," "nothing." That's it. Three lines, most days, written in whatever app, notebook, or voice memo is easiest to reach on your worst day, because the version of logging that actually happens on a bad day beats the elaborate version that only happens on a good one.

The value of even this much shows up two or three days later, when you're checking back the way the last section described. A three-line entry from Tuesday is what lets you connect Thursday's crash to something specific, instead of it just arriving as another bad day for no reason you can name.

**What this means today:** pick the three things you'll log — a number if you have one, a word for how you feel, a word for what you did — and log them today, even if today is a bad day. Especially if today is a bad day.

## What to do today

Start with one week of noticing, before you try to change anything. Log your three lines. If you're tracking heart rate or HRV, look at it against your own last month, not against a general chart. When you do something, mark your calendar to check back in two and three days, and actually look. If a rule of thumb like the heart-rate-plus-fifteen ceiling is useful to you, use it — as a rule of thumb, not a verdict. And if a test comes back normal while you don't feel normal, write down exactly what you felt; that gap is real information, not something to talk yourself out of.

None of this requires more energy than you have today. It requires noticing what you already have — your own history — and starting to read it as the instrument it actually is. Chapter 3 takes what Attune helps you see and turns it into the daily practice of Care.
