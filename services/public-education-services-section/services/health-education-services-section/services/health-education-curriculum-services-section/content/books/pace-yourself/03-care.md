# Chapter 3: Care

Pause set your ceiling before you feel it coming. Attune taught you to read your own history instead of a stranger's average. This chapter is about what happens in the space those two verbs protect — the actual hours of being sick, and the people, tools, and conversations that make those hours more bearable. Care, in PACE Yourself, means one thing specifically: repairing what strain has broken, never charging you for the repair.

## What this program will not do

A lot of what gets sold to people with a chronic illness is built to need you back tomorrow. A streak you don't want to break. A guilt nudge when you miss a day. A subscription that quietly assumes you'll keep paying whether or not it's helping. A confident diagnosis from a wearable that, if you read the actual research behind it, is one small study away from being an open question. None of that is in this program, on purpose.

Streaks and guilt are the sharpest problem, because of what you already know from Pause: the cost of overdoing it arrives late and lands hard. A habit tracker that makes you feel bad about a missed day is asking for exactly the thing pacing exists to prevent — pushing past today's real capacity to protect a number on a screen. A program built for post-exertional malaise cannot also be built to punish rest. And a confident-sounding number — a score, a percentage, a "you're at risk" flag — sold as more certain than the evidence behind it actually is, is its own kind of harm: it takes a genuinely open question and hands it to you as settled, which either scares you into overcaution or, when it turns out to be wrong, teaches you to stop trusting anything the tool tells you.

There's a plainer way to say why this matters, underneath the specific rules. Real protection is something people build together and draw on together — the guideline a clinic follows because other patients' outcomes taught it something, the pacing knowledge that circulated through ME/CFS communities for decades before long COVID gave it a bigger audience, the fact that you are reading this book at all because people before you fought to get post-exertional malaise taken seriously. Protection that instead gets sold to you as something only you can buy, one subscription at a time, walling you off rather than connecting you to what others have already learned, is a thinner and lonelier kind of safety. Care, here, means staying on the first kind.

## Caring for the body: the orthostatic conversation

If standing up makes you dizzy, lightheaded, or foggy, and a clinic has told you your blood pressure was normal, you are not imagining the mismatch. One of the house's own working explanations for post-exertional malaise proposes that the two arms of your body's stress response — the fast nervous-system arm and the slower hormone arm — stop coordinating with each other in this illness. If that is right, the specific, testable prediction it makes is not that your blood pressure drops. It's that blood flow to your brain can fail while your blood pressure holds steady, because the vessels aren't responding normally to the signals telling them to constrict. That is exactly the presentation that gets people told their exam was normal. This is a well-supported explanation, not yet a proven one — flag it as reasoning from mechanism, not as an established diagnosis.

Whether or not that specific explanation turns out to be right, orthostatic intolerance itself is common and real in this population. A postural heart-rate jump on standing — postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or POTS — shows up in roughly one in a hundred people at some point after any infection, and in around three in ten people already sick enough with long COVID to be seen at a specialty clinic [@yong2023; @bjornson2024]. Separately, small-fiber neuropathy — damage to the finest nerves, the ones that help control blood vessel tone — turns up on skin biopsy at strikingly elevated rates in post-COVID patients compared to healthy people [@donadio2024]. One small, unblinded study found people with this neuropathy improved more often after treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin than people who went untreated, though the study's authors were explicit about how far that particular signal is from proof [@dalakas2024].

What actually helps, in practice: compression on your legs and abdomen, worn before you stand rather than after you're already dizzy. Deliberate positional strategies — sitting on the edge of the bed before standing, crossing your legs and tensing your calves while upright, raising the head of your bed a few inches at night, avoiding long stretches of still standing (a shower chair is not giving up, it's compression's cousin). Fluids and salt, increased under a clinician's guidance rather than guessed at. And medication — the drugs clinicians reach for here (beta-blockers, midodrine, ivabradine among them) only with a clinician who knows your case, never self-started. The best current trial evidence on this is worth knowing before that conversation: a large platform trial testing one such drug against placebo for long-COVID POTS found it did what it was supposed to do to the heart rate, but that alone didn't move people's symptoms — what did move the needle was that same drug paired with structured, coordinated non-drug support [@fudim2026recoverautonomic; @recoverautonomicivabradine2026]. The drug and the support work as a pair, not as substitutes for each other.

## Sleep that doesn't refresh

Unrefreshing sleep is not a side complaint in this illness — it's one leg of the same three-legged diagnostic stool as post-exertional malaise and the fatigue itself [@iom2015]. You can sleep eight or nine hours and wake up as tired as when you lay down. That is not a discipline failure and it does not mean you're sleeping "wrong." It means treating sleep quantity as the whole goal will disappoint you; the honest aim on a bad week is protecting the conditions around sleep — dark, quiet, a consistent wind-down, a bedroom that isn't also your workstation — rather than expecting a fixed number of hours to fix how you feel the next day.

## Hydration, electrolytes, and temperature — labeled honestly

Extra fluids and salt (electrolytes) are standard first-line advice for orthostatic intolerance generally, extended to long COVID by clinical practice rather than proven in a long-COVID-specific trial — that is a reasonable extension from the broader POTS literature, not yet its own tested result in this population, and it should be offered to you with exactly that caveat. The same honesty applies to heat: POTS symptoms are characteristically worse in warm environments and improve lying down [@grubb2015], which is a documented feature of the diagnosis, not a promise that any particular cooling strategy will fix a given day. Given that, a cooling towel or a fan by the bed on a hot day is a reasonable comfort measure, offered as exactly that — comfort, not treatment.

## Sensory load and the rooms you rest in

Cognitive exertion counts as exertion, and so does sensory load — bright light, noise, a cluttered visual field, a phone buzzing every few minutes. None of that is separately proven to trigger post-exertional malaise the way physical exertion is documented to, but it costs the same kind of attention and arousal your body is already short on, so the practical move is the same: build a low-stimulus space you can retreat to before you're in a flare, not after. An eye mask and a pair of earplugs are unglamorous, but they turn "I need to lie down somewhere dark and quiet" from a scramble into something you already have ready.

## The clinician conversation

Walking into an appointment with a clinician who doesn't know this territory well, the most useful thing you can say plainly is: normal results here don't rule out what you're describing. A standard blood-pressure check can miss the cerebral-perfusion pattern above entirely, because it's designed to. Bring your own data if you track it — a printed pacing log, or an exported history from a free tool like the pacing calculator at pacing.561.group — so the conversation starts from your own pattern instead of your memory of a bad week. Ask directly whether pacing, not a fixed exercise schedule, is what they'd recommend; UK national guidance explicitly recommends pacing over graded, schedule-based exercise programs for this illness [@nice2021ng206]. It's also fair to ask how the clinic hears about a recommendation that made someone worse — one survey of specialist clinics found none had a systematic way of tracking treatment-related harm, and none had recorded a single case of it despite people dropping out of care [@mcphee2019]. A clinician who can sit with "that's a real, disproportionate, delayed reaction, not you being dramatic" [@stussman2020] is one worth keeping.

## Caring for a person who has this

If someone you love has post-exertional malaise, the single most useful thing you can internalize is that the second day — not today — is the actual test of whether today went okay. A visit, a conversation, even a good day out, can look fine in the moment and cost three days later. Plan for that instead of being surprised by it: check in the day after, not just the day of. Help that costs the person nothing to receive — dropping off food instead of hosting, a phone call kept short and endable instead of a long visit, running an errand without needing to be thanked for it in person — is worth more than help that requires them to perform wellness for you while receiving it. And when a plan needs to change because of a flare, treating that as normal and expected, not as a disappointment they have to apologize for, is itself a form of care.

## What to do today

Pick one thing from this chapter that costs you nothing to try this week: raise the head of your bed a couple of inches, put an eye mask by your pillow, write down what today actually cost you so you have real data for your next clinician visit instead of a guess. Care is not a bigger ask than that. It's the ordinary, unglamorous work of making today survivable — and it is never going to ask you to push through to earn it.

This is not medical advice; talk with a clinician who knows your case before changing medication, fluids, or salt intake.
