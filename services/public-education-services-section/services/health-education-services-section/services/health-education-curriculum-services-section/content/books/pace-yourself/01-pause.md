# Chapter 1: Pause

Pause is the first move of PACE Yourself, and it is the one that goes against everything you have probably been taught about pushing through. Pause means stopping before the limit, not at it. Not when you feel bad. Not when your body finally objects. Before.

That sounds backward if you have never lived with post-exertional malaise (PEM) — the delayed, disproportionate symptom flare that follows exertion in myalgic encephalomyelitis, long COVID, and the other infection-associated chronic illnesses. If you have, you already know why waiting for the signal is the wrong plan: the signal doesn't come on time. Book 1 walked through what PEM is and the honest evidence for pacing as a strategy against it. This chapter doesn't repeat that ground. It takes one piece of that science — the delay — and asks what it actually requires you to do, today, before you feel anything at all.

## Why the moment cannot be felt

Ordinary fatigue gives you a warning you can act on. You get tired, you notice, you sit down. The feedback loop is fast enough to steer by.

Post-exertional malaise breaks that loop. A short walk, half an hour of housework, a stretch of concentrated thinking, even a hard conversation — something modest enough that you might not register it as "exertion" at all — can produce a severe flare 12 to 72 hours later. Not a bad afternoon. A flare that lasts days to weeks, not hours [@stussman2020]. In long COVID specifically, more than half of people report experiencing this kind of delayed crash within the first few months after infection [@an2024]. This is not a rare or unusual reaction; for a large share of people with these illnesses, it is the baseline pattern.

The delay is exactly what makes "listen to your body" incomplete advice here. By the time your body tells you something was too much, the exertion that caused it is a day or two in the past, and the choice you'd want to make differently already happened. You cannot feel the ceiling at the moment you cross it, because the cost hasn't arrived yet.

There is a second piece to this, and it changes how you should picture your own capacity. It is tempting to think of your energy as a tank you can run down and then have to refill — get a little more tired with every task, until you're empty. The research on exercise capacity in myalgic encephalomyelitis points to something different: capacity behaves less like a tank and more like a rate — a ceiling on how much your body can do at once, not a reserve that simply depletes. Two studies using a repeated exercise test on consecutive days found that people with myalgic encephalomyelitis showed measurably lower performance on the second day than the first — a pattern healthy people and people with ordinary deconditioning do not show [@keller2014; @snell2013]. A larger 2026 study using the same protocol did not find that same second-day decline, so this specific test result is genuinely unsettled in the research right now [@mancini2026]. What is not in dispute across these studies is the basic shape of the problem they are all trying to measure: people are hitting a functional ceiling at levels of exertion that would barely register for someone without the illness. If capacity is a rate ceiling rather than a stock, the failure doesn't show up as "ran out late in the day." It shows up as failing at low intensity, sooner than seems like it should be possible — which is exactly what pacing has to plan around.

**What this means today:** you cannot rely on real-time symptoms to tell you when to stop, because the bill for today's activity may not arrive until the day after tomorrow. The plan has to be set before you start, using what you already know about your own pattern — not decided in the moment by how you currently feel.

## The forms of pausing

Pausing is not one thing. It takes several shapes, and most people end up using more than one at a time.

**Pre-emptive rest blocks** are scheduled stops built into your day whether or not you feel you need them yet — twenty minutes lying down after breakfast, a rest period built into the middle of an errand, a fixed stopping point on a task before it's finished. The point of a pre-emptive block is that it doesn't wait for a symptom to justify it. You take it because it's the plan, not because you've earned it by suffering first.

**Time-boxed activity** puts a limit on duration rather than on how the activity feels. Instead of "I'll fold laundry until I'm done," it's "I'll fold laundry for fifteen minutes, then stop, regardless of how much is left." This matters because how a task feels in the first fifteen minutes is a poor predictor of what it will cost you two days later — the feedback you'd normally use to decide "a little more is fine" isn't trustworthy here.

**Cognitive and emotional exertion count too.** This is the part people most often miss, because it doesn't look like exertion. Concentrating hard at a screen, following a fast conversation, doing your taxes, sitting with a difficult phone call, holding your composure through a stressful meeting — all of these can trigger the same delayed flare as physical activity. If your pacing plan only accounts for physical movement, you can do everything "right" physically and still get flattened by a day that was mentally or emotionally demanding. A pause has to budget for all three: body, mind, and feeling.

**What this means today:** look at your day for cognitive and emotional load, not just physical tasks. A pause plan that only tracks steps and stairs is only pacing a third of what actually costs you.

## The heart-rate ceiling: a rule of thumb, not a rule

Because the moment cannot be felt, a lot of people look for a number instead — something objective to watch instead of an unreliable internal signal. The most common version of this is a heart-rate ceiling: stay below a certain heart rate during activity, and stop or slow down if you go above it.

The best-known version of this is the Workwell Foundation's rule of thumb: keep your heart rate below your resting heart rate plus 15 beats per minute during activity. It is worth knowing this rule exists, because it circulates widely in patient communities and clinical practice, and it is a genuinely useful anchor for many people. It is also worth knowing, plainly, what it is and isn't: it is clinical practice guidance, developed by a rehabilitation practice with real experience treating this illness — not a threshold that has been tested in a dedicated study and shown to predict or prevent post-exertional malaise. The same is true of the other common rule of thumb, staying under a fixed percentage of your age-predicted maximum heart rate: it is a reasonable, cautious starting point, not a validated instrument.

That distinction matters in a specific way. A heart-rate ceiling can be a useful, honest tool — a number you can watch in real time, when the delayed nature of PEM makes real-time symptoms useless. But it should be held the way you'd hold any rule of thumb: as a starting estimate to test against your own experience and adjust, not as a scientifically proven line that guarantees safety below it and danger above it. Nobody has published the study that would let anyone say that with confidence, for this particular number, yet.

**What this means today:** if you use a heart-rate ceiling, use it as a starting guess you refine from your own results — not as a promise. Watch what happens after you stay under it, and after you go over it, and let your own pattern, not the number alone, tell you where your real ceiling sits.

## The calendar is not the signal

There is a second kind of ceiling that deserves the same skepticism, and it's more familiar because it's built into how most rehabilitation and fitness advice works: the calendar. Do a little more this week than last week. Increase duration or intensity on a fixed schedule, regardless of how you're doing, on the theory that steady, gradual increases will build your capacity back up.

This approach has a name — graded exercise therapy — and for many conditions it works exactly as intended: deconditioning responds to gradual reintroduction of activity. For post-exertional malaise, it is the wrong tool, because it is built on the assumption pacing exists to reject: that today's tolerable-seeming exertion is safe to build on, when the actual cost of today's exertion won't be visible until the day after tomorrow. A schedule that increases activity regardless of symptom response will, sooner or later, increase activity past a ceiling it cannot see coming.

This is not a new or untested claim. "PACE" was the name of a large 2011 UK trial that tested graded exercise therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy for myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome. The trial had real, documented methodological problems: its outcome measures and recovery thresholds were changed partway through the trial, the thresholds it used to count someone as "recovered" overlapped with the criteria used to let someone into the trial in the first place, and it did not measure post-exertional malaise at all. The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence reviewed this record and, in its 2021 guideline, withdrew its earlier recommendation for graded-exercise-based treatment, replacing it with a caution that pacing — not a fixed schedule — may be more appropriate [@nice2021ng206]. That is published record, not advocacy: the trial's own design choices are why its results are no longer treated as a reliable guide to what to do.

PACE Yourself takes its name from the same three letters on purpose, and does the opposite of what that trial's approach did. It is pacing — the strategy that trial's schedule-based approach set aside — reclaimed as the name for the thing patients actually do to manage this illness. The calendar is not the signal here. Your own recent history is.

**What this means today:** if a plan tells you to do more this week than last week simply because a week has passed, that plan is answering to the wrong authority. Any increase in activity should answer to how you actually did after the last one — not to the date.

## Pausing on a good day

The hardest pause to take is the one on a day when you feel fine. If your envelope for a task is twenty minutes and you're at minute fifteen feeling completely okay, every instinct says keep going — you're doing well, why waste it?

This is the discipline pausing actually asks of you: stopping at fifteen when you could plausibly keep going to twenty, or beyond. Not because fifteen is a magic number, but because the whole point of staying inside your envelope is to never find out, the hard way, where the edge of it actually was today. A good day is not evidence your envelope has grown. It is one data point, and pushing past it to test that theory is how the two-day-later crash gets set up.

This is also where pacing differs most sharply from an ordinary "listen to your body and rest when needed" approach. Ordinary fatigue rewards pushing a little on a good day — you'll feel it if you overdo it, and you can back off. Post-exertional malaise punishes that same instinct on a delay long enough that the good day and the bad day never feel connected to each other. Stopping early on a day that felt easy is not caution for its own sake. It is the only way the delay in this illness can be planned around at all.

**What this means today:** notice the moment you catch yourself thinking "I feel good, I could keep going." That thought is the cue to stop, not the reason to continue.

## What to do today

You don't need a perfect system to start pausing. You need a few honest decisions, made in advance, before you're relying on a symptom that will arrive too late to help you.

Pick one recurring task in your day — a chore, a work block, a walk — and set a time limit for it before you start, shorter than you think you need. Notice, separately from physical activity, one thing today that costs you cognitively or emotionally, and give it a boundary the same way. If you use a heart-rate number, write down what you're using it for and treat it as a guess you're testing, not a rule you've proven. And the next time a task is going well and you feel the pull to keep going a little longer, treat that feeling itself as the stop sign.

None of this requires knowing your exact envelope yet. Attune, the next chapter, is about how you find that out from your own data over time. Pause is what buys you the room to look: stop early enough, consistently enough, that the two-day-later flare has nothing to tell you, because you never crossed the line it would have punished.
