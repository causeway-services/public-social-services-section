# Chapter 4: Engage

## What "engage" does not mean

If you have spent any time around chronic-illness advice, "engage" probably makes you brace. It sounds like the start of a pep talk: get back out there, push a little further each week, don't let the illness win. That is not what this chapter means, and the difference is not a matter of tone. It is a difference in mechanism.

Book 1 walked through why post-exertional malaise breaks the ordinary rule of "push a bit, rest, repeat": the flare from an exertion doesn't show up right away. It can take 12 to 72 hours to arrive, and once it does, it can last days to weeks, not hours [@stussman2020]. That delay is the whole problem with a calendar-based plan. A schedule that says "walk five minutes longer this week than last" is written before your body has reported back on this week, because it can't report back that fast. By the time the flare from Tuesday's push lands on Thursday, the schedule has already told you to push again on Wednesday.

That specific plan — fixed increases in activity on a predetermined timetable, regardless of how you respond — has a name: graded exercise therapy. It is a real, evidence-supported treatment for real conditions, mainly ones where deconditioning is the actual problem. It is also the approach the 2011 PACE trial tested and recommended for ME/CFS, using methods and recovery thresholds that the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence later found unreliable enough to withdraw its own earlier recommendation over — no post-exertional-malaise measurement, outcome goalposts moved mid-trial, "recovery" thresholds that overlapped with the criteria for getting into the trial in the first place. NICE's 2021 guideline replaced that recommendation with pacing instead, for exactly the population this book is written for [@nice2021ng206]. This isn't a footnote about one flawed study. It is why this brand is named PACE Yourself in the first place: a deliberate reclaiming of a word that trial's approach never actually did.

Patients have told researchers directly what happens when the graded-exercise approach meets a body that runs on post-exertional malaise instead of ordinary deconditioning. In one large patient-reported-outcome survey, more than half of respondents who had tried graded exercise therapy reported that it made them substantially worse [@geraghty2017]. And when researchers went looking for how well that harm gets tracked, they found a gap: a freedom-of-information survey of NHS ME/CFS specialist clinics in England found none had a systematic protocol for recording treatment-related harm, even though every clinic acknowledged patients dropping out of treatment — which means harm may be going unrecorded rather than not happening [@mcphee2019]. Put those two findings together and the honest picture is: patients report real, common harm from calendar-based pushing, and the systems meant to catch that harm mostly aren't looking.

So here is what engagement actually means in this program: you advance when your body has told you the last exertion is fully paid off, not when a calendar says a week has passed. Progress is real, but it is symptom-contingent and reversible — if a relapse happens, you step back, not through it. There is no finish line where you graduate off pacing into "normal" activity on a fixed timetable. This is the mechanistic opposite of graded exercise therapy, not a gentler version of it.

**What this means today:** if a plan for your week says "more than last week" without asking how last week actually went, that plan is asking the wrong question. The right question is always: has the cost of what I already did been paid off yet?

## Choosing what to spend on

Once pushing-through and calendar progression are off the table, the practical question becomes: given a fixed and often small amount of energy, what do you spend it on?

This is where the "energy envelope" from earlier in this program stops being an abstraction and becomes a daily decision. Book 1 explained the mechanism behind why the envelope has to be respected even on a day you feel fine: aerobic capacity behaves like a rate, not a reserve you draw down and refill, so the failure point shows up at low intensity rather than after you've used up some stockpile. There is no bank of extra energy sitting behind a good day that you're free to spend just because you have it. The envelope on a good day is still the envelope.

Inside that envelope, though, you get to choose. This is usually called energy accounting or energy budgeting, and the comparison to money is useful because it points at the right question. A financial budget doesn't ask "how do I spend nothing," it asks "given what I have, what actually matters enough to spend it on." The same is true here. If today's envelope holds room for one significant thing, the question isn't whether to spend it — you're going to, one way or another, even by accident — the question is whether you spent it on the thing you'd have chosen if you'd been asked in advance.

This is also where pacing stops being purely defensive. The point was never to do as little as possible; it was to avoid the crashes that make the bad months, so that what you do have goes toward what you value. Some days that's a task that has to happen. Some days it's a phone call with someone you love instead of the laundry. Both are legitimate spending. What makes it a budget rather than a guess is writing down, even loosely, what a task actually costs you — and noticing, over weeks, which categories of activity reliably cost more than they look like they should from the outside. Cognitive effort, social effort, and standing upright all draw on the same limited account, even when only one of them looks like "activity."

**What this means today:** before you spend today's envelope, ask which one thing you'd protect if you could only protect one — and protect that one first, before the day fills up with things that arrived by accident.

## Work, study, and relationships inside the envelope

The energy-accounting frame gets harder, not easier, once other people are involved — a job, a class, a family that has its own schedule. Nobody else's calendar was built with a 12-to-72-hour delayed flare in mind, which means the honest version of engaging with work, study, or relationships usually requires you to say something out loud that the people around you did not expect to hear.

The core adjustment is the same one from the rest of this chapter, translated into someone else's terms: build in recovery time before you're asked to perform again, not after you've already crashed. A workday that ends at five and expects you fresh at nine the next morning is, for many people with post-exertional malaise, not actually asking for one day of work — it's asking for the cost of that day to already be paid off by morning, which the 12-to-72-hour delay [@stussman2020] makes an unreasonable bet more often than not. The honest ask, whether to an employer, a professor, or a partner, is rarely "less work." It's usually "the same work, spread differently" — a compressed schedule, an asynchronous deadline instead of a live one, a standing understanding that a bad day means a shifted day, not a missed obligation.

This is uncomfortable to ask for, partly because it requires explaining a delay most people have never had to think about. It helps to say the mechanism plainly rather than just the request: "the cost of today doesn't show up today — if I overdo it now, you'll see it Thursday, not this afternoon." That sentence does real work. It reframes what looks like flakiness or inconsistency as a predictable, plannable fact about how your body settles its accounts, not a character trait.

Relationships carry their own version of this. Emotional exertion spends from the same envelope as physical exertion — a hard conversation can cost as much as a walk you didn't take. People who care about you can usually handle knowing that in advance far better than they can handle watching you disappear for a week afterward with no explanation. Telling someone "that conversation mattered to me and it's also going to cost me the next two days" is not a burden you're putting on them. It's the information they need to actually be there for the version of you that shows up on day three.

**What this means today:** pick one place — work, school, or one relationship — where you have not yet said the delay out loud, and say it once, plainly, as a fact about timing rather than an apology.

## Engage the evidence

Book 1's final chapter taught you how to read a study yourself: check the case definition, check the sample size, check whether anyone else has replicated it, read the confidence interval instead of just the headline. That skill doesn't get re-taught here — go back to that chapter if you want the mechanics again. What belongs in this chapter is what to do with that skill once you have it: use it, on purpose, as part of how you engage with your own illness.

Research on infection-associated illness moves in a loop, and understanding the loop changes how you read a new headline. Patient-reported experience — what actually happens to people pacing in the real world — is what motivates researchers to run small trials in the first place. Those small trials get pooled into meta-analyses, which tell you, honestly, how much confidence the field has actually earned so far. The most recent pacing meta-analysis pooled just five studies and found a large point estimate for fatigue improvement that still fell short of statistical significance, alongside smaller effects on pain and function the authors themselves called trivial [@sanalhayes2024]. That is not a discouraging result to read — it's the loop working exactly as it should: patient experience generated a hypothesis worth testing, a handful of small trials tested it, and the honest state right now is "promising, not yet proven," which is precisely what the study's own authors said.

You can watch this loop happen in real time with the two-day exercise-test marker discussed in Book 1. One study found a measurable decline on day two [@keller2014], a larger controlled study confirmed it [@snell2013], and then a newer, larger 2026 study did not replicate it [@mancini2026]. That is not researchers failing each other. That is the loop mid-cycle, with the next study yet to be run. Watching a live disagreement like this, instead of only reading settled textbook facts, is part of what it means to engage the evidence as it actually exists.

The most current example of the loop for this book's own subject is RECOVER-AUTONOMIC, a large NIH-funded platform trial testing management approaches for long-COVID dysautonomia. Its ivabradine arm — 181 participants, randomized, placebo-controlled — reported a secondary finding worth knowing: patients who received coordinated non-drug care (which includes activity pacing guidance) alongside their assigned treatment reported greater symptom improvement than those on medication management alone [@recoverautonomicivabradine2026]. That result has, as of this writing, been presented at a cardiology conference but has not yet appeared in a peer-reviewed journal publication — the trial's design and rationale are published [@fudim2026recoverautonomic], but the results themselves are still preliminary, conference-only evidence, not yet closed out through peer review. It is genuinely encouraging and it is not yet the last word.

**What this means today:** when you see a new headline about pacing, long COVID, or ME/CFS, ask the same four questions Book 1 taught you before you let it change how you feel: who was studied, how many, has anyone else confirmed it, and is this preliminary or established.

## Engage community and advocacy

The honest state of the pacing evidence — real, promising, and still thin — is not a private fact for you to sit with alone. It's an argument for what should get funded next, and you are allowed to make that argument.

Trials specifically designed around post-exertional malaise, with PEM measured directly rather than assumed, and with symptom-contingent designs instead of fixed schedules, are overdue. The gap is not subtle: the field has one meta-analysis pooling five small pacing studies [@sanalhayes2024], one contested two-day exercise-test marker with studies pointing in opposite directions [@keller2014; @snell2013; @mancini2026], and a documented absence of systematic harm-tracking at the clinics that patients actually attend [@mcphee2019]. That is not enough evidence to run a fifteen-year-old field on, and it is not an accident that it's thin — post-exertional malaise has historically been under-recognized outside ME/CFS communities, and clinical trials in this space are expensive and only recently well-funded at all.

What to ask for, concretely, when you have the energy for advocacy — a public comment to a funding body, a conversation with a clinician-researcher, a note to a patient organization — is specific rather than general: trials that measure post-exertional malaise as a primary outcome, not an afterthought; designs that let treatment adapt to a participant's actual response instead of running on a fixed schedule; and routine, structured harm reporting at the clinics doing this work, so that what happened to the patients in the McPhee survey [@mcphee2019] stops being invisible. None of that requires you to become a research advocate full time. It requires knowing what a good trial for this condition would look like, so that when you do have a moment of energy to spend on it, you spend it asking for the right thing.

**What this means today:** you don't owe the research field your energy. If you have some to spare, spend it asking for one specific thing — PEM measured directly, or harm tracked routinely — rather than a general "more research."

## Engage the tools

Part of engaging on your own terms is keeping your own data where you can actually use it. The free pacing calculator at pacing.561.group computes over the readings you enter and says nothing you didn't ask it to; your history there can be exported and re-imported as your own file, on your own terms, at any time. It is yours — not locked behind an account, not something you have to request back.

That same exported history is built to travel. The identical file you export can be handed to a clinician, or fed into other tools built to read it, without you re-entering a single number. A paid, agent-facing version of the same pacing compute exists specifically for that use — for clinicians and other tools that want to query the analysis programmatically — but it is a service other systems can call on your behalf, never something you are required to use yourself. The free, human-facing calculator is the whole tool for a person managing their own pacing; the paid option exists downstream of it, for people building on top of your data with your say-so, not instead of it.

A companion agent, PACER, is specified to extend this same approach — a daily check-in that collects your readings, keeps you company inside your energy limit, and imports the same export file the calculator already produces, so your history moves between tools without you losing custody of it. As of this writing it is specified but not yet built; when it exists, it will follow the same rule as everything else in this section: your readings stay on your device unless you choose otherwise.

**What this means today:** if you're tracking anything about your pacing right now — on paper, in an app, in your head — ask whether you could get that history out in a form you could hand to someone else, unprompted, if you ever needed to. If the answer is no, that's worth fixing before you need it.

## What to do today

Engagement, in this program, is not a reward you earn for getting better. It's the ordinary shape of a day and a week lived inside your envelope: you do the things that matter to you, you plan for the delayed cost rather than being surprised by it, you say the mechanism out loud to the people who need to understand it, and you let the evidence — not a calendar — tell you when to do more.

Concretely, today: pick the one thing in your day you'd protect first if the envelope was tight. If there's a place — work, school, one relationship — where the delayed cost of exertion has never been said out loud, say it once, plainly. If you're not sure your own tracking could be handed to someone else tomorrow if you needed it to be, take the five minutes to check. And the next time a headline about this illness crosses your feed, run it through the same four questions before you let it change how you feel about today.

None of this promises a cure or a date by which you'll be doing more than you are now. What it offers instead is a way of moving that doesn't cost you the next week to get through this one.
