# The Second Day — Issue 002

_A wearable can show you your own heart rate and heart-rate variability, hour by hour. Here's the honest line between that and what the ads imply: it cannot detect, predict, or diagnose a post-exertional malaise crash — and here's what the actual studies say it can do instead._

## What Your Wearable Actually Knows About You

Last issue we told you the field's flagship objective marker of post-exertional malaise — the two-day exercise test — is currently, honestly contested. This issue starts from the question that follows naturally from that: if the specialized, maximal-effort, cardiologist-supervised version of that test is in dispute, what does it mean that there's an app on your phone claiming to do something similar with a $200 wristband?

We put that question to the actual evidence, the same way we did with the two-day test, and we want to walk you through what we found, because the answer isn't "wearables are useless" and it isn't "wearables can read your body's crash warning light" either. It's something narrower and, we think, still genuinely useful — just not the thing most pacing apps imply they're selling.

Start with the rule you've probably already heard, if you've spent any time in ME/CFS or long-COVID pacing communities: keep your heart rate under your resting heart rate plus 15 beats per minute, and you'll stay inside a safe pacing zone. It's called the Workwell heuristic, after the rehabilitation practice that popularized it — the same practice, notably, behind one of the two studies that found the two-day-test decline we covered last issue. We went looking for the study that validates the specific plus-15 number. We searched PubMed directly for it. There isn't one. That doesn't make the rule useless — a lot of good clinical practice starts as an experienced clinician's rule of thumb before anyone runs the trial — but it means the number on your wrist reads as more precise than the evidence behind it actually is, and you deserve to know that before you build your day around it.

Now the newer science, which comes with its own honest asterisks. In 2025, a team led by Nana Yaw Aboagye fitted 68 people with post-COVID fatigue with heart and activity monitors for up to three weeks and tried to predict each person's fatigue level the next day from their heart-rate-variability patterns — how much the time between heartbeats varies, a signal that tracks how hard your autonomic nervous system is working. Their best model explained about 79% of the variation in next-day fatigue — a striking result. It's also a result from one cohort of 68 people, tuned and tested on that same group, with no separate group of people used to check whether it generalizes — to a different wearable, a different clinic, or even just a different batch of patients. It's real and it's promising. It is not yet a validated predictor you should trust with your Tuesday.

A separate, smaller study asked a different question: can you train heart-rate variability upward, and does that help? Thirteen long-COVID patients completed a month of twice-daily slow breathing exercises using a chest-strap heart monitor and a phone app, and the people who finished it improved significantly on several measures of disability, autonomic symptoms, and quality of life. The authors called it what it was — a feasibility study, thirteen completers, no comparison group of people who didn't do the breathing exercises — and they said plainly that a larger controlled trial is the next step, not a conclusion already reached.

The most relevant new finding this year comes closest to answering the question a pacing tool actually needs answered. A team led by Twan Ruijgt continuously tracked heart-rate variability in 121 long-COVID patients and 21 healthy controls, and separately measured, in the lab, the exercise intensity at which each person's body shifts from primarily aerobic to primarily anaerobic metabolism — their ventilatory threshold. What they found: exercising near or above that personal threshold left a measurable mark. Heart-rate variability stayed suppressed for up to 24 hours afterward, and the suppression went deeper the harder and longer the exertion. The researchers' own framing of this, stated plainly in the paper, is that it supports using wearables to help people manage overexertion — not that it hands anyone a validated number that means "you're about to crash."

That distinction is the whole point of this issue. What that finding demonstrates is something happening within one person, compared to that same person's own other days — not a threshold that reads across bodies, and not a marker anyone has shown predicts a post-exertional malaise flare before it happens. "Your heart-rate variability today is lower than your usual pattern, and that followed unusually hard exertion" is a sentence the evidence actually supports. "Your heart-rate variability just told us you're about to have a crash" is not — nobody has published the study that would earn that sentence, and until someone does, any product making that claim is saying more than the field currently knows.

So here's what we think a biometric pacing tool can honestly offer, built from everything above: it can show you your own numbers, plainly. It can tell you when today looks different from your own recent history — more suppressed HRV, an elevated resting heart rate, exertion that pushed past where you've personally been pushing lately — because that's exactly the kind of within-person comparison the Ruijgt study actually measured. What it cannot do, honestly, is diagnose post-exertional malaise, predict a crash before it lands, or hand you a single number backed by more certainty than the studies behind it actually carry. We think "here's how today compares to your own baseline" is a real, useful thing a wearable can tell you. We don't think anyone should let a device tell them it knows more than that.

## The Evidence State

### "Stay under resting heart rate plus 15 bpm and you'll avoid triggering a crash."

**Unverified heuristic.** This is the most widely circulated biometric pacing rule in patient and clinical communities, originating from the Workwell Foundation's rehabilitation practice. A direct search for a published study validating this specific threshold returned no results. It should be treated as informal clinical guidance, not a tested instrument — nothing here suggests it's wrong, only that it hasn't been shown right.

### "The two-day exercise test proves post-exertional malaise is objectively measurable, so a wearable could detect it too."

**Contested at its own source.** As covered last issue: two controlled or partly controlled studies (Keller 2014, n=22; Snell 2013, n=51 patients plus 10 controls) found a real day-two decline in exercise capacity. The largest and most recent attempt (Mancini 2026, n=58 patients plus 25 controls) found none. If the specialized, in-lab, maximal-effort version of this marker is itself disputed, a wearable's indirect approximation of it inherits that same open question, not a settled one.

### "Heart-rate variability can predict tomorrow's fatigue level."

**Single cohort, unreplicated.** Aboagye and colleagues (2025) trained a model on 68 people with post-COVID fatigue and explained 79% of next-day fatigue variation from HRV and activity data — a real, encouraging result, tested and tuned on the same 68 people, with no external cohort yet used to check whether it generalizes.

### "HRV biofeedback training reduces long-COVID symptoms."

**Single small feasibility study, promising, not yet an efficacy result.** Thirteen long-COVID patients who completed a month of HRV biofeedback breathing training (Corrado et al., the HEARTLOC study, 2024) showed statistically significant improvement on several disability and symptom measures. The trial was designed and registered as a feasibility study, with no control arm; the authors themselves call for a larger controlled trial before treating the result as established.

### "Exercising above your personal threshold leaves a biometric trace you can actually see."

**Single cohort, unreplicated — but the clearest within-subject signal so far.** Ruijgt and colleagues (2026) found heart-rate variability suppressed for up to 24 hours after long-COVID patients exercised near or above their own ventilatory threshold, worsening with harder and longer exertion (n=121 patients, 21 controls). This is the strongest evidence base for "your wearable can flag when today differs from your own pattern" — and, as the authors state directly, it supports overexertion management, not a validated PEM-detection threshold.

### "None of this matters unless pacing itself actually works."

**Hypothesis, not yet established.** Every claim above is about how a device might guide pacing — it says nothing about whether pacing works in the first place. The one meta-analysis of pacing trials (five small studies, covered in issue 001) found a large effect on fatigue that fell just short of conventional statistical significance. A biometric tool built on the evidence above still rests on a pacing strategy the field itself calls promising, not proven.

## From the Desk

**The pacing evidence note.** This issue draws directly from a new structured accounting of the full biometric-pacing evidence base — every claim above traced to its primary source, sample size and effect size quoted rather than carried forward from an earlier summary. It's the same discipline the synthesis paper we mentioned last issue applies to mechanisms, applied here to the tools people are actually being sold.

**A mechanistic note worth knowing about, even though it isn't cited above.** A companion piece we've been drafting argues that several systems researchers keep finding disrupted in ME/CFS — stress-hormone output, the fight-or-flight response, cell energy production, and inflammation that doesn't shut off — read better as one coordination failure than separate problems, and that this is why a crash arrives late and lasts so long. We didn't cite it in the evidence section above because it's a mechanistic argument, not a pacing-tool validation study, and we haven't sourced the specific framing properly yet — that's the job of the companion piece itself, and we'll bring it to you, citations and all, in a future issue.

**Where this leaves the umbrella term.** Post-exertional malaise sits inside the broader family the National Academies and NIH now call infection-associated chronic illness — the same umbrella covering long COVID and ME/CFS that we've referenced since issue one. (The Choutka et al. citation above uses a related but distinct term, "post-acute infection syndromes" — same family, different coinage.) The wearable question in this issue applies across that whole family, not just to one diagnosis.

## The Promise

Every claim here carries a plain-language epistemic label, checked against the primary source before we publish — never copied forward from an earlier draft, an earlier issue, or our own memory of the finding. We don't force every finding into one of a few stock words: a single unreplicated cohort is called a single unreplicated cohort, an unvalidated heuristic is called an unverified heuristic, and a finding where credible groups' results actually disagree is called contested. What stays fixed is the discipline, not the vocabulary — no claim gets a more confident label than the evidence earns, and we won't let "single cohort" quietly become "proven" by next issue.

That's the whole promise. Thanks for reading issue 002 — we'll see you next time.
